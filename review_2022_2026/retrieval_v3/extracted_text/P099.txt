 An Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) solution is presented for 
Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) that would enhance the 
transparency, trust, and efficiency of cybersecurity operation s. With 
the increasing complexity of infrastructures, explainability in IDS has 
been an urgent need since organizations require justifiable decisions 
made by artificial intelligence . This is particularly important in 
controlled industries like finance, medical, and military, where it is as 
important to know the logic behind the alerts as much as detection 
accuracy. Nonetheless, the traditional deep learning models, despite 
their effectiveness in network threat detection, are “black boxes,” that 
is, they provide minimal insights into their decision-making. This non-
transparency thus leads to  a lack of user confidence and prevents 
adoption in Security Operations Centers (SOCs). Thus, to address this 
challenge, a hybrid deep learning model was created with the help of 
post-hoc explanatory tools like SHAP (Shapley  Additive 
Explanations) and LIME (Local Interpretable Model -Agnostic 
Explanations). In addition, SHAP gives a universal insight into the 
behavior of the model, whereas LIME describes individual 
predictions, which allows analysts to visualize feature contributions 
and explain anomalies. Moreover, the model was tested on three 
benchmark datasets, namely NSL -KDD, UNSW -NB15, and 
CICIDS2017, and each of them reflects various network threats, 
including DoS, brute -force, and infiltration attacks. After removing 
bias through preprocessing, the XAI -IDS reached a recall rate of 
98.4% on the CICIDS2017 dataset, which is better than the results 
from the baseline models.  Besides that, SHAP and LIME 
visualizations also singled out such key features as the Source Bytes, 
the Destination Port, and the Flow Duration as the most influential. As 
a result, more trust and less cognitive load were observed in user -
centered testing, which corresponds to such ethical artificial 
intelligence principles as the EU AI Act (2025) and the NIST AI Risk 
Management Framework (2023).

1. INTRODUCTION 
 
Cybersecurity has become a major concern because attacks are getting advanced, and new technologies like the 
Internet of Things (IoT), cloud computing, and 5G networks are being used more quickly [1], [2]. Intrusion 
detection systems (IDS) are important tools that help detect and reduce threats as they happen, acting as the first 
line of defense in complex networks [3], [4], [5] . In the past, IDS methods were divided into two main types:

signature-based, which find known threats by comparing activity with a list of known threats, and anomaly -
based, which look for unusual behavior that might indicate a problem. Both methods work well for some types 
of attacks, but struggle with more advanced threats, like zero-day attacks and malware that changes its form [6], 
[7], [8], [9]. 
 
However, Artificial Intelligence and deep learning have significantly enhanced IDS by making threat detection 
more accurate and helping systems adapt to new threats. Techniques like deep neural networks, convolutional 
neural networks, and recurrent network s let IDS find complicated patterns in large sets of data [10], [11], [12]. 
However, these models are often "black boxes," meaning their decisions are hard to understand, which makes 
cybersecurity experts doubt their reliability and makes it harder to use them in real work [13], [14], [15]. To fix 
this, explainable AI (XAI) has come into play. XAI methods, such as SHAP, LIME, and attention mechanisms, 
help make AI-driven IDS results easier to understand. This allows analysts to check how decisions are made and 
verify alerts, which builds trust in these AI systems [16]. 
 
Additionally, cybersecurity threats have gotten more complex, diverse, and ubiquitous, causing enterprises to 
rely more heavily on Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) as part of their security strategy. These systems monitor 
network traffic for anomalous or dangerous activities [17]. Traditionally, intrusion detection systems (IDS) were 
classified into two types: signature -based and anomaly -based [18]. Signature-based solutions are effective for 
known threats, but they cannot identify unexpected attacks such as zero -day exploits. Anomaly-based systems, 
on the other hand, are good at discovering new forms of intrusions, but can generate a lot of false a lerts [19]. 
The use of artificial intelligence, particularly machine learning and deep learning, has substantially increased 
IDS's ability to identify complicated attack activities accurately. However, many AI -based IDS are black box 
models, making it difficult for security analysts to comprehend how judgments are made [20]. The lack of 
transparency undermines confidence and the real -world application of these technologies [21]. Explainable 
Artificial Intelligence (XAI) solves this issue by offering simple explanations for how artificial intelligence 
models make judgments. This improves user confidence, accountability, and regulatory compliance [22], [23]. 
This study aims to implement Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) in the IDS to ensure the balance between 
performance and transparency to enhance accountability and trust in security operations among users. Hence, 
the major contributions of this study to the current body of knowledge are as follows: 
- We propose a hybrid CNN -LSTM-based XAI -IDS framework that integrates SHAP and LIME for 
interpretable and accurate intrusion detection. 
- We introduce human-centered evaluation metrics—analyst trust, decision time, and cognitive workload —
to complement traditional performance measures. 
- We validate the model on three benchmark datasets (NSL-KDD, UNSW-NB15, CICIDS2017), confirming 
robustness and high generalization capability. 
- We employ SHAP-based feature importance ranking for explainable and reproducible feature optimization. 
- We align the proposed framework with ethical artificial intelligence standards (EU AI Act, 2025; NIST AI 
RMF, 2023) to promote accountability in cybersecurity artificial intelligence.  
 
This hybrid and interpretable IDS model demonstrates that explainability can coexist with high detection 
performance, advancing responsible, human -centered artificial intelligence applications in cybersecurity 
defense. 
 
2. OVERVIEW OF INTRUSION DETECTION SYSTEMS 
 
Intrusion detection Systems are critical components of cybersecurity installations, designed to monitor network 
or system activity to detect malicious behavior or rule violations [20], [24]. There are three sorts of systems: 
signature-based, anomaly-based, and those that incorporate artificial intelligence [16], [22]. Each variety has its 
own characteristics, benefits, and downsides. 
• Signature-based IDS 
Signature-based intrusion detection systems (IDS) identify intrusions by analyzing predetermined attack patterns 
known as signatures. These signatures function as fingerprints for known harmful activity, helping the system 
to swiftly identify previously en countered threats [20], [24]. Snort and Suricata, which use rule -based ways to 
monitor network traffic in real time, are commonly used examples of this approach [16]. Signature-based IDS is 
very good at detecting known threats and responding rapidly without requiring a lot of computer power [25]. 
But there's a major disadvantage, because these systems rely on existing signatures, they struggle to detect new 
or unknown assaults, such as zero -day exploits or complex malware that changes shape [22], [26]. They are 
unable to detect these types of threats since they have never been encountered before. Furthermore, keeping

signature databases up to date is a continuous process, which might slow things down and make it difficult to 
detect newly emerging threats rapidly [27]. 
 
• Anomaly-based IDS 
Anomaly-based intrusion detection systems function by first determining what typical behavior looks like in a 
system or network. They then check for anything that deviates from this regular pattern, which could indicate a 
security problem, even if it's a novel sort of assault [20], [24]. This technology is essential since it detects zero -
day assaults, which are novel risks that traditional systems may not identify [16], [22]. However, these systems 
can occasionally generate too many false warnings. This occurs when regular behavior is incorrectly recognized 
as suspicious, generating uncertainty for security professionals and slowing down work in security operations 
centers [25], [26]. It can also erode people's trust in the system. Statistical analysis, combining related data, and 
altering detection thresholds are all utilized to aid in this process [20]. However, achieving the correct balance 
between identifying serious threats and minimizing false alarms remains a major difficulty [22], [24]. 
 
2.1 AI-Powered Intrusion Detection Systems 
 
The advent of artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning has altered the way intrusion detection works, 
allowing systems to learn from data and recognize complex assault patterns. AI -powered intrusion detection 
systems are classified into three cate gories: machine learning, deep learning, and hybrid systems that mix the 
two methodologies. 
 
2.1.1 Machine learning (ML): Machine learning -based intrusion detection systems use algorithms such as 
Random Forest, Support Vector Machines, and Decision Trees to decide if network traffic is normal or 
dangerous. These models provide some explainability, allowing analysts to underst and the reasons for a 
detection, which is critical for meeting compliance requirements and conducting investigations. While these 
models perform well on ordered data sets, their usefulness may be reduced in complex, fast -changing contexts 
where new risks emerge regularly, according to Patil et al.'s [1] study. The Machine learning model is presented

2.2 Explainable Artificial Intelligence 
 
Explainable Artificial Intelligence, or XAI, is essential for making AI-powered intrusion detection systems more 
understandable, straightforward, and reliable. XAI aims to show how AI models arrive at their decisions, 
allowing human experts to properly che ck, review, and respond to the results from the system. Fig. 3 presents 
the Explainable XAI framework for an Intrusion Detection System.

• Data Collection:  Raw input data are gathered, such as Network traffic packets, log files, firewall alerts, 
endpoint activity, IoT device telemetry, or sensor data. This stage provides a comprehensive view of system 
activity to detect normal vs. abnormal behavior. 
•  Data Preprocessing:  Prepares raw data for analysis, using techniques such as data cleaning (removing noise, 
duplicates), feature extraction (converting raw traffic into meaningful features like IPs, ports, byte count), and 
normalization/scaling to make values comparable. This ensures the intrusion model receives high -quality, 
structured data. 
•  Intrusion Model: Core AI/ML engine that classifies or detects intrusions using approaches such as    
Machine Learning (Decision Trees, Random Forests, Support Vector Machine) , and Deep Learning (CNNs 
for spatial data, RNNs for sequential traffic, Autoencoders for anomaly detection). This phase predicts either 
normal traffic or a suspicious attack. 
• XAI Layer: Makes black -box AI models interpretable and transparent. techniques deployed are  SHAP 
(values), which show the contribution of each feature to the prediction, LIME, which provides local 
approximations to explain single predictions., Grad-CAM, which visualizes what the DL model focused on, 
and Counterfactuals, which explain what changes would alter the decision. This stage transforms predictions 
into trustworthy, explainable reasoning. 
•  Human-Understandable Explanations: Converts technical model outputs into clear  insights for humans, 
rules (e.g., “More than 50 failed logins in <1 min”), feature importance rankings, visual dashboards, graphs, 
or textual explanations, to assist analysts in quickly understanding why an alert was triggered.  
• Security Analyst:  Final decision -maker using AI insights , verifies alerts, investigates potential attacks, 
triggers mitigation (blocking IP, isolating host, alerting SOC).  XAI ensures analysts trust AI predictions, 
reducing false positives and improving response time.

To detect intrusions, the proposed system combines deep learning and explainable AI techniques. The method 
starts with typical datasets such as NSL -KDD, CICIDS2017, UNSW -NB15, and ToN_IoT/Bot -IoT. These 
datasets contain labeled data for training and testing models. They are often employed in recent research to assess 
the effectiveness of IDS models, making results more comparable and accurate [30]. The data is then cleaned up 
and made easier to deal with. Important characteristics are identified, and the data is streamlined to accommodate 
complex inputs. This phase is significant since it removes extraneous information and improves the model's 
performance in a variety of situations. A recent study used SHAP to select the best features during this stage, 
resulting in better intrusion detection performance on the CICIDS2017 and NSL -KDD datasets  [27]. The 
cleaned data is fed into a deep learning model, which consists of several layers, including an input layer, some 
hidden layers such as CNN, RNN, or a combination of these, and an output layer to aid with categorization. 
Deep learning is adept at reco gnizing both spatial and time -based assault patterns, making it an excellent tool 
for detecting incursion. For example, Chen et al. [3] demonstrated that an IDS using two modules with CNN and 
attention techniques performed better on the NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15 datasets. 
However, deep learning models are frequently referred to as "black boxes" since their decision-making 
process is difficult to comprehend. To address this issue, the framework contains an XAI layer that employs 
techniques such as SHAP, LIME, attention visua lization, Grad-CAM, and counterfactual explanations. These 
strategies demonstrate which attributes or traffic characteristics influenced the model's predictions. A recent 
study on explainable deep learning for IoT networks uses LSTM, SHAP, and feature permutation importance to 
comprehend both individual predictions and overall model behavior [31]. The XAI layer converts its outputs 
into simple explanations, such as highlighting the most critical features, using visual dashboards, or generating 
simple rules. For example, the system may explain a brute -force attack by enforcing a rule that states: " If there 
are more than 100 failed login attempts in 60 seconds, it means someone is attempting to log in illegally." 
According to research, rule-based explanations increase security analysts' faith in the system while also reducing 
false alerts in real-world work contexts. 
Finally, the security analyst evaluates the model's output. Knowing why the model generated its 
predictions allows the analyst to determine whether the warnings are genuine, take actions such as banning 
specific IP addresses, and update the rules employed by the intrusion detection system. According to research, 
understanding how the model works is critical for connecting automatic detection and human judgment in 
cybersecurity [30], [32]. Furthermore, XAI contains strategies for improving the clarity and transparency of AI 
models while maintaining their capacity to make accurate predictions.  
Key concepts in XAI include:

Fidelity: Ensuring that the explanations presented correspond to how the model actually works.  
Interpretability: The model's decisions should be simple to grasp, even for those who lack specialist knowledge. 
Robustness: The model should be dependable even when confronted with difficult or hazardous inputs.  
Usability: The insights supplied should help you make real-world decisions [22], [23]. However, some models, 
such as decision trees and rule-based classifiers, are inherently simple to comprehend. Their architecture enables 
analysts to observe how decisions are made and why certain inputs produce specific results. While these models 
are good for concise explanations, they typically do not perform as well with large, complicated datasets from 
intrusion detection systems as more advanced models, such as deep neural networks [4], [8]. 
 
2.3.1 Post hoc XAI Tools 
 
Post-hoc methods are used to describe how black -box models operate after they have been trained. These tools 
make it easier to understand the model's decisions. Some common methods include:  
 
• Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP):  This method provides both broad and specific insights into how 
different variables influence predictions. It contributes to understanding how each variable affects the model's 
output [33]. 
 
• Local Interpretable Model -agnostic Explanations (LIME):  This technique generates simple, local 
approximations of the model's behavior around specific predictions, making them easier to interpret [34]. 
 
2.3.2 Emerging techniques 
 
Recent studies have addressed the development of improved XAI algorithms that are tailored to deep learning 
models applied in intrusion detection systems. Attention visualization is one of such methods that indicate the 
parts of the input data that the mod els, such as LSTMs or CNNs, are most affected by the decisions they make 
[35]. Moreover, the gradient-based techniques are used to estimate the sensitivity of the model to the changes in 
the input and the effect that these changes have on the output [36]. Moreover, interpretable deep models are 
hybrid interpretable machine learning algorithms that are implemented on top of deep learning architectures to 
ensure high performance and to guarantee model transparency [37], [38]. 
 
2.4 Taxonomy of the XAI Approaches in IDS   
 
Explainable intrusion detection systems may be categorized on the basis of their transparency. To begin with, 
Model-specific explainability is concerned with the use of intrinsically explainable models, including decision 
trees or logistic regression, which give direct information on the way predictions are made. Conversely, model -
agnostic post-hoc explainability is a post -hoc method that uses SHAP, LIME, anchors, and counterfactuals to 
interpret trained black-box models, and thus can help gain a better und erstanding without modifying the model 
[20], [29] . Additionally, hybrid explainability integrates interpretable machine learning elements with deep 
learning models to attain excellent detection and comprehensible and interpretable human -readable reasoning 
[4]. Moreover, domain-specific explainability can be used to apply XAI to specific scenarios like the Internet of 
Things [39], SCADA systems [40], and vehicular ad hoc networks (VANETs) to make sure that explanations 
are domain -specific and meaningful in each area of application [41]. This rating demonstrates the balance 
between how simple the system is to grasp, how well it scales, and how accurate it is at detecting dangers, 
allowing professionals to select the optimum solution for each case. 
 
2.5 Benchmark datasets in the IDS research 
 
Datasets are used extensively in the development and testing of intrusion detection systems, particularly those 
based on artificial intelligence. These datasets include labeled network traffic that is used to train models, test 
their performance, and establish benchmarks for comparison. 
 
2.5.1 Legacy datasets 
 
NSL-KDD: An improved version of KDD'99 with reduced redundancy that is still widely used, but it is 
becoming less suitable for assessing today's security concerns [42].  
KDD'99: One of the earliest datasets used to evaluate intrusion detection systems, but its issues with repetition 
and bias render it unreliable for contemporary research.

2.5.2 Modern datasets 
 
CICIDS2017: Contains real-world network traffic with various forms of attacks such as DDoS, botnet, brute 
force, and penetration, providing a diverse set of testing scenarios [43].  
UNSW-NB15: Offers a variety of attack types and traffic behaviors that are representative of today's network 
environments [44]. 
CIC-DDoS2019 and TON_IoT: Emphasis on IoT networks and DDoS assaults as emerging dangers to current

2.5.3 Dataset Limitations 
 
Most datasets used today are either made up or gathered in controlled settings, even though there have been 
some improvements. Because of this, they might not fully show the real complexity, changes, and random issues 
found in actual production networks. This creates a difference between what is found in research and what works 
in real situations. This shows how important it is to use real -world traffic data along with standard datasets to 
make intrusion detection systems more reliable and effective [19], [45].

2.8 Human Factors and Usability of the Security Operations Center 
 
Explainability is more than just a technological aspect; it also plays an important role in generating trust, making 
choices, and increasing efficiency in security operations centers. 
 
2.8.1 Trust calibration 
Analysts frequently disregard black-box IDS signals, resulting in the underuse of AI results. Kalakoti et al. [48] 
demonstrated that SHAP explanations assisted analysts in calibrating trust, reducing both over - and under -
reliance. Ables et al. [25] validated this in controlled usability research, in which explanations raised acceptance 
rates by 30%.  
2.8.2 Cognitive workload reduction 
Ebrahimi et al. [26] analyzed workload using NASA -TLX measures and discovered that XAI dashboards 
decreased mental demand scores by up to 25%. Jiang et al. [51] improved on this by providing interactive 
dashboards that allowed analysts to dynamically query IDS alarms, significantly lowering workload.  
 
2.8.3 Decision Speed and Accuracy 
The incorporation of SHAP and LIME into dashboards allowed analysts to make faster and more accurate 
judgments [58]. For example, detecting an "unusual HTTP request frequency" that triggered an SQL injection 
alert reduced response time by 18%. 
2.8.4 Risks of Over-transparency 
While explanations increase confidence, excessive elaboration risks information overload or even adversarial 
exploitation [53]. This emphasizes the importance of balance: explanations should be useful without leaking 
sensitive model flaws. 
2.9 Regulatory and ethical perspectives 
The use of XAI-IDS raises major regulatory and ethical concerns. 
2.9.1 Regulatory Frameworks 
 
• EU AI Act: IDS is classified as a high-risk AI system that requires transparency, accountability, and human 
monitoring (European Commission, 2021). XAI helps IDS meet these requirements by making judgments 
that are traceable and auditable [23]. 
•  NIST AI Risk Management Framework (2023):  In AI, trustworthiness, justice, and explainability are 
emphasized. To meet US regulatory standards, IDS deployed in critical infrastructure must have explainable 
decision-making processes. 
 
2.9.2 Ethical Considerations 
• Accountability: Who is to blame when an XAI-IDS generates a false warning or misses an attack? [21]. 
• Fairness: IDS must avoid prejudice against specific traffic categories or user groups [51]. 
• Privacy: XAI must be utilized to explain encrypted traffic [50] while avoiding disclosing sensitive data. 
• Adversarial Risks: Explanations may highlight system weaknesses that attackers could exploit [53]. 
 
2.9.3 Balancing transparency and security 
 
Explanations must be context -sensitive: provide enough detail for analysts while remaining abstract enough to 
prevent misuse [16]. Future IDS should use multi -layered explanation tactics and tailor information to diverse 
stakeholders (analysts, executives, and regulators). 
 
2.10 Gaps in the Literature 
 
• Dataset realism: There have been few studies that validate live enterprise traffic. 
• Scalability: SHAP and LIME require computational resources. 
• Human evaluation: Most studies lack significant user studies. 
• Adversarial Robustness: Limited research on assaults on XAI mechanisms

The research employed an experimental quantitative design to develop, train as well and test an Explainable 
Artificial Intelligence (XAI) -enhanced Intrusion Detection System (IDS). The purpose of the research was to 
test both the detection and interpretabi lity using the benchmark cybersecurity data through the aid of empirical 
testing. The deep learning models were integrated with the post -hoc explainability mechanisms, which were 
implemented to be user-centric with respect to transparency, trust, and usability [24], [25]. 
It was designed with the combination of deep learning architectures, namely Convolutional Neural Networks 
(CNN) and Long Short -Term Memory (LSTM) networks, and post -hoc explainability methods to balance 
predictive performance and explainability [22], [59].  
The IDS was empirically tested on benchmark data and metrics of performance, including accuracy, 
recall, and F1 -score, which is common practice in AI -based intrusion detection [20]. Also, human -friendly 
measures of interpretability were used to assess the trust of the analysts and the usability, which is up-to-date in 
explainable cybersecurity systems [26], [48]. 
 
3.1 Datasets 
To achieve generalizability and comparative validity, three well -known datasets of IDS NSL -KDD, UNSW-
NB15, and CICIDS2017 were utilized. The NSL -KDD dataset is a conventional reference point when it comes 
to the detection of legacy threats in the network [20]. UNSW-NB15 was the contemporary enterprise traffic 
where the types of attacks were Fuzzers, Exploits, Reconnaissance, and Backdoors [24]. CICIDS2017 provided

real-time, time-based traffic with DDoS, Brute Force, Web, and Infiltration attacks [48]. The pre -treatment of 
the datasets, as well as the conversion of the data to similar feature vectors, was performed. The data was divided 
into 70 percent training, 15 percent validation, and 15 percent testing to ensure consistency in model evaluation, 
as some of the earlier methods of research in IDS [22], [26]. 
 
3.2 Data Preprocessing 
Preprocessing was performed by removing noise, normalizing, and one -hot encoding of categorical attributes 
[20]. The SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique) was used to deal with the issue of class 
imbalance [24]. SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) was used to rank features in order of importance to 
improve both the model performance and explainability [59]. Attributes that were redundant or showed low 
variance were discarded, and this enhanced the convergence and interpretability of the model [22], [25]. 
 
3.3 Model Architecture 
The system was based on a hybrid CNN -LSTM deep learning model, which was aimed to learn spatial and 
temporal network features:  
• Layers of Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) elicited spatial correlations of feature patterns.  
• The Long Short -Term Memory (LSTM) layers were used to model time -series network flows sequential 
dependencies. 
• Dense layers learnt representations combined and generated multi -class classifications through a Softmax 
output layer. Table 3 illustrates model configuration parameters.

3.4 Explainable AI Integration 
Post hoc XAI techniques were incorporated into the model to ensure its transparency and auditability:  
• SHAP offered the importance of features globally by calculating the effect of each input feature on the 
output of the models.  
• LIME (Local Interpretable Model -Agnostic Explanations) provided local explanations, which explained 
each prediction of a particular traffic session.  
• The deep feature activations in the CNN layers were identified through Grad -CAM visualizations and 
showed what behaviors of the network caused alerts.

The addition of SHAP and LIME ensured that the detection was greater than 97% and it also gave the explainable 
decision paths. The small computational cost (10 percent) was compensated by the high level of transparency, 
which was previously found by Mohale and Obagbuwa [24].

5.1 Analyst Trust and Usability 
Clear attributions of features led to better confidence of the analysts and better response accuracy. The main 
result of human trust calibration was similar to the results of Ables et al. [25] and Kalakoti et al. [16], which 
indicates the importance of interpretability when adopting AI in SOC settings.  
 
5.2 Operational Efficiency 
The introduction of the Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) to Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) has had 
a profound impact on the efficiency of operation in the Security Operations Centers (SOCs). The visual results 
that could be explained by SHAP an d LIME allowed the analysts to trace the alerts to particular features of the 
network, which facilitated the prioritization of responses and minimized redundant checks [25], [26] . These 
interpretable reasons assisted analysts in seeing the logic behind the alerts, which allowed the analyst to make 
decisions more quickly and reduce security incidents more efficiently [48]. The results are related to the previous 
studies that explainability fills the gap between automated AI reasoning and human decision -making in the 
context of cybersecurity [22], [24] . Moreover, the XAI -IDS framework helped to comply with and be 
accountable according to the international standards, including the EU AI Act (2025) and the NIST AI Risk 
Management Framework (2023), which both require transparency and explainability as the preconditions of 
ethical AI implementation [20]. The system increased regulatory alignment by making it easier to interpret and 
to have human oversight, and it increased the productivity of analysts and operational trust.  
 
5.3 Limitations 
Although the model has been performing well in terms of quantitative performance, the model has limitations,

• Dataset realism: Controlled datasets can be suboptimal models of live traffic patterns.  
  
• Moderate latency was added by the computational cost of SHAP explanations.  
 
• Divergence Explanation: discrepancy between SHAP and LIME weightings.  
6. FUTURE RESEARCH DIRECTIONS 
This research has shown that the application of Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) to Intrusion Detection 
Systems (IDS) based on deep learning will enhance both the level of transparency and the performance. There 
are still several aspects that can be improved in the future through research and development. It is suggested that 
the following directions should be considered to develop the field: 
 
6.1 Real-Time Adaptive XAI for IDS 
Adaptive explainable models that can provide real-time feedback in dynamic cybersecurity locations should also 
be considered in future research. However, the existing post hoc algorithms, such as SHAP and LIME, while 
informative, are computationally heavy and therefore cannot be applied to monitor live traffic. Consequently, 
there is a growing need to develop Light -based explainability models that are stream -based models that can 
enhance the latency and responsiveness of high-speed networks [16], [26], [53].  
 
6.2 Distributed Explainability and Federated Learning 
 The federated and distributed learning paradigms can be used to apply XAI to improve privacy and scalability 
of data in a decentralized network. In this context, Federated explainability models can become the focus of the 
next generation of research, allowing for the determination of the local model explainability even when sensitive 
enterprise or IoT data is not accessible [32], [52]. 
 
6.3 XAI Adversarial Robustness and Security 
Explainability models can be adversarially manipulated because explanations can expose weaknesses in models. 
More research is required to come up with resilient XAI systems that are resistant to adversarial perturbation but 
maintain transparency [53]. This direction of research will make sure that explainability does not unintentionally 
provide additional attack vectors. 
 
 
6.4 XAI-IDS Cross-domain Implementations 
The explainable intrusion detection models have to be applied in other security -sensitive systems such as 
industrial control systems, health, and vehicle networks. All these areas require investigation concerning specific 
problems of heterogeneity, privacy , and real -time responsiveness of information in future research [39], [41], 
[50]

6.5 Large Language Model Interactions (LLMs) 
New developments in large language models offer avenues for applying natural language reasoning and report 
generation to intrusion detection systems. Future research can explore the idea of LLM-enhanced explainability, 
in which models are capable of conver ting the complex alerts into readable, context -sensitive decision -
supporting narratives that aid with situational awareness of analysts. 
 
7. CONCLUSION 
The study has formulated and tested an Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) based Intrusion Detection 
System incorporating deep learning and interpretability systems to enhance transparency and trust. The hybrid 
CNN-LSTM model was highly detected (up to 98.4%), and human comprehension of alert reasoning was greatly 
developed by SHAP and LIME explanations. Empirical findings proved that explainability enhances analyst 
confidence, decreases the time required to make decisions, and enhances human AI cooperation in cybersecurity 
operations. The study adds to both the academic theory and the practical defense systems by showing that 
accuracy and efficiency can be achieved with interpretability.  
This study concludes that explainable models of IDS are the key to having understandable, responsible, and 
credible cybersecurity systems in the era of AI-driven cybersecurity.
