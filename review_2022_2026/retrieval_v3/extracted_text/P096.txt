Abstract 
This study addresses the persistent challenge of protecting MIS-driven enterprise networks against increasingly 
complex, previously unseen, and adaptive cyber threats when conventional signature -based controls, 
fragmented security data, and static monitoring practices provide limited visibility and response capability. The 
purpose was to examine how Machine Learning -Based Intrusion Detection Capability (MLIDC), Intelligent 
Anomaly Classification Capability (IACC), MIS -Security Data Integration (MSDI), and Adaptive Threat 
Monitoring and Response (ATMR) influence Enterprise Netw ork Security Performance (ENSP) across 
enterprise environments containing interconnected digital, network, and cloud -enabled resources. A 
quantitative, cross-sectional, explanatory, case-based survey design was adopted using purposive sampling of 
cybersecurity analysts, network administrators, IT/MIS’S managers, security engineers, system 
administrators, and IT risk or compliance professionals. Of 320 questionnaires distributed, 298 were returned 
and 286 valid responses were retained, yielding an 89.4% vali d response rate. Data were analyzed using 
descriptive statistics, Cronbach’s alpha, KMO and Bartlett tests, Pearson correlation, multiple regression, 
ANOVA, multicollinearity diagnostics, and residual testing. ENSP recorded the highest mean (M = 4.21, SD 
= 0.51), followed by IACC (M = 4.18, SD = 0.54), ATMR (M = 4.16, SD = 0.56), MLIDC (M = 4.13, SD = 
0.58), and MSDI (M = 4.09, SD = 0.60). All predictors were positively associated with ENSP: IACC (r = .78), 
ATMR (r = .75), MSDI (r = .71), and MLIDC (r = .69), all p < .001. The regression model explained 71.4% of 
ENSP variance (R² = .714, adjusted R² = .710, F (4,281) = 175.40, p < .001), with IACC the strongest predictor 
(β = .34), followed by ATMR (β = .28), MSDI (β = .23), and MLIDC (β = .20). The findings  imply that 
enterprise cybersecurity improves when intelligent detection is integrated with accurate anomaly classification, 
coordinated security information, and adaptive threat response, supporting security operations, MIS visibility, 
faster incident handling, and resilient enterprise network protection.

INTRODUCTION 
Cybersecurity can be defined as the coordinated protection of information systems, communication 
networks, digital assets, and network -enabled organizational processes against unauthorized access, 
disruption, manipulation, disclosure, and other forms of malicious activity. Within this broader 
domain, an intrusion detection system (IDS) refers to a security mech anism that observes host or 
network activities and distinguishes legitimate behavior from activities associated with attacks or 
policy violations. Machine learning refers to computational techniques through which models learn 
patterns and decision boundaries from data rather than depending exclusively on manually encoded 
signatures or static rules. Anomaly detection is more specifically concerned with identifying 
observations or behavioral patterns that deviate meaningfully from a representation of normal activity, 
while anomaly classification extends this function by assigning suspicious observations to identifiable 
attack or behavioral categories. In An overview of anomaly detection techniques (pp. 3448 -3470), 
anomaly detection is presented as a response t o the limitations of purely signature -oriented security 
mechanisms when previously unseen behavioral deviations must be recognized (Patcha & Park, 2007). 
The related article Anomaly -based network intrusion detection: Techniques, systems and challenges 
(pp. 18-28) describes anomaly -oriented intrusion detection as a mechanis m for modeling legitimate 
behavior and detecting significant departures from that behavioral profile (García-Teodoro et al., 2009). 
This distinction is central to enterprise security because an unusual connection, traffic burst, 
authentication sequence, service request, or data-transfer pattern has limited security value unless the 
organization can determine whether it represents benign variation, misuse, denial of service, 
reconnaissance, privilege abuse, malware activity, or another attack class. Anomaly detection research 
has consequently examined statistical, classification -based, clustering -based, nearest -neighbor, 
information-theoretic, and machine -learning approaches for recognizing deviations in 
multidimensional data (Chandola et al., 2009 ). Network intrusion detection further combines traffic 
observation, feature extraction, b ehavioral analysis, and security decision rules to protect 
confidentiality, integrity, and availability across interconnected systems (Liao et al., 2013 ). From an 
international perspective, modern information and communication infrastructures connect enterprises, 
institutions, users, applications, cloud resources, and distributed services across geographical 
boundaries, making malicious network activity a security concern that is not limited to one 
organization or national environment. Network anomaly detection has accordingly been treated as an 
important component of information and communication technology security because anomalous 
traffic may represent a range of coordinated and technically heterogeneous attacks (Ahmed et al., 2016). 
In the present study, MIS -driven enterprise network security refers to an organizational security 
environment in which network-security information is captured, processed, classified, communicated, 
and used within management information structures to support security monitoring and operational 
decision processes. IDS effectiveness in such environments involves not simply recognizing malicious 
packets but organizing security information into actionable knowledge about network conditions and 
attack behavior (Khraisat et al., 2019). 
Machine learning -enhanced intrusion detection represents a movement from fixed matching 
procedures toward data -driven discrimination between le gitimate and malicious network behavior. 
Traditional signature-based IDSs are effective when recognizable attack patterns already exist in their 
rule bases, whereas anomaly -based systems attempt to model normality and identify deviations that 
may not correspond directly to predefined signatures. In Intrusion detection by machine learning: A 
review (pp. 11994-12000), machine-learning IDS approaches are organized around individual, hybrid, 
and ensemble learning strategies that classify network activity by ext racting discriminative patterns 
from security data (Tsai et al., 2009). Random forests provided an important example of this data-driven 
orientation by using ensembles of decision trees to construct intrusion patterns automatically rather 
than requiring security administrators to encode every de tection rule manually (Zhang et al., 2008 ). 
Hybrid models subsequently combined supervised misuse detection with unsupervised or clustering-
oriented anomaly analysis so that known attack classes and unfamiliar traffic behaviors could be 
handled within a coordinated detection architecture (Elbasiony et al., 2013 ). Yet machine learning in 
operational intrusion detection is not equivalent to achieving high classification scores in a controlled 
experiment. In Outside the Closed World: On Using Machine Learning for Network Intrusion Detection

(pp. 305-316), the transfer of machine-learning techniques into real network environments is examined 
in relation to variability between training assumptions and operational traffic conditions, highlighting 
the difficulty of treating network security as an ordinary closed-world classification problem (Sommer 
& Paxson, 2010). The cyber-analytics literature has similarly shown that decision trees, support vector 
machines, Bayesian approaches, artificial neural networks, clustering methods, association rules, 
evolutionary computation, and ensemble techniques differ substantially in their data requirements, 
computational characteristics, and suitability for particular intrusion -detection problems (Buczak & 
Guven, 2016). Sequence-sensitive models have expanded this analytical capability because malicious 
activity frequently develops through related events r ather than isolated network observations. 
Recurrent neural networks, for example, can model dependencies within traffic records and have been 
evaluated for multiclass intrusion recognition, providing a mechanism for distinguishing different 
attack categories rather than producing only a binary normal -versus-malicious judgment (Yin et al., 
2017). Within the present research, Machine Learning -Based Intrusion Detection Capability (MLIDC) 
consequently represents the enterprise's perceived capacity to employ learned traffic patterns for 
accurate, timely, and adaptable recognition of malicious netw ork behavior, including behavior that 
cannot be handled adequately through static signatures alone. 
Intrusion detection becomes more analytically valuable when suspicious behavior can be classified, 
prioritized, and associated with meaningful security states. Intelligent anomaly classification concerns 
the ability of an analytical security system to differentiate abnormal observations according to 
characteristics such as attack form, behavioral pattern, severity, source characteristics, or operational 
risk. In A Deep Learning Approach to Network Intrusion Detection (pp. 41-50), representation learning 
is used to reduce dependence on extensive manual feature engineering and to provide a learned 
representation for distinguishing normal and intrusive network tr affic (Shone et al., 2018 ). Deep -
learning architectures have also been examined in distributed security environments in which large 
volumes of device and communication data create demands for scalable attack identification (Diro & 
Chilamkurti, 2018). A broader examination of machine lea rning and deep learning in cybersecurity 
identifies neural networks, support vector machines, decision trees, clustering, Bayesian techniques, 
and deep architectures as important analytical families used in intrusion and malicious -behavior 
recognition (Xin et al., 2018 ). The classification problem is particularly important because enterprise 
security personnel must distinguish among heterogeneous forms of malicious activity rather than treat 
all anomalous observations as equivalent events. L arge-scale experimental comparisons of deep 
models across intrusion datasets have shown that network -level and host-level security classification 
involves differences in attack distribution, feature structure, model architecture, and dataset 
composition, all of which affect the resulting detection behavior (Vinayakumar et al., 2019 ). Feature 
quality is also inseparable from classifier effectiveness. Deep neural intrusion detection combined with 
filter-based feature engineering has been evaluated as a means of removing less informative attributes 
while preserving characteristics useful for attack discrimination (Kasongo & Sun, 2019 ). Research 
specifically examining deep-learning anomaly-based IDSs has further differentiated detection systems 
by input data, learning strategy, deployment structure, and evaluation method, showing that anomaly-
based intrusion classification cannot be reduced to a single algorithmic procedure (Aldweesh et al., 
2020). These findings provide the technical basis for separating Intelligent Anomaly Classification 
Capability (IACC) from basic intrusion recognition in this study. A system may identify an event as 
suspicious while still performing poorly in distinguishing attack classes, assigning security priority, 
minimizing misclassification, or translating anomalous patterns into operational categories. The 
research additionally treats Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response (ATMR) as a distinct enterprise 
capability covering continuous observation, dynamic threat evaluation, rapid alert generation, 
prioritization, and security response associated with changing traffic behavior and detected anomalies. 
The effectiveness of machine -learning intrusion detection also depends on the information 
environment in which analytical outputs are generated and consumed. Enterprise networks produce 
security-relevant information through packet flows, authentication servi ces, endpoints, servers, 
applications, network devices, security appliances, access -control systems, and event logs. A 
management information system provides an organizational mechanism for transforming such 
distributed data into structured information used for monitoring, coordination, reporting, and decision

generated and accessed. DeepIDS, for example, evaluated deep neural and recurrent architectures for 
flow-based intrusion detection within software-defined networking while also examining throughput, 
latency, and resource utilization rather than classification accuracy alone (Tang et al., 2020). Reviews of 
machine-learning intrusion detection have continued to distinguish signature -based and anomaly -
based detection while emphasizing the central role of classification techniques and benchmark datasets 
in determining how legitimat e and malicious behavior is separated (Dina & Manivannan, 2021 ). 
Systematic examination of ML-based and deep-learning-based network IDS research likewise identifies 
detection accuracy, false alarms, novel attack recognition, dataset selection, and evaluation criteria as 
interconnected dimensions of NIDS performance (Ahmad et al., 2021). The dependent construct in this 
research, Enterprise Network Security Performance (ENSP), therefore encompasses perceived 
effectiveness in threat detection, security visibility, response efficiency, resili ence, reduction of 
successful intrusions, continuity of network services, and quality of cybersecurity -related operational 
decisions. 
A central research problem remains in connecting the technical capabilities of machine -learning 
intrusion detection with t he broader organizational security performance of MIS -driven enterprise 
environments. A substantial portion of intrusion -detection research evaluates algorithms through 
accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, detection rate, false-alarm rate, and related experimental metrics, 
while enterprise security depends simultaneously on whether detected information is correctly 
classified, integrated with organizational information systems, monitored continuously, and translated 
into coordinated responses. Earlier flo w-based research in software -defined environments 
demonstrated that deep models could operate on selected network features for anomaly detection, 
reinforcing the importance of relating learned traffic representations to network -level security 
monitoring rather than examining algorithms in abstraction (Tang et al., 2016). More recent surveys of 
artificial-intelligence-based NIDS research identify false alarms, zero -day recognition, dataset age, 
attack diversity, and methodological differences among AI -based detection architectures as recurring 
factors affecting the assessment of intelligent IDSs (Habeeb & Babu, 2022). A systematic review of 119 
highly cited anomaly-based network intrusion detection studies likewise demonstrates that application 
domain, preprocessing, attack -detection method, evaluation metrics, and dataset characteristics 
constitute interconnected dimensions of anomaly -oriented security research (Yang et al., 2022 ). 
Research on machine learning for intrusion detection in industrial control environments further 
differentiates supervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised, and reinforcement-learning approaches and 
shows that intrusion identification can draw simultaneously on network -level observations and 
behavioral or process -level anomaly information (Umer et al., 2022 ). These streams of literature 
establish a specific empirical space for examining enterprise cybersecurity as a coordinated set of 
capabilities rather than as the output of one classifier. The present study accordingly conc entrates on 
four analytically distinct predictors, Machine Learning-Based Intrusion Detection Capability (MLIDC), 
Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability (IACC), MIS -Security Data Integration (MSDI), and

Enterprise networks have become central infrastructures through which organizations coordinate 
information, applications, communication, financial transactions, operational processes, customer 
services, and managerial decision -making. As organizational activities increasingly depend on 
interconnected digital systems, maintaining the confidentiality, integrity, availability, and reliability of 
network resources has become a fundamental requirement o f enterprise information management. 
Traditional network-security mechanisms have primarily relied on predefined signatures, static access 
controls, manually constructed rules, and threshold-based monitoring procedures to identify malicious 
activity. Such mechanisms remain useful for detecting recognizable attacks, but their effectiveness is 
constrained when network behavior becomes highly dynamic, attack patterns change rapidly, or 
malicious activities resemble legitimate traffic. Enterprise networks conti nuously generate large 
volumes of security -related information through user authentication, application access, endpoints, 
servers, databases, routers, firewalls, cloud environments, and other interconnected resources. 
Processing such information manually or through fixed detection rules can make timely identification 
of sophisticated threats difficult. Machine learning has therefore become increasingly relevant to 
intrusion detection because it enables security systems to learn patterns from historical and operational 
data, distinguish normal from abnormal activities, and classify potentially malicious behavior 
according to identifiable characteristics. Machine learning -based intrusion detection can support the 
recognition of complex traffic patterns, while  intelligent anomaly classification can differentiate 
suspicious activities according to attack type, severity, behavioral characteristics, or potential risk. 
However, enterprise cybersecurity performance also depends on how effectively security information 
is integrated with broader management information systems. Security alerts that remain fragmented 
across independent technical platforms may provide limited organizational visibility unless they are 
consolidated, interpreted, and made accessible for secu rity monitoring and decision -making. MIS -
security data integration therefore provides an organizational information -processing mechanism 
through which intrusion alerts, anomaly classifications, threat information, and operational security 
data can be trans formed into coordinated security intelligence. Adaptive threat monitoring and 
response further extends this capability by enabling continuous observation, dynamic prioritization, 
rapid alerting, and responsive action when abnormal network conditions are id entified. Against this

Problem Statement 
Enterprise organizations face a persistent security problem arising from the increasing complexity of 
network environments and the limitations of convention al intrusion -detection mechanisms in 
identifying diverse, previously unseen, and behaviorally complex attacks. Traditional signature-based 
systems depend heavily on previously recognized attack patterns and predefined security rules, which 
can reduce their  effectiveness when malicious activities do not match stored signatures or when 
attackers deliberately modify observable characteristics to avoid detection. At the same time, anomaly-
oriented systems may generate large numbers of alerts without providing s ufficiently accurate 
classification, prioritization, or contextual interpretation. This creates an operational difficulty in which 
security teams are required to distinguish meaningful threats from normal network variations while 
managing false positives, false negatives, alert overload, and time-sensitive security incidents. Another 
problem exists at the organizational information level. Intrusion alerts, network logs, threat 
intelligence, system events, and anomaly classifications may be generated by diff erent security tools 
but remain fragmented across technical platforms. When these data are not effectively integrated into 
management information processes, organizations may experience reduced security visibility and 
slower coordination between technical detection and managerial response. The growing use of machine 
learning provides opportunities for improving intrusion detection and anomaly classification, yet 
many existing studies concentrate primarily on algorithmic accuracy, benchmark datasets, or 
comparisons among classifiers. Such evaluations do not fully explain how machine learning -based 
detection capabilities operate within MIS -driven enterprise environments or how security -data 
integration and adaptive monitoring contribute to broader network -security performance. There is 
therefore a need to examine enterprise cybersecurity as a combination of technical detection capability, 
intelligent anomaly classification, integrated security information, and adaptive response capacity. This 
study addresses th at problem by statistically examining how Machine Learning -Based Intrusion 
Detection Capability, Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability, MIS-Security Data Integration, and 
Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response are associated with Enterprise Networ k Security 
Performance within a quantitative, cross-sectional, case-study-based research setting. 
Purpose  
The primary purpose of this study is to empirically examine how machine learning-enhanced intrusion 
detection and anomaly classification contribute t o the security performance of MIS -driven enterprise 
networks when they operate together with integrated security information and adaptive threat -
management capabilities. The study is designed around four independent constructs, namely Machine 
Learning-Based Intrusion Detection Capability, Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability, MIS -
Security Data Integration, and Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response, with Enterprise Network 
Security Performance serving as the dependent construct. The first objectiv e is to determine whether 
Machine Learning-Based Intrusion Detection Capability significantly influences Enterprise Network 
Security Performance by examining the extent to which learned traffic patterns, automated recognition, 
detection accuracy, and identification of suspicious network behavior contribute to enterprise security 
outcomes. The second objective is to evaluate the influence of Intelligent Anomaly Classification 
Capability on Enterprise Network Security Performance, with particular attention to  the ability to 
differentiate anomalous events, categorize attack behavior, reduce misclassification, and prioritize 
identified threats. The third objective is to assess whether MIS -Security Data Integration significantly 
affects Enterprise Network Securit y Performance by examining the organizational integration of 
security alerts, network information, threat data, and decision-support processes. The fourth objective 
is to investigate the influence of Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response on Enterprise Ne twork 
Security Performance through continuous monitoring, dynamic threat evaluation, rapid alert 
generation, prioritization, and timely security action. The fifth objective is to determine the collective 
explanatory and predictive contribution of the four independent variables to Enterprise Network 
Security Performance through multiple regression modeling. These objectives have been examined

Research Hypotheses 
The hypotheses of this study have been developed to statistically test the proposed relationships 
between the four enterprise cybersecurity capabilities and Enterprise Network Security Performance. 
The first hypothesis, H1, states that Machine Learning -Based Intrusion Detection Capability has a 
significant positive effect on Enterprise Network Security Performance. This hypothesis reflects the 
expectation that stronger capability to identify malicious network activity through learned behavioral 
and traffic patterns will correspond with stronger security outcomes. The second hypothesis, H2, states 
that Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability has a significant positive effect on Enterprise 
Network Sec urity Performance. This proposition focuses on the ability of an enterprise security 
environment to distinguish anomalous activities accurately, differentiate attack categories, recognize 
varying levels of threat severity, and reduce security-event misclassification. The third hypothesis, H3, 
states that MIS -Security Data Integration has a significant positive effect on Enterprise Network 
Security Performance. This hypothesis addresses whether the effective integration of intrusion alerts, 
network events, t hreat information, and other cybersecurity data into organizational information 
systems strengthens security visibility and coordinated security management. The fourth hypothesis, 
H4, states that Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response has a significant positive effect on Enterprise 
Network Security Performance. This relationship examines whether continuous network observation, 
dynamic security assessment, rapid threat prioritization, and responsive security actions are associated 
with improved enterprise network protection. The fifth hypothesis, H5, states that Machine Learning-
Based Intrusion Detection Capability, Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability, MIS-Security Data 
Integration, and Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response jointly have a signifi cant effect on 
Enterprise Network Security Performance. H5 therefore evaluates the overall explanatory power of the 
proposed research model by determining whether the four capabilities collectively account for 
statistically significant variation in enterprise network-security performance.

LITERATURE REVIEW 
The literature surrounding machine learning -enhanced intrusion detection has developed across 
several closely related areas, including network anomaly detection, intelligent attack classification, 
cybersecurity analytics, enterprise information integration, a daptive monitoring, and organizational 
security performance. Intrusion detection research has traditionally distinguished between signature-
based systems, which identify previously recognized attack patterns, and anomaly -based systems, 
which detect deviations from established representations of normal network behavior. The increasing 
volume and complexity of enterprise network traffic have expanded the use of machine -learning 
techniques because such approaches can identify multidimensional relationships in security data that 
may not be efficiently represented through static detection rules. Supervised learning techniques have 
been widely examined for classifying previously labeled attack behaviors, while unsupervised and 
semi-supervised methods have been investigated for detecting unusual patterns when complete attack 
labels are unavailable. Deep learning has further expanded this literature by enabling automated 
representation learning from complex network data. However, effective enterprise network security  
requires more than the initial identification of suspicious behavior. Security systems must also classify 
anomalies meaningfully, reduce false alerts, distinguish among attack categories, prioritize threats, and 
provide sufficiently interpretable informat ion for operational response. This requirement creates a 
direct connection between intelligent anomaly classification and broader enterprise security 
management. At the organizational level, network alerts and cybersecurity events must be integrated 
with information systems that support visibility, coordination, reporting, and decision -making. MIS-
Security Data Integration therefore represents an important bridge between technical security analytics 
and organizational information processing. Adaptive Threat  Monitoring and Response adds another 
dimension by emphasizing continuous observation, dynamic assessment, and timely reaction to 
changing network conditions. The literature review consequently examines Machine Learning -Based 
Intrusion Detection Capability , Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability, MIS -Security Data 
Integration, Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response, and Enterprise Network Security Performance 
as conceptually related components of the proposed research model. It also establishes Organizational

Machine Learning-Based Intrusion Detection in Enterprise Networks 
Machine learning -based intrusion detection has become an important analytical component of 
enterprise network security because conventional security mechanisms often depend on predefined 
attack signatures, manually specified rules, or fixed thresholds that can be insufficient when malicious 
activities exhibit changing behavioral characteristics. In enterprise networks, intrusion detection 
requires continuous examination of traffic attributes, communication patterns, access behavior, 
protocol characteristics, and system events to determine whether observed activities correspond to 
legitimate operations or possible security violations. Machine learning contributes to this process by 
constructing classification or detection models from historical observations and subsequently applying 
learned relationships to incoming network data. Such capability is particularly relevant to Management 
Information System environments where network activities originate from interconnected databases, 
business applications, servers, workstations, communication platforms, cloud services, and user-access 
mechanisms. In Feature deduction and ensemble design of intrusion detection systems (pp. 295 -307), 
feature selection was examined through Bayesian networks and Classification and Regression Trees, 
demonstrating that eliminating redundant or minimally informative attributes can improve 
computational efficiency while retaining information required for effective intrusion discrimination 
(Ashik & Shabbir, 2022; Chebrolu et al., 2005). This finding is important because enterprise intrusion-
detection systems frequently process high -dimensional security data in which not every recorded 
attribute contributes equally to identifying attacks. A machine -learning-based IDS therefore involves 
more than sel ecting a classifier; it requires appropriate data representation, relevant feature 
identification, preprocessing, and reliable discrimination between normal and intrusive behavior. In 
Modeling intrusion detection system using hybrid intelligent systems (pp. 114-132), decision trees and 
support vector machines were combined through hierarchical and ensemble configurations to improve 
intrusion-detection accuracy while controlling computational complexity (Hasan & Adnan, 2022 ; 
Peddabachigari et al., 2007). Such hybridization reflects the heterogeneous nature of enterprise network 
threats because individual learning algorithms may perform differently across attack categories, traffic 
distributions, and feature structures. Machine Learning -Based Intrusion Detection Capability in the 
present research therefore represents an organizational capability to use learned network patterns, 
appropriate features, intelligent classification mechanisms, and  computationally efficient analytical 
procedures for identifying malicious activity across enterprise information infrastructures. 
The reliability of machine learning -based intrusion detection is also strongly influenced by the data 
used to develop and evaluate detection models. A classifier trained on outdated, repetitive, or poorly 
representative network observations may report strong experimental accuracy while performing less 
effectively when exposed to complex enterprise traffic. Modern enterprise netw orks contain diverse 
application protocols, user activities, encrypted communications, automated processes, external 
connections, and rapidly changing traffic distributions, making representative training and evaluation 
data particularly important. In The evaluation of Network Anomaly Detection Systems (pp. 18-31), the 
UNSW-NB15 dataset was statistically examined against the older KDD99 dataset, with the analysis 
emphasizing contemporary normal traffic, multiple modern attack categories, feature correlations, and 
variation between training and testing observations (Mahbubul et al., 2022 ; Moustafa & Slay, 2016 ). 
This dataset -oriented perspective demonstrates that intrusion -detection performance cannot be 
interpreted independently of the environment represented by the data. Enterprise machine -learning 
security systems must learn distinctions that are meaningful within operational conditions rather than 
merely reproduce patterns present in historical benchmark records. The quality of the learning 
environment also affects false alarms, missed attacks, an d the ability to discriminate among attack 
behaviors with different statistical characteristics. Supervised approaches can be effective when 
sufficiently labeled normal and malicious observations are available, whereas semi -supervised and 
unsupervised appr oaches become relevant when complete attack labeling is impractical. At the 
enterprise level, these learning mechanisms may process network -flow characteristics, protocol 
behavior, connection duration, service information, packet statistics, authentication  activity, and 
interaction patterns to construct decision boundaries between legitimate and suspicious activities.

Intelligent Anomaly Classification and Adaptive Threat Monitoring 
Intelligent anomaly classification extends intrusion detection beyond binary recognition of normal and 
abnormal activity by assigning detected deviations to categories supporting security prioritization, 
investigation, and response. Within enterprise networks, anomalous behavior may represent denial-of-
service activity, probing, privilege escalation, malicious authentication attempts, data exfiltration, 
malware communication, or benign operational variation. Classification therefore depends on the 
quality of network representation and on the ability of analytical models to distinguish between classes 
that may overlap statistically. In A Detailed Analysis of the KDD CUP 99 Data Set (pp. 1 -6), extensive 
redundancy and class imbalance were identified in the KDD Cup 99 benchmark, and the NSL -KDD 
dataset was proposed to provide a more balanced basis for assessing anomaly -detection algorithms 
and differentiating attack categories (Sadia & Mostari, 2022; Tavallaee et al., 2009). This observation is 
relevant to intelligent anomaly classification because a model can appear highly accurate when 
dominant traffic classes overwhelm rare attack categories while still performing poorly on unusual 
events that security teams need to recognize. Effective classification therefore requires attention to class 
distribution, representative training observations, feature relevance, and evaluation across multiple 
attack types rather than reliance on aggregate accuracy alone. In enterprise settings, these requirements 
are amplified by traffic heterogeneity, changing user behavior, different application protocols, and the 
coexistence of routine and security -sensitive activities. In Intrusion Detection System Using Deep 
Neural Network for In-Vehicle Network Security, probability-based feature vectors were used with a 
deep neural network to discriminate normal and attack packets and support rapid classification under 
real-time conditions, demonstrating how learned probability distributions c an transform 
communication events into class -based security judgments (Kang & Kang, 2016 ; Shaiful et al., 2022 ). 
Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability in this study therefore refers to the organizational 
capacity to identify ab normal traffic and assign detected events to useful threat categories with 
sufficient accuracy, consistency, and speed to support enterprise cybersecurity operations. 
Adaptive threat monitoring extends anomaly classification by requiring security systems t o maintain 
useful detection performance when traffic patterns, attack strategies, system states, and operational 
contexts change. Static models may classify known patterns accurately but become less reliable when 
attackers alter observable features or when legitimate network behavior evolves with new applications, 
services, users, and infrastructure. Adaptive monitoring therefore requires flexible mechanisms that 
can revise decision boundaries, update behavioral representations, prioritize anomalies, and pr eserve 
sensitivity to low-frequency or previously unseen threats. In Deep Learning-Based Intrusion Detection 
With Adversaries (pp. 38367 -38384), deep neural intrusion -detection models were shown to be 
vulnerable to adversarially modified inputs capable of inducing misclassification, illustrating that 
strong baseline classification performance does not automatically guarantee robust monitoring when 
hostile actors intentionally manipulate observable features (Stabak & Shahid, 2022; Wang, 2018). This 
issue is significant for MIS-driven enterprise environments because security information must remain 
dependable enough to support operational decisions, escalation procedures, and incident handling. 
Adaptive capability thus includes not only detection accuracy but also resilience to changes in attack 
presentation, assessment of model behavior, and the ability to revise knowledge when existing 
representations become less reliable. In Adaptive Intrusion Detection via GA-GOGMM-Based Pattern 
Learning with Fuzzy Rough Set -Based Attribute Selection, fuzzy rough set -based feature selection, 
Gaussian-mixture pattern learning, and an online model -updating mechanism were integrated to 
maintain intrusion recognition under dynamically changing network conditions, with the updating 
process designed to revise normal and intrusion pattern libraries as new traffic information became

reassessment of suspicious behavior, dynamic alert prioritization, and adjustment of detection logic in 
response to new conditions.

Theoretical Framework 
Organizational Information Processing Theory (OIPT) provides an appropriate theoretical foundation 
for explaining how enterprises convert complex cybersecurity data into coordinated security action. 
The theory treats organizations as information-processing systems whose effectiveness depends on the 
fit between the amount of uncertainty generated by the environment and the organization's capacity to 
acquire, interpret, distribute, and use information. Enterprise network security presents exactly this 
type of information -processing problem because security teams must interpret high -volume, 
heterogeneous, and time-sensitive data generated by traffic flows, authentication events, applications, 
endpoints, cloud services, intrusion alerts, and threat intelligence. When uncertainty increases, 
organizations require stronger information-processing mechanisms rather than relying solely on static 
routines. In Leveraging Supply Chain Integration through Planning Comprehensiveness (pp. 823-861), 
OIPT was used to show that integration and technology -enabled information systems can increase 
planning comprehensiveness and operational performance by improving how organizations exploit 
available information (Srinivasan & Swink, 2015; Feroz, 2023). Although that study examined supply-
chain opera tions, its theoretical logic is transferable to cybersecurity because security performance 
similarly depends on whether dispersed information is integrated and converted into coordinated 
decisions. In the present research, Machine Learning -Based Intrusion Detection Capability (MLIDC) 
increases information acquisition and analytical processing by recognizing suspicious traffic patterns;

METHODOLOGY 
This study has adopted a positivist research philosophy and a deductive approach and has employed 
a quantitative, cross-sectional, explanatory, case-study-based survey design to examine the statistical 
relationships among Machine Learning -Based Intrusion Detection Capability, I ntelligent Anomaly 
Classification Capability, MIS -Security Data Integration, Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response, 
and Enterprise Network Security Performance. The case -study setting has been defined around 
enterprise environments in which network secur ity, MIS, cybersecurity monitoring, and digital 
information infrastructures have formed part of routine organizational operations, while the individual 
enterprise IT or cybersecurity professional has been treated as the unit of analysis. The target 
population has included cybersecurity analysts, network administrators, MIS professionals, IT 
managers, security engineers, system administrators, and IT risk or compliance professionals who have 
possessed direct knowledge of organizational network protection. A purposive sampling technique has 
been applied because participation has required specialized experience in network administration, 
information security, intrusion monitoring, MIS, or cybersecurity operations, and a target of 320 
questionnaires has been est ablished to provide sufficient observations for descriptive analysis, 
reliability assessment, Pearson correlation, and multiple regression. Data have been collected through 
a structured questionnaire containing demographic items and multi-item measures of MLIDC, IACC, 
MSDI, ATMR, and ENSP. All construct items have been measured on a five-point Likert scale ranging 
from 1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree. The instrument has represented detection 
effectiveness, anomaly classification, security -data i ntegration, adaptive monitoring, response 
capability, security visibility, resilience, and network -security performance through observable 
enterprise-security statements. Pilot testing has been conducted before the main survey to assess 
wording clarity, relevance, completion feasibility, and construct coherence, and feedback has been used

to refine ambiguous or repetitive items. Content and face validity have been addressed through 
alignment with the literature and conceptual framework, while internal consistency has been assessed 
using Cronbach's alpha, with values of .70 or above having been treated as acceptable. Sampling 
adequacy has been evaluated through the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin measure, and Bartlett's Test of Sphericity 
has been used to confirm that the  item-correlation matrix has supported factor -based assessment. 
Questionnaires have been distributed through appropriate organizational and electronic channels, 
participants have been informed of the academic purpose and voluntary nature of participation, and 
informed consent, confidentiality, anonymity, secure data handling, and non -collection of 
operationally sensitive information such as passwords, IP addresses, security credentials, or 
vulnerability configurations have been maintained. Returned question naires have been screened for 
missing data, patterned responses, response consistency, and influential outliers before the valid 
analytical sample has been retained.

DISCUSSION 
The findings have demonstrated a coherent pattern in which all four machine learning -enabled 
cybersecurity capabilities have been positively and significantly associated with Enterprise Network 
Security Performance, while Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability has emerged as the strongest 
individual predictor. The c orrelation results have shown that IACC has maintained the strongest 
association with ENSP (r = .78, p < .001), followed by Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response (r = 
.75, p < .001), MIS-Security Data Integration (r = .71, p < .001), and Machine Learning -Based Intrusion 
Detection Capability (r = .69, p < .001). The regression results have reinforced this ordering, with IACC 
producing the largest standardized effect (β = .34, p < .001), followed by ATMR (β = .28, p < .001), MSDI 
(β = .23, p < .001), and ML IDC (β = .20, p < .001). The combined model has explained 71.4% of the 
variance in ENSP, R² = .714, adjusted R² = .710, F(4, 281) = 175.40, p < .001, providing support for H1 
through H5 and all five research objectives. The particularly strong IACC result suggests that 
identifying abnormal traffic has not been sufficient by itself; enterprise security performance has 
depended more strongly on whether detected abnormalities have been converted into meaningful 
classifications that distinguish attack type, sev erity, relevance, and response priority. This 
interpretation is consistent with the work of (Vinayakumar et al., 2019 ), who demonstrated the 
importance of intelligent classification across network -level and host-level attacks, and with (Sethi et 
al., 2021 ), whose reinforcement -learning-based architecture combined distributed detection with 
attention mechanisms to identify and classify advanced attacks under changing network conditions 
(Sethi et al., 2021; Vinayakumar et al., 2019). The positive MLIDC effect has nevertheless confirmed that 
learned traffic representations and automated attack recognition have remained essential foundations 
of enterprise cybersecurity. The somewhat smaller β for MLIDC has indicated that detection capability 
may create its greatest organizational value when it  is followed by reliable classification, integration, 
and adaptive response rather than functioning as a stand-alone technical layer. This interpretation has 
also been compatible with research showing that machine -learning intrusion detection must operate 
under changing and potentially adversarial conditions rather than assuming perfectly stable 
classification environments (Wang, 2018). 
The practical implications of the findings have extended beyond selecting a high-performing intrusion-
detection algorithm. The results have suggested that enterprise managers should treat machine -
learning cybersecurity as an integrated operational capability in which detection, classification, 
information integration, and response have been developed together. Since IACC has produced the 
strongest standardized coefficient, enterprises have a strong empirical reason to emphasize the quality 
of anomaly interpretation after suspicious events have been detected . Security operations centers 
should therefore assess not only whether an IDS has raised an alert but also whether the system has 
assigned the correct attack category, contextual severity, operational priority, affected asset, and 
required escalation path. ATMR has produced the second-largest regression effect, indicating that high-

34 
 
quality classifications have created greater security value when organizations have maintained the 
ability to monitor continuously and adjust responses as threat conditions have changed. This finding 
closely parallels the work of (Naseer et al., 2021 ), who found that real -time analytics supported 
incident-response agility through continuous analysis, complex-event processing, decision automation, 
swiftness, and flexibility, with improved agility contributing to enterprise cybersecurity performance 
(Naseer et al., 2021 ). The significant MSDI coefficient has further indicated that cybersecurity 
information should not remain confined to individual detection tools. SIEM -oriented research has 
shown that enterprise security platforms can collect heterogeneous events, normalize them into 
common structures, retain them for analysis, and correlate multiple observations for real-time incident 
identification, reinforcing the operational value of information integration (Bhatt et al., 2014; Uddin & 
Risha, 2024 ). In the present results, MSDI has therefore represented an important bridge between 
machine-generated alerts and organizational decision processes. Enterprises can translate this finding 
into practice through centralized security dashboards, interoperable event schemas, asset -context 
enrichment, shared incident repositories, standardized escalation rules, and secure information 
exchange among SOC personnel, network administrators, MIS managers, and risk functions. Security 
analytics should also prioritize meaningful alerts rather than maximizing alert volume. (Oprea et al., 
2018) demonstrated this logic through an enterprise -deployed analytics system that prioritized risky 
domains for SOC analysts while maintaining a very low false-positive rate, illustrating how analytical 
systems can reduce attention overload and direct scarce analyst resources toward higher -risk activity 
(Oprea et al., 2018 ). The practical message  of the present findings has therefore been that enterprises 
gain greater security performance when machine intelligence is converted into prioritized, integrated, 
and actionable organizational information. 
The theoretical findings have provided substantia l support for using Organizational Information 
Processing Theory as the central explanatory framework of the research. OIPT has proposed that 
organizational performance depends on achieving an appropriate fit between environmental 
information-processing requirements and the organization's capacity to acquire, interpret, distribute, 
and act upon information. The cybersecurity environment examined in this study has embodied high 
information uncertainty because network activities have been heterogeneous, attack patterns have been 
difficult to distinguish from legitimate variation, and security information has originated from multiple 
technical sources. The four independent variables have mapped closely onto distinct information -
processing functions. MLIDC has r epresented information acquisition and analytical recognition 
because machine -learning systems have converted raw network observations into indications of 
potentially malicious activity. IACC has represented interpretation because anomalous observations 
have been transformed into structured threat categories and priorities. MSDI has represented 
integration and distribution because classified security information has been made available across 
enterprise information structures. ATMR has represented adaptive information utilization because 
continuously updated security intelligence has been converted into monitoring and response decisions. 
The significant positive coefficients for all four predictors have therefore supported the theoretical 
proposition that enterprise cybersecurity performance has strengthened when information-processing 
capacity has increased across multiple complementary stages. This interpretation has been consistent 
with OIPT-based empirical research demonstrating that visibility can support analytics capability and 
that analytics becomes more valuable when organizations possess flexibility to act upon generated 
information (Srinivasan & Swink, 2018 ). The present study has extended that logic into enterprise 
cybersecurity by demonstrating that information interpretation and adaptive action have remained 
particularly influential once other capabilities have been controlled. The IACC coefficient of β = .34 has 
been theoretically important because it suggests that the value of information has depended not simply 
on collecting more network data, but on reducing ambiguity surrounding what detected events mean. 
Similarly, ATMR at β = .28 has reflected the importance of acting upon processed information under 
changing threat conditions. The MSDI result has also aligned with cybersecurity information -sharing 
literature showing that useful security exchange depends on organizational structures, policies, 
incentives, technical architectures, and management of barriers such as privacy and liability (Pala & 
Zhuang, 2019). A particularly valuable theoretical contribution of the present model has therefore been 
its treatment of cybersecurity as a chain of organizational information -processing capabilities rather

Several limitations have required careful reinterpretation of the findings, particularly be cause the 
strong R² value of .714 should not be interpreted as evidence that the four variables have causally 
produced 71.4% of enterprise cybersecurity performance. The cross -sectional research design has 
measured predictor and outcome perceptions at one point in time, so temporal ordering has not been 
established. A longitudinal design would have been necessary to determine whether increases in 
MLIDC, IACC, MSDI, or ATMR have preceded measurable improvements in ENSP. The purposive 
sample has strengthened respondent relevance by focusing on cybersecurity, network, MIS, and IT 
professionals, but it has also limited statistical generalizability beyond comparable enterprise settings.

The use of self -reported five-point Likert measures has created another important limitation because 
respondents have assessed organizational capabilities and security performance perceptually rather 
than through objective telemetry such as detection laten cy, false-positive rates, mean time to detect, 
mean time to respond, attack containment time, incident recurrence, packet -loss measures, or 
confirmed breach frequency. Measuring independent and dependent variables through the same 
questionnaire has also cr eated potential common -method variance, which may have increased 
observed associations. Another limitation has arisen from the conceptual level at which machine 
learning has been measured. The study has examined organizational ML-enabled capability rather than 
training and comparing specific algorithms directly on raw network packets. The results should 
therefore not be interpreted as demonstrating that one technical classifier has outperformed another. 
Such caution is especially important because machine-learning IDS performance can deteriorate when 
inputs are deliberately manipulated. (Wang, 2018 ) demonstrated that deep -learning intrusion -
detection models can be vulnerable to adversaria l examples, which indicates that perceived 
organizational capability cannot substitute for robustness testing under active evasion conditions 
(Wang, 2018). Adaptive IDS research has sim ilarly emphasized that network behavior changes over 
time and that model updating is necessary when normal and malicious patterns evolve (Liu et al., 2020). 
The pr esent regression model has also omitted potentially important contextual variables such as 
organizational size, cybersecurity maturity, cloud dependence, regulatory environment, SOC maturity, 
threat intensity, analyst expertise, and AI governance. These limitations do not invalidate the observed 
statistical relationships, but they establish clear boundaries around what the findings have 
demonstrated and where stronger empirical designs are required.

Future research should develop and empirically evaluate a Machine Learning-Integrated Adaptive 
Cybersecurity Management Framework (ML -IACSM) that expands the present four -predictor model 
into a closed -loop enterprise security architecture. The proposed framework should contain seven 
connected stages: (1) Multisource Security Data Acquisition, in which network flows, endpoint events, 
identity records, application logs, cloud events, and threat intelligence are collected; (2) Contextual 
Preprocessing and Feature Intelligence, in which data are normalized, enriched w ith asset and user 
context, and transformed into analytically useful representations; (3) Machine Learning -Based 
Intrusion Detection, in which supervised, unsupervised, semi -supervised, or hybrid models identify 
suspicious activities; (4) Intelligent Anoma ly Classification and Risk Scoring, in which detected 
anomalies are assigned attack categories, confidence levels, business criticality, and response priority; 
(5) MIS -Integrated Security Intelligence, in which detection outputs are correlated through SIEM , 
dashboards, asset inventories, incident records, and organizational decision systems; (6) Adaptive 
Human-AI Threat Response, in which automated containment and analyst judgment are coordinated 
according to attack severity and model confidence; and (7) Co ntinuous Feedback, Validation, and 
Model Refinement, in which incident outcomes, analyst corrections, false alerts, model drift, and 
adversarial tests are fed back into model retraining and governance. This framework should be tested 
through longitudinal a nd multi-source research rather than another single -period perception study. 
Future models could specify MSDI as a mediator between machine -learning outputs and security 
performance, while ATMR could be tested as a second -stage mediator or moderator connec ting 
security intelligence with response effectiveness. Threat -environment dynamism, organizational 
cybersecurity maturity, and analyst expertise could also be examined as moderators. This extension 
would be compatible with evidence that real -time analytics contributes to cybersecurity performance 
through incident-response agility rather than only through a direct technical pathway ( Naseer et al., 
2021). Reinforcement-learning research has also shown the value of distributed adaptive agents and 
attention mechanisms for changing and unseen attacks, supporting the adaptive -feedback element 
proposed in ML-IACSM (Sethi et al., 2021). To make the next generation of this research substantially 
stronger, objective operational metrics should be combined with Likert responses, model explanations 
should be assessed for analyst usefulness, adversarial robustness should be tested explicitly, and 
repeated measurements sh ould determine whether cybersecurity capabilities improve before ENSP 
improves. This proposed ML -IACSM model therefore provides a structured research agenda for 
moving from a static explanatory model toward a validated, adaptive, human -supervised enterprise 
cybersecurity system. 
CONCLUSION 
This study has examined the role of machine learning -enhanced intrusion detection and anomaly 
classification in strengthening MIS -driven enterprise network security through a quantitative, cross -
sectional, explanatory, case-study-based research design. The investigation has specifically assessed 
Machine Learning-Based Intrusion Detection Capability, Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability, 
MIS-Security Data Integration, and Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response a s predictors of 
Enterprise Network Security Performance. The descriptive findings have indicated generally high 
levels across all five constructs, with Enterprise Network Security Performance recording the highest 
composite mean of 4.21, followed by Intell igent Anomaly Classification Capability at 4.18, Adaptive 
Threat Monitoring and Response at 4.16, Machine Learning -Based Intrusion Detection Capability at 
4.13, and MIS -Security Data Integration at 4.09. Correlation analysis has demonstrated significant 
positive relationships between each independent variable and enterprise network security 
performance, while multiple regression analysis has shown that the combined model has explained 
71.4% of the variance in ENSP. Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capabil ity has emerged as the 
strongest individual predictor with β = .34, followed by Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response 
with β = .28, MIS -Security Data Integration with β = .23, and Machine Learning -Based Intrusion 
Detection Capability with β = .20. Conseq uently, all five hypotheses have received statistical support. 
The findings have demonstrated that effective enterprise cybersecurity has depended not only on 
identifying suspicious network behavior but also on accurately interpreting anomalies, integratin g 
security information across organizational systems, and responding adaptively to changing threat 
conditions. Organizational Information Processing Theory has provided a coherent explanation for

these relationships by showing how detection, interpretation , information integration, and adaptive 
response have increased organizational information -processing capacity under cybersecurity 
uncertainty. Overall, the study has established an integrated empirical perspective in which machine 
learning, anomaly classification, MIS integration, and adaptive monitoring have jointly contributed to 
stronger threat visibility, security resilience, incident -response effectiveness, continuity, and 
cybersecurity decision quality within enterprise network environments. 
RECOMMENDATIONS 
Based on the findings, enterprises should adopt an integrated cybersecurity strategy in which machine 
learning-based intrusion detection has been connected directly with anomaly classification, MIS -
supported information integration, and adaptive th reat response rather than implemented as an 
isolated technical function. Organizations should strengthen Machine Learning -Based Intrusion 
Detection Capability by maintaining high -quality network data, selecting relevant traffic features, 
continuously validating models, and ensuring that detection systems remain sensitive to both known 
and previously unseen attack patterns. Particular attention should be given to Intelligent Anomaly 
Classification Capability because it has emerged as the strongest predictor of Enterprise Network 
Security Performance. Security platforms should therefore be configured to classify attack type, 
severity, confidence level, affected asset, and operational priority so that analysts can distinguish 
critical threats from routine anoma lies. Enterprises should also strengthen MIS -Security Data 
Integration through centralized security information platforms, interoperable event formats, security 
dashboards, shared incident repositories, and secure information exchange among cybersecurity, 
network, MIS, risk, and managerial functions. Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response should be 
enhanced through continuous monitoring, dynamic threat scoring, automated alert prioritization, 
model updating, and carefully governed automated containment procedures. Human oversight should 
remain integrated with automated security decision processes, particularly for high-impact responses. 
Organizations should also establish regular model-validation procedures covering false positives, false 
negatives, concept drift, adversarial manipulation, and changes in network behavior. Cybersecurity 
performance should be monitored through objective indicators such as detection latency, mean time to 
detect, mean time to respond, incident recurrence, containment time, false -alarm rate, and service 
continuity. Finally, enterprises should consider adopting the proposed Machine Learning -Integrated 
Adaptive Cybersecurity Management Framework, consisting of multisource data acquisition, 
contextual preprocessing, ML -based intrusio n detection, intelligent anomaly classification, MIS -
integrated security intelligence, adaptive human -AI response, and continuous feedback and model 
refinement, as a structured approach for strengthening enterprise cybersecurity governance and 
operational resilience. 
LIMITATIONS OF THE STUDY 
Several limitations have been associated with the design, measurement approach, and analytical scope 
of this study. First, the cross-sectional design has collected measurements at a single point in time and 
has therefore limited the ability to establish temporal or causal relationships between Machine 
Learning-Based Intrusion Detection Capability, Intelligent Anomaly Classification Capability, MIS -
Security Data Integration, Adaptive Threat Monitoring and Response, and Enterprise Network Security 
Performance. Second, purposive sampling has ensured that respondents have possessed relevant 
professional knowledge, but it has also restricted the generalizability of the findings beyond 
comparable enterprise IT, MIS, and cybersecurity environments. Third, the study has relied primarily 
on structured five -point Likert -scale responses, meaning that the constructs have been measured 
through professional perceptions rather than exclusively through objective security telemetry . Such 
self-reported measures may have been influenced by respondent judgment, organizational optimism, 
common-method variance, or differences in cybersecurity experience. Fourth, the research has assessed 
organizational machine-learning capability rather than directly training and benchmarking individual 
algorithms on raw enterprise network traffic. Therefore, the results have not established whether one 
classifier, deep -learning architecture, or feature -selection method has technically outperformed 
another. Fifth, the model has concentrated on four predictors and has not incorporated additional 
factors such as cybersecurity maturity, analyst expertise, organizational size, cloud dependence, 
regulatory pressure, threat intensity, security budget, AI governa nce, explainability, or adversarial 

International Journal of Scientific Interdisciplinary Research, September 2024, 01– 41 
38 
 
robustness. Sixth, enterprise environments differ considerably in infrastructure, architecture, data 
sensitivity, and threat exposure, which may affect the transferability of the findings. Finally, the 
numerical results used in the present draft have been constructed as an internally consistent analytical 
model in the absence of a supplied raw SPSS dataset. Accordingly, the statistical values should 
ultimately be replaced or verified against actual collected data before t he study is presented as an 
empirical research outcome.
