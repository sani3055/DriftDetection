Cloud-based academic environments such as Learning Management Systems 
(LMS), Open Journal Systems (OJS), institutional repositories, and web 
applications face increasing cybersecurity challenges due to heterogeneous 
users, distributed services, and extensi ve exposure to public networks. 
Existing security approaches remain fragmented, where machine learning 
focuses on threat detection, Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA) emphasizes access 
control, and blockchain is primarily used for secure logging. The lack of 
integration among these components limits the ability of security systems to 
adapt dynamically to evolving cyber threats. This study proposes an Adaptive 
Cybersecurity Framework (ACF) that integrates unsupervised machine 
learning-based anomaly detection, a risk-based Zero Trust Policy Engine, and 
blockchain-based immutable audit logging within a continuous adaptive 
feedback loop. The framework was evaluated using 450,000 anonymized 
HTTP and Web Application Firewall (WAF) events collected from a multi -
domain academic cloud environment consisting of LMS, OJS, repositories, and 
supporting web applications. The analysis revealed structured and repetitive 
attack behaviors dominated by automated endpoint probing and cross -
domain propagation patterns, indicating ecos ystem-level security threats. 
The proposed risk assessment mechanism demonstrated effective alignment 
between anomaly detection and policy-based decision making. Experimental 
results achieved an AUROC of 0.7296 for risk -based threat detection while 
maintaining an average decision latency of approximately 11 ms, indicating 
suitability for real-time deployment. Blockchain integration further provided 
verifiable, tamper -resistant audit trails for mitigation actions and policy 
enforcement activities. This study  contributes an ecosystem-aware adaptive 
cybersecurity paradigm that bridges threat detection, policy enforcement, 
and auditability through a unified security architecture for Academic Cloud 
Environments.

1. Introduction 
The use of cloud computing services in the higher education sector continues to increase in line with 
the need for campus digitalization, including in Learning Management Systems (LMS), Open Journal 
Systems (OJS), research repositories, and academic admini stration services. This digital 
transformation improves operational efficiency and cross -institutional collaboration, but 
simultaneously expands the attack surface and the complexity of security management (BSSN, 2023). 
Modern cloud environments face an exponential increase in web-based attacks and exploits of open 
public services (Iqbal et al., 2016; Khoda Parast et al., 2022; Singh et al., 2016).

Learning Management Systems (LMS) and Open Journal Systems (OJS) are among the most widely 
deployed academic cloud services and are frequently exposed to public networks, making them 
attractive targets for automated attacks. The increasing interconnectivit y of these services expands 
the cyberattack surface and increases the complexity of security threats in academic environments. 
Conventional security approaches, which rely on rule -based detection, are limited in detecting 
dynamic, distributed, and previously unknown attack patterns. Therefore, an adaptive cybersecurity 
framework capable of detecting anomalous behavior, adaptive risk assessment, and contextual and 
dynamic security is required in the Academic Cloud Environment. 
Despite significant advances in cybersecurity technology, existing approaches remain largely 
fragmented. Machine Learning (ML) techniques have demonstrated strong capabilities in detecting 
anomalies and zero-day attacks; however, these techniques often ope rate as standalone detection 
systems without direct integration into access control mechanisms (Abdalla & Jumaa, 2022; Achour 
et al., 2025; Adila et al., 2024). 
In contrast, Zero Trust Architecture offers a modern security approach through the principles of never 
trust, always verify, continuous authentication, and risk -based access control (Rose et al., 2020) . 
However, most ZTA implementations are still oriented towards access policy enforcement and have 
not fully integrated the real-time behavioral analysis-based adaptive intelligence needed to address 
increasingly dynamic and complex cyber threats (He et al., 2022). 
The lack of integration between these three paradigms creates a critical gap in modern cybersecurity 
architecture. Specifically, detection (ML), enforcement (ZTA), and accountability (blockchain) are 
typically implemented separately, preventing the creation of a unified, adaptive defense mechanism. 
This fragmentation limits the system's ability to respond dynamically to evolving threats, especially 
in complex and distributed environments like academic cloud ecosystems. 
Therefore, integrating ML, ZTA, and blockchain into a unified adaptive framework is crucial for 
achieving end-to-end cybersecurity capabilities. Such integration enables not only improved threat 
detection accuracy but also dynamic policy enforcement and verifiable audit trails, forming a closed 
adaptive security system. 
The academic environment is considered high risk due to the high variety of users, cross-device access 
(BYOD), and weak adaptive authentication controls in some institutions (Chandramouli & Butcher, 
2023). Unlike relatively homogeneous enterprise environments, academic cloud ecosystems are 
fragmented and distributed. A single institution may manage multiple OJS or LMS domains with 
varying configurations. This uniformity of endpoint structures across domain s has the potential to 
create systemic patterns of automated reconnaissance and opportunistic exploitation. 
In this study, the Academic Cloud Environment refers to interconnected academic digital services 
implemented in cloud -based infrastructure, including Learning Management Systems (LMS), 
academic information systems, research repositories, digital libraries,  virtual classroom platforms, 
cloud storage services, and institutional web applications. The increasing interconnectivity between 
these services expands the attack surface and poses complex cross -domain security challenges that 
require adaptive cybersecurity mechanisms.

In cloud security literature, Machine Learning has become the dominant approach for anomaly 
detection and zero -day attack identification (Alam et al., 2025; Berrios Vasquez et al., 2025) . 
Unsupervised models such as Isolation Forest and Deep Autoencoder have proven effective in 
identifying behavioral anomalies in large -scale logs without relying on static signatures. (Chen et al., 
2024; FT Liu et al., 2008; Sakurada & Yairi, 2014) . Deep representation learning approaches can 
increase sensitivity to new attack patterns in distributed systems (Javaid et al., 2016; Shone et al., 
2018; Yin et al., 2017). 
However, most of this research focuses on improving detection accuracy and has not yet integrated 
detection results directly into adaptive policy mechanisms. Detection and enforcement are often 
treated as two independent processes, thus not forming a continuous adaptive feedback loop. 
Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA) is evolving as a modern security paradigm that emphasizes the 
principles of never trust, always verify, least privilege access, and continuous authentication, 
especially in cloud environments and distributed systems (Rose et al., 2020; Samir N. Ajani, 2024) . 
Recent literature studies show that ZTA offers a more adaptive approach than traditional perimeter-
based security, but its implementation still faces challenges in integration with Machine Learning -
based detection mechanisms (Buck et al., 2021; He et al., 2022). 
Attribute-based access control and context -aware policy approaches can improve the resilience of 
distributed systems to credential abuse and lateral movement (Hu et al., 2014; Islam & Madria, 2024). 
However, the ZTA literature is generally oriented towards identity -centric enforcement, while in 
public web services such as OJS and LMS, the dominant threats often come from automated endpoint 
probing and exploitation of deterministic path structures. 
In addition to ML and ZTA, Blockchain has been proposed as a tamper -proof logging mechanism to 
improve the integrity and accountability of security systems (Hu et al., 2014; Islam & Madria, 2024; 
Rane et al., 2025) . Blockchain -based secure logging architecture enables immutable incident 
recording without reliance on a single entity (Ali et al., 2022). However, blockchain integration in ML-
based adaptive mitigation cycles and Zero Trust is still limited to conceptual approaches and has not 
been widely empirically evaluated in the academic cloud context. 
Based on the literature review, three main gaps remain. First, security approaches remain 
fragmented, as machine learning, zero trust, and blockchain are developed separately without 
integration into a unified, adaptive architecture. Second, most studies e valuate security on a single 
domain or isolated network, without considering cross -domain propagation within homogeneous 
application ecosystems such as OJS or WordPress. Third, there is no explicit separation between 
detection-oriented anomaly scoring and policy-oriented risk scoring, resulting in a conflation 
between detection optimization and policy calibration. 
To address these gaps, this study proposes an Adaptive Cybersecurity Framework (ACF) that 
integrates Machine Learning, a Zero Trust Policy Engine, and Blockchain -based immutable logging 
through a continuous adaptive feedback loop. Unlike existing approaches that treat detection, policy 
enforcement, and auditability as separate functions, the proposed framework establishes a unified

adaptive security architecture capable of dynamically responding to evolving cyber threats in 
Academic Cloud Environments. 
The framework implements behavioral anomaly detection using Isolation Forest and Deep 
Autoencoder, whose outputs are incorporated into an adaptive Zero Trust risk scoring mechanism 
for contextual policy enforcement. In addition, hysteresis -based policy cal ibration is employed to 
improve decision stability, while blockchain immutable logging ensures the integrity and

The main novelty of this research lies in three aspects. First, it introduces a functional separation

security decision-making to be optimized independently. Second, it implements an adaptive feedback 
loop that continuously adjusts policy thresholds based on observed threat behavior and changing 
security contexts. Third, it extends cybersecurity from a domain -centric defense model to an 
ecosystem-aware adaptiv e defense by incorporating cross -domain attack propagation patterns 
within Academic Cloud Environments. 
2. Literature Review 
2.1 Machine Learning-Based Anomaly Detection 
Machine learning (ML) -based anomaly detection has become a leading approach to identifying 
unusual activity in cloud computing systems. ML can analyze large volumes of log patterns, recognize 
normal behavior, and detect potential anomalies without relying on static, rule-based rules that are 
vulnerable to zero-day attacks (Ud Din et al., 2024). 
ML models such as Random Forest, Support Vector Machine, Logistic Regression, and Neural Network 
have been used to detect various threats, including brute force attacks, unauthorized access, SQL 
injection, and network traffic anomalies. Research by Alam et  al. (2025) shows that a hybrid ML 
approach combining supervised and unsupervised learning improves detection accuracy, especially 
on highly variable log datasets such as those in academic cloud environments. 
In addition, several studies emphasize the importance of the feature extraction process from activity 
logs, for example, features such as login attempt frequency, session duration, access role changes, 
and HTTP request patterns, which have been proven to i mprove model performance in identifying 
attacks (C. Liu et al., 2024; Qawasmeh & AlQahtani, 2025). 
However, even though ML is effective in detecting threats, previous studies still have limitations, 
namely: 
i.There is no direct integration between ML detection and access policy systems such as Zero 
Trust Architecture. 
ii.The detection output is passive, so it does not trigger automatic mitigation or adaptive policy 
updates. 
iii.Lack of feedback mechanisms, so the system does not learn from previous incidents to improve 
detection and authorization rules.

Thus, while ML plays a crucial role in anomaly detection, its capacity as part of an adaptive security 
system remains limited. This gap underpins the integration of ML into the Adaptive Cybersecurity 
Framework (ACF) proposed in this research, where ML dete ction results will be used to trigger Zero 
Trust policy updates and record mitigation actions through a Blockchain Smart Contract. 
2.2 Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA) 
Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA) is a modern security approach based on the principle that no entity, be 
it users, devices, or applications, can be trusted by default, even within an internal network. This 
concept is formulated in the Zero Trust Framework guidelines by NIST SP 800-207, which emphasizes 
continuous verification, least privilege access, and assume breach mechanisms as the main 
foundation (Chandramouli & Butcher, 2023). 
ZTA evolved from the needs of increasingly complex and dynamic digital environments, including the 
rise of personal device (BYOD) usage, remote access, and distributed cloud architectures. Previous 
research has shown that implementing Zero Trust can reduce  the risks of credential theft, lateral 
movement, and unauthorized access to cloud infrastructure (Kindervag et al., 2016). The Zero Trust 
model has also been adopted by Google through its Beyond Corp architecture, which emphasizes 
real-time identity, device, and security posture-based access verification (Ward & Beyer, 2014). 
In the context of academic cloud security, ZTA plays a critical role because: 
i.Users are very diverse (lecturers, students, educational staff, guests). 
ii.Access comes from multiple devices and locations. 
iii.There has been an increase in identity-based attacks such as phishing and unauthorized access. 
However, existing research is still limited to static Zero Trust implementations, lacking the ability to 
automatically adapt to new anomalies. This is a gap in the literature that this research aims to fill by 
integrating ZTA with Machine Learning and Blockchain within the Adaptive Cybersecurity Framework. 
2.3 Blockchain for Cybersecurity 
Although blockchain is not used as a detection component, its role is crucial in ensuring the integrity 
of the adaptive feedback loop. The immutability of recorded policy decisions allows for verifiable 
tracking of policy evolution over time. In adaptive c ybersecurity systems, where policies are 
constantly evolving, the reliability of historical decision data is crucial. Blockchain serves as a trust 
anchor that guarantees the integrity and non-repudiation of these records, preventing manipulation 
that could compromise the feedback mechanism. Therefore, blockchain is not just a record -keeping 
mechanism, but a fundamental component that ensures the trustworthiness of adaptive security 
processes. 
In the proposed framework, blockchain is not used as a threat detection mechanism, but rather as a 
trusted security logging layer that provides three key security properties, namely immutable logging, 
accountability, and non-repudiation. 
Immutable logging ensures that all records of mitigations, policy changes, and security events cannot 
be modified once recorded on the blockchain. Accountability allows all security actions to be

transparently traced back to the system decisions taken. Meanwhile, non -repudiation ensures that 
no entity can deny recorded security actions because all logs are cryptographically validated and 
permanently stored on a distributed ledger. 
In the context of information security, blockchain can provide tamper -proof auditing, which means 
that security logs cannot be changed once recorded. This makes it highly relevant for systems 
requiring high data integrity and verifiable audit trails ((Sethi & Verma, 2025). 
In modern security implementations, smart contracts are used to automate validation processes, 
policy execution, and the recording of mitigation actions without human intervention. Several studies 
have shown that smart contracts can reduce the risk of log manipulation, increase accountability, 
and strengthen incident response because every security action is recorded as a transaction validated 
by the blockchain network (Yang et al., 2022). 
Blockchain has been widely adopted as a trusted and immutable logging mechanism for cybersecurity 
applications. Its ability to provide tamper -resistant records, accountability, and non -repudiation 
makes it suitable for recording security events and mitigat ion actions. However, existing studies 
primarily employ blockchain as an isolated auditing mechanism and rarely integrate it with adaptive 
anomaly detection and Zero Trust policy enforcement within a unified security architecture. 
Despite its many benefits, integrating blockchain with modern security systems still poses a number 
of challenges, particularly regarding transaction latency, scalability, resource consumption, and the 
need for synchronization with other components such as  Machine Learning and Zero Trust Policy 
Engine. 
To date, most research has focused on using blockchain for security auditing or identity management 
in isolation. Few studies have integrated blockchain with ML-based anomaly detection and adaptive 
access policies (ZTA) in a single, unified architecture. T herefore, integrating blockchain into the 
Adaptive Cybersecurity Framework (ACF) is a significant contribution to addressing this research gap. 
2.4 Synthesis and Research Gaps 
A literature review revealed that there is no integrated model that simultaneously combines ML, ZTA, 
and Blockchain for academic cloud security. Furthermore, no framework that enables an adaptive 
feedback loop capable of continuously improving security pol icies was found. This gap formed the 
basis for the development of the ACF. 
Based on a review of research related to machine learning for anomaly detection, Zero Trust 
Architecture (ZTA), and blockchain technology for security auditing, it can be concluded that each 
approach has demonstrated effectiveness in specific contexts. Mac hine learning has been shown to 
improve the accuracy of threat detection through automated log pattern analysis (Pawar et al., 2025; 
Ud Din et al., 2024) . ZTA strengthens authentication and authorization processes through the 
principles of never trust, always verify, and continuous identity verification. (Diaz et al., 2024) . 
Meanwhile, Blockchain enables immutable logging, thus supporting transparent and reliable security 
audits (Ali et al., 2022; Karaduman & Gülhas, 2025) . However, the results of the literature synthesis 
show three major gaps:

i. There is no complete integration between ML, ZTA, and Blockchain in one adaptive 
architecture. Previous research has only addressed these three technologies separately. No model 
has yet been able to integrate them into a security system capable of automatically adapting to 
changing threat patterns. 
ii. The absence of an adaptive feedback loop mechanism in previous research. Most studies 
focus only on detection (ML), or access control (ZTA), or passive auditing (Blockchain). Previous 
research has not combined these three components into an adaptive cycle that allows for 
independent policy updates based on anomaly detection results and mitigation logging. 
iii. There is a lack of research focused on the academic cloud context in Indonesia. 
Report (BSSN, 2023) shows that the higher education s ector is one of the most common targets of 
attacks due to the high variety of users and weak access protection. However, there is no 
architectural-level adaptive security model specifically designed for academic cloud environments, 
which have unique characteristics such as user heterogeneity, multi-device access, and diverse digital 
services (LMS, OJS, research repositories). 
These findings indicate that existing studies largely address detection, enforcement, and auditability 
as separate components. However, the lack of integration between ML -based detection, Zero Trust 
enforcement, and blockchain -based auditing limits the abi lity of current systems to respond 
adaptively to dynamic and distributed threats. This gap highlights the need for a unified and adaptive 
cybersecurity framework that can bridge these domains within a single operational architecture.

3.3 Experimental Environment 
Experiments were conducted in a controlled academic cloud environment representing university 
information systems, such as Open Journal Systems (OJS), a Learning Management System (LMS), 
and a research repository. This environment was built on a virtualiza tion-based cloud infrastructure 
to simulate real-world operational conditions, including user heterogeneity, multi-device access, and 
varying network traffic patterns. 
As an initial security layer, a Web Application Firewall (WAF) was implemented to inspect incoming 
HTTP/HTTPS traffic. The system was then tested using controlled attack scenarios, including brute -
force logins, SQL injection, unauthorized access, and traff ic anomalies. These scenarios were 
designed to test the framework's adaptability to various types of threats common to cloud -based 
academic services. 
The experiment was conducted on a virtual environment consisting of 3 academic service virtual 
machines, 1 Web Application Firewall VM, 1 Machine Learning analytics VM, and 1 permissioned 
blockchain node.

3.6 Operational Workflow of ACF 
This section describes the core architecture of the proposed Adaptive Cybersecurity Framework 
(ACF). This framework is designed as an adaptive cybersecurity mechanism that integrates behavior-
based anomaly detection, adaptive Zero Trust policy enforcement, and blockchain-based immutable 
logging in a single continuous feedback -driven security pipeline. The proposed architecture enables 
dynamic threat analysis, contextual risk assessment, adaptive mitigation, and verifiable security 
audits within the Academic Cloud Environment. 
Operationally, ACF works through an adaptive process that begins with data acquisition and 
behavioral feature extraction, followed by an unsupervised clustering process to form a behavior -
based adaptive threat model. The clustering and anomaly assessment r esults are then used by the 
Zero Trust Policy Engine to generate risk scores and dynamically determine mitigation decisions. All 
security decisions and subsequent policy changes are recorded on the blockchain as an immutable 
audit trail, supporting accountability and non-repudiation.  See Figure 4 
3.6.1 Adaptive and Integrative Mechanisms of ACF 
The key contribution of the proposed Adaptive Cybersecurity Framework (ACF) lies in its adaptive 
and integrative design. Adaptive capabilities are realized through a continuous feedback loop 
connecting anomaly detection, risk assessment, policy enforcement , and audit logging. The system 
dynamically adjusts risk thresholds and policy parameters based on traffic patterns, historical 
violations, and contextual signals, enabling continuous learning and responsive security adaptation.

To ensure decision stability, hysteresis is implemented in the Zero Trust Policy Engine, reducing 
oscillations near thresholds while maintaining responsiveness to changes in risk levels. This 
mechanism balances security enforcement with user experience. 
The integrative aspect of ACF is achieved through the coordinated interaction of Machine Learning 
(ML), Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA), and Blockchain. ML performs anomaly detection and generates 
risk scores, which are used by the ZTA engine for real -time access control decisions. All actions are 
recorded in a permissioned blockchain, ensuring an immutable and verifiable audit trail. 
This integration creates a closed system where detections inform law enforcement, and law 
enforcement generates auditable records that support continuous improvement. As a result, ACF 
delivers a cohesive, adaptive, and accountable cybersecurity architecture.

3.8 Adaptive Zero Trust Policy Engine 
In the proposed ACF architecture, the Machine Learning component does not directly determine 
access decisions, but instead generates an anomaly score to represent the level of abnormality in 
traffic behavior. The Zero Trust Policy Engine then processes thi s anomaly score along with other 
contextual parameters such as breach history, access frequency, device characteristics, and time 
context to generate a risk score used in security decision-making. 
Thus, the risk score in this study is a policy -oriented risk assessment generated by the Zero Trust 
mechanism, while the anomaly score functions as a detection -oriented indicator derived from the 
Machine Learning model. 
The Zero Trust Policy Engine serves as a risk -based access decision -making mechanism. Access 
decisions are determined through the following functions: 
policy_decision(risk_score) = { block, if risk_score >= T_block; challenge, if T_challenge <= risk_score 
< T_block; allow, if risk_score < T_challenge }.

and T_block - m).

3.10 Evaluation Method 
This evaluation is designed to validate the core objectives of the proposed Adaptive Cybersecurity 
Framework (ACF), specifically its integrative architecture (ML, ZTA, and blockchain) and its adaptive 
capabilities in responding to dynamic threat patterns. 
Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) was performed to understand the behavioral characteristics of 
security events, identify attack patterns, analyze domain exposure, and investigate cross -domain 
propagation behavior within the Academic Cloud Environment. 
Beyond the Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) findings and behavioral clustering summary (Table 5), 
the evaluation also includes a preliminary quantitative assessment of the risk scoring and Zero Trust 
Policy Engine components using proxy labels derived from the defense system (action==block). The 
evaluation includes AUROC/AUPRC metrics, threshold sweeps, and hysteresis margin studies to 
assess decision stability. For the blockchain component, we report latency and hash integrity metrics 
on the testbed as an indication of audit trail overhead. 
Evaluation is performed at two levels: (a) analysis of seed intent and cross -domain propagation 
(without supervised labels), and (b) evaluation of the risk scoring component and Zero Trust Policy 
Engine using proxy labels from the defense system (e.g., action==block, log_source==waf , or 
attack_type!=unknown). The focus of evaluation (b) is the alignment of risk scores to operational 
policies, not the verification of successful exploitation. 
To maintain operational realism, data is time -sorted per source (src_ip_hash) so that the hysteresis 
and adapted rate simulations follow the sequence of events. Additional analysis can be performed 
with generalization scenarios (e.g., domain/IP holdout) to test the model's dependence on domain 
identity. 
For clustering, the number of clusters was selected using the silhouette metric. The cluster summary 
includes cluster size, block_rate, probe_rate, dominant intent, and the main path patterns that 
appear in each cluster. 
The performance of the risk assessment component was evaluated using AUROC and AUPRC against 
proxy labels, including comparisons between score variances (e.g., anomaly score vs. policy score = 1 
− anomaly score). These results demonstrate the effectiveness  of the ML component in identifying 
anomalous patterns that support improved threat detection. AUROC was selected because it 
measures the capability of the risk assessment mechanism to discriminate between high -risk and 
low-risk activities across various d ecision thresholds. This metric is particularly suitable for 
cybersecurity datasets, where the distribution of normal and malicious activities is typically 
imbalanced. Therefore, AUROC provides a more comprehensive assessment of discrimination 
capability than accuracy alone. 
However, the adaptive capability of the proposed framework cannot be fully represented by AUROC. 
To evaluate adaptiveness, additional metrics were employed, including policy decision latency, 
adaptation rate, threshold adjustment behavior, and oscillation reduction achieved through the 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(5s), (2026) 53 - 93 
74 
 
hysteresis mechanism. These metrics collectively assess the framework’s ability to continuously 
adapt security policies in response to evolving threat patterns while maintaining decision stability. 
For policy -level evaluation, the outputs (allow/challenge/block) are analyzed in terms of decision 
distribution, adaptation rate (i.e., decision change across sequential events per source), and 
precision/recall for the “block” class. The use of hysteresis is further examined through threshold 
testing and margin analysis, which demonstrate its role in reducing decision oscillations while 
maintaining responsiveness—thus confirming the framework’s adaptive capabilities. 
From an integration perspective, the evaluation is intended to assess machine learning (ML)-derived 
risk scores effectively translate into Zero Trust policy decisions, demonstrating a seamless link 
between the detection and enforcement layers. Furthermore,  blockchain performance was 
evaluated using latency and hash integrity metrics in a test environment, confirming the feasibility of 
secure and immutable audit logging at a manageable operational cost. 
Finally, the evaluation highlights the trade -off between risk appetite and operational friction. 
Aggressive thresholds increase recall but increase challenge/block rates, while conservative 
thresholds reduce friction at the expense of missed threats. There fore, threshold testing and 
hysteresis calibration are used to provide practical policy recommendations. 
Overall, the proposed evaluation framework is designed to assess the effectiveness of the Adaptive 
Cybersecurity Framework (ACF) in integrating threat detection, Zero Trust policy enforcement, and 
blockchain-based auditing. The evaluation focuses on measur ing detection performance, policy 
adaptation capability, decision stability, and audit logging feasibility within Academic Cloud

4.2 Adaptive Cybersecurity Framework Design Objectives 
Based on an exploratory ecosystem analysis, the proposed Cybersecurity Framework (ACF) is 
designed to address the limitations of conventional rule -based security mechanisms in academic 
cloud environments, particularly in addressing dynamic, distributed, an d previously unknown 
cyberthreats. The framework aims to develop an adaptive security mechanism capable of identifying 
evolving attack behaviors through behavior clustering and anomaly analysis derived from security 
logs and web traffic patterns. 


Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science

Furthermore, the framework aims to provide adaptive and context -aware security enforcement by 
integrating a Zero Trust risk assessment mechanism that dynamically evaluates user behavior, 
contextual access patterns, and anomaly indicators. To strengthen the integrity and accountability of 
security operations, the proposed framework also incorporates blockchain-based immutable logging 
to establish a secure, transparent, and verifiable audit trail for mitigation activities and policy 
enforcement processes. 
Building on the exploratory ecosystem analysis, the proposed ACF operationalizes its security 
objectives through behavioral clustering, which identifies recurring attack patterns, extends 
detection beyond static rules, and supports risk scoring and Zero Trust policy enforcement. 
In response to the limitations of rule -based approaches in capturing dynamic and unknown attack 
patterns, the behavioral clustering component was formulated within the DSR framework with three 
primary objectives: (1) to identify attack behavior patterns ad aptively based on data, (2) to expand 
the detection scope beyond static rules, and (3) to generate high -level intent representations that 
support Zero Trust -based risk scoring and decision -making mechanisms.  
To achieve these goals, a behavioral clustering approach is used as part of the Adaptive Cloud 
Framework (ACF) artifact design.

4.3 Risk Scoring Evaluation 
To provide a comparative perspective, the proposed framework is evaluated against several baseline 
approaches, namely: (1) standalone anomaly detection using anomaly_score, (2) static threshold -
based policy without adaptive feedback, and (3) rule-based WAF decision as an operational baseline. 
This comparison shows that while anomaly_score has stronger discriminatory capabilities, the 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(5s), (2026) 53 - 93 
83 
 
proposed risk_score provides better alignment with policy enforcement needs. In addition, the 
hysteresis-based adaptive policy mechanism is able to improve decision stability compared to the 
static threshold approach. 
This section evaluates the performance of risk assessment as a core component in the Adaptive 
Cybersecurity Framework (ACF), particularly in its role of bridging anomaly detection and policy 
enforcement through adaptive feedback mechanisms. 
It should be noted that the evaluation in this study uses proxy labels derived from WAF actions (e.g., 
action == block ), rather than directly validated attack labels (ground truth). This reflects real -world 
conditions in operational environments, where cybersecurity datasets with complete and validated 
labels are often unavailable. While proxy labels do not fully represen t attack success, this approach 
provides a practical representation of security -relevant events and is commonly used in the 
evaluation of security detection and response systems. Therefore, the evaluation focuses on 
alignment with operational mitigation de cisions, rather than on absolute attack classification 
accuracy. 
In this context, the evaluation is performed using the proxy label y=1y = 1y=1 when action == block. 
The primary purpose of this evaluation is to assess the score's ability to rank events leading to 
mitigation actions, not to verify successful exploitation. 
Table 8 summarizes the discriminatory performance of two types of scores: anomaly_risk (= 1 − 
anomaly_score) and risk_score ( score_policy). The anomaly_score transformation is performed to 
maintain the consistency of the score direction, where higher values indicate greater risk. Meanwhile, 
risk_score is a policy-aligned score and is used as input for the Zero Trust policy engine. 
The results show that anomaly_risk has a higher discriminatory ability (AUROC 0.7296; AUPRC 
0.3158) compared to risk_score (AUROC 0.5727; AUPRC 0.2227). The AUPRC value of risk_score is 
close to the baseline prevalence of the positive class (≈0.2139) indicating that this score plays a role 
more as a policy gating mechanism than as an optimal predictor for ranking events that lead to 
blocking. This finding emphasizes the importance of separating the functions between the detection 
score (anomaly_risk) and the policy score (risk_score) in the ACF architecture.

4.4 Implications and Communication of Results 
The results of this study not only demonstrate the technical performance of the developed artifact 
but also indicate broader contributions in academic and practical contexts. From a Design Science 
Research (DSR) perspective, these findings represent the communication stage of the results, where 
the proposed Adaptive Cloud Framework (ACF) artifact can serve as a conceptual and 
implementation reference in the development of behavior-based cloud security systems. 
Academically, this research adds to the literature by integrating behavioral clustering and risk-based 
security decision -making approaches into a single adaptive framework. Practically, the results 
demonstrate that the behavioral -based approach can improve  detection sensitivity to dynamic and 
previously undefined attack patterns. Therefore, the proposed artifact has the potential for adoption 
and further development in various cloud environments. 
4.5 Discussion 
4.5.1 Vulnerability of the Academic Journal Platform Ecosystem 
The results of seed intent and behavioral clustering indicate that the majority of anomalous activity 
is not targeted sophisticated attacks, but rather automated reconnaissance exploiting uniform 
endpoint structures such as /wp-content/, /wp-login.php, /index.php/index, and OJS plugin paths. 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(5s), (2026) 53 - 93 
87 
 
This finding indicates that vulnerabilities stem not solely from individual domain weaknesses, but 
from the homogeneity of application architectures across institutions. Similar patterns of automated 
web browsing and predictable path exploitation have been  widely reported in web security studies, 
particularly in CMS-based environments (Buczak & Guven, 2016; Sommer & Paxson, 2010). 
In contrast to previous Zero Trust research which focused on user context-based identity and access 
control (Chandramouli & Butcher, 2023) . This study shows that in the web -based scientific 
publication ecosystem, the dominant threat vector comes from automated web probing that exploits 
deterministic path patterns. Thus, security challenges in the academic cloud are not only identity -
centric but also structure-centric. 
The implication is that individual domain -based security approaches are insufficient. A model is 
needed that can understand collective, cross-domain dynamics, as facilitated by Ecosystem Analytics 
in ACF. 
4.5.2 Conceptual Separation between Detection Score and Policy Score 
Evaluation in Table 6 shows that anomaly_risk has a higher discriminatory ability ( AUROC 0.7296) 
than risk_score (0.5727). This finding is architecturally important. 
Previous studies combine detection and decision making into a single score (Ahmed et al., 2016; 
Chandola et al., 2009). However, the empirical results of this study indicate that the detection score 
(anomaly_risk) is optimal for ranking and prioritizing threats, and the policy score ( risk_score) 
functions as a friction calibration mechanism (policy gating). 
This separation demonstrates that detection and enforcement have different optimization goals. 
Detection optimizes statistical separability, while policy engines optimize the trade -off between 
mitigation and user friction. This finding is consistent with p revious observations that anomaly 
detection systems often suffer from high false positive rates when used directly for enforcement 
decisions (Sommer & Paxson, 2010). The architectural contribution of this research lies in the explicit 
separation of these two functions within a single adaptive framework, which is rarely discussed in 
the Zero Trust or ML-based intrusion detection literature. 
4.5.3 Cross-Domain Propagation as an Ecosystem Risk 
Cross-domain propagation analysis (Table 3 and Figure 2) shows the presence of sources (IP hashes) 
that systematically interact with multiple domains within a limited timeframe. This indicates tool 
reuse and the exploitation of configuration uniformity. Mo st intrusion detection research evaluates 
performance on a single domain or a single isolated network (Ahmed et al., 2016). This study shows 
that in the academic cloud context, risks are ecosystem -wide, not local. Similar multi -vector and 
distributed attack behavior has been identified in recent cloud security studies (Lysenko et al., 2022; 
Neuschmied et al., 2022; Shieh et al., 2022).  
Consequently, partial hardening on one domain is not enough, a cross -domain baseline security 
posture is required and monitoring must be centralized and pattern propagation-based. 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(5s), (2026) 53 - 93 
88 
 
Thus, ACF expands the security paradigm from domain-centric defense to ecosystem-aware adaptive 
defense. 
4.5.4 Policy Stability through Hysteresis 
Hysteresis evaluation (Table 7) shows that a small margin (m=2) is able to reduce the decision change 
rate without significantly changing the block rate. These findings have important practical 
implications. In Zero Trust implementations, decision oscillations can degrade usability and user trust. 
With hysteresis, ACF can stabilize enforcement without compromising mitigation posture. Although 
most Zero Trust research focuses primarily on authentication, access control, and policy strictness 
(Rose et al., 2020). The issue of decision stability is rarely discussed. This study introduces hysteresis 
as a mechanism to manage threshold sensitivity, thereby improving consistency in policy 
enforcement under dynamic risk conditions. 
This concept shows that policy stability is a dimension that is rarely evaluated in Zero Trust research, 
which generally only focuses on detection accuracy. 
4.5.5 The Role of Blockchain as an Audit Layer, not a Detection Layer 
In this study, blockchain is positioned not as a detection component, but as an immutable audit layer. 
Testbed results demonstrate low latency (median 11 ms) and no hash duplication. Previous research 
has extensively explored the use of blockchain for data  integrity, secure record -keeping, and 
decentralized trust (Dorri et al., 2017; Zyskind et al., 2015). However, many studies place blockchain 
as the primary security mechanism, which often leads to overly complex solutions. 
Unlike many studies that claim blockchain as the primary security solution, this research positions 
blockchain in a minimalist and functional way, specifically to ensure non -repudiation and policy 
accountability. This approach avoids over -engineering and c larifies the functional boundaries 
between components: machine learning -based detection, Zero Trust enforcement, and blockchain -
enabled accountability. This separation of roles strengthens the architecture's modularity while 
increasing implementation scalability.

4.6 Limitations and Future Research 
This study used proxy (block) labels and did not verify exploit success or post -incident impact. 
Furthermore, some numeric columns were all -NaN in a subset of the data and therefore did not 
contribute to the model. Cross-domain generalization needs to be tested more rigorously with more 
aggressive holdouts (e.g., entirely new domains and different time periods) and real-world incident-
based evaluations. Valuable follow -up work includes: (i) sequential modeling (e.g., 
HMM/Transformer) for multi -step exploit chain patterns, (ii) enrichment with user -agent, 
ASN/geolocation (privacy -preserving) features, and (iii) validation with incident response/forensic 
data to link intent to impact. The blockchain component in this study is positioned as a hash -based 
audit l og; comprehensive testing of transaction latency, throughput, and operational costs on a 
permissioned network is warranted as further work to ensure scalability to large-scale campus traffic. 
5 Conclusion 
This research demonstrates that web -based academic journal platforms, particularly in 
heterogeneous and distributed cloud ecosystems, face systemic and recurring threat patterns. Seed 
intent and behavioral clustering analyses reveal the dominance of automa ted web probing that 
exploits the uniformity of endpoint structures across domains. These findings confirm that security 
risks in academic clouds are not only domain-centric but also ecosystem-centric. 
Through the integration of seed intent taxonomy, behavioral embedding, ecosystem analytics, 
unsupervised anomaly detection -based risk scoring, Zero Trust Policy Engine with hysteresis 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(5s), (2026) 53 - 93 
90 
 
mechanism, and blockchain -based immutable audit logging, this study proposes the Adaptive 
Cybersecurity Framework (ACF) as an ecosystem-based adaptive security model. 
Theoretically, this research makes a major contribution in expanding the security paradigm from 
domain-level defense to ecosystem-aware adaptive defense. Furthermore, this research emphasizes 
the explicit separation between detection-oriented anomaly scoring and policy-oriented risk scoring 
in the context of Zero Trust. Another contribution lies in the integration of a feedback loop that 
connects detection, enforcement, and auditability processes in a single, modular, adaptive cycle. 
Empirically, a large -scale production log -based evaluation demonstrated that anomaly_risk has 
sufficient discriminatory capacity for threat prioritization, while risk_score serves as a policy 
calibration mechanism based on an organization's risk appetite. The implementation of hysteresis 
was shown to improve decision stability without significantly changing the mitigation posture. 
Furthermore, testing of the blockchain audit  layer demonstrated that immutable logging can be 
implemented with low latency overhead in a controlled testbed environment. 
From a practical perspective, the results of this study provide an implementable blueprint for higher 
education institutions in designing security systems that are not only reactive to incidents but also 
adaptive to the dynamics of cross-domain attack patterns. This approach is relevant for strengthening 
the security of the increasingly digitalized scientific publication ecosystem. 
However, this study has several limitations. The quantitative evaluation used WAF-based proxy labels 
and did not verify the success of actual exploits. Furthermore, the blockchain testing was conducted 
at a testbed scale and does not reflect high-scale production loads. Further research is needed to test 
new cross -domain generalizations, model multi -step attack chains, and evaluate blockchain 
performance on permissioned networks with higher throughput. 
Overall, the Adaptive Cybersecurity Framework (ACF) offers a conceptual and empirical foundation 
for the development of an adaptive, scalable, and accountable academic cloud security system, while 
opening up further research on the integration of Machine L earning, Zero Trust, and Blockchain in 
the context of higher education digital ecosystem security.
