Abstract
This article reviews approaches based on artificial intelligence (AI), which contributes to the security of cyber environ-
ments. We examine existing techniques using several indicators: explainability, performance and robustness. These 
indicators have been chosen based on their importance for user acceptance and interpretability of the approach. Indeed, 
the AI field is vast and is divided into several sub-domains. The two most well-known sub-domains are symbolic AI (rep-
resentation of knowledge, rules and operations based on symbols) and numeric AI (calculations and algorithms using 
numeric information, focusing on the result, not the representation of knowledge). While most approaches investigated 
come from numeric AI, we conclude on the need for hybrid AI systems, combining the advantages of both AI sub-fields 
while maximising the protection provided against cyberattacks.

1 Introduction
The Internet has dramatically expanded the scope of the cyber environment by increasing the number of interconnected 
devices, e.g., the Internet of Things (IoT) and wireless (sensor) networks. In addition to traditional computing devices, appli-
cations, systems, and services, the cyber environment now includes data and software stored on removable media and

transmitted over various types of networks, as well as the people who use these devices and networks and the physical 
structures hosting them [1].
Not a day goes by without news demonstrating the vulnerability of today’s IT systems and applications. The number of 
attacks and data breaches is on the rise [2], while attacks are becoming increasingly precise and sophisticated. Additionally, 
the expansion of cloud computing usage depicts a new threat landscape; in France, attacks on the cloud have increased 
by 630% in 2020 compared to 2019 [3]. Each wrongly configured cloud identity becomes an opportunity for attackers, and 
privileged access is aimed by almost all advanced attacks [4].
While some things may appear obvious, such as the need to protect enterprises’ operational parts, not all threats are always 
well identified in this highly evolving landscape. Most requirements and regulations are known to human users, such as 
business leaders’ legal obligations and responsibilities in the operation and control of their IT. Implementing system protec-
tion in the form of a security policy becomes mandatory. It encompasses developing procedures, defining the actions to be 
undertaken and the persons responsible, determining the concerned data in electronic and paper form (files, messages, etc.), 
network transactions, software applications and databases, not to mention service continuity and disaster recovery plans.
Cybersecurity is a subset of information security that protects electronic data and information. Cybersecurity considers 
security at an organisational level and at all levels (including physical security). Cybersecurity protects devices, networks, and 
sensitive information from unauthorised access, use, disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction.
When considering cybersecurity in a vast and complex environment, one must encompass dimensions and axes beyond 
network security of the online world, integrating data and physical security. In cybersecurity, security is evaluated through 
three fronts: confidentiality, integrity, and availability (also known as the CIA triad). For a specific organisation or context, 
the emphasis on these factors may differ, thus resulting in different choices regarding security and privacy tools and imple-
mentations. For example, in the case of an organisation that considers confidentiality to be of the highest importance, 
access control and encryption would be more enforced than in the case of an organisation that focuses on data availability. 
Traditional approaches to cybersecurity include log monitoring, network scans, and intrusion detection systems [5]. These 
methods rely on pre-defined rules and patterns to identify and alert potential security threats. However, these methods can 
be ineffective due to the volume and complexity of data and the evolving nature of cyber threats.
AI is increasingly used in cybersecurity to address these limitations. AI-based approaches can analyse vast amounts of 
data in real-time and identify patterns and anomalies that may indicate a security threat. This allows for more accurate and 
efficient threat detection and response. Additionally, AI-based systems can be continually trained and updated to adapt to 
new threats, making them more resilient to the evolving nature of cyber-attacks [6].
Indeed, AI-based approaches have the potential to significantly enhance the effectiveness of cybersecurity measures and 
help organisations better protect against cyber threats. However, using AI in cybersecurity raises legitimate concerns, such 
as the potential for bias and the need for explainable AI (XAI) [7].
The two most well-known sub-domains are symbolic AI (representation of knowledge, rules and operations based on 
symbols) and numeric AI (calculations and algorithms using numeric information, focusing on the result and not on the 
representation of knowledge) [8].
These two branches of AI are further defined in section 1, along with an overview of cybersecurity, including a list of 
the most typical cyberattack categories. Section 2 details the selected approach to comb through the literature about AI 
for cybersecurity. A review of the most relevant state-of-the-art approaches is given in section 3 for these four categories: 
network security, endpoint security, application security and user security. The findings are analysed in section 4. Section 5 
concludes this article and provides future directions.
2  Related background
While no single theoretical framework currently encompasses all the concepts, techniques, constructs, and technolo -
gies within the field of AI, the standard AI foundations allow the creation of hybrid systems using a combination 
of different AI methods. This enables the modelling of cognitive phenomena and the development of intelligent 
systems in a flexible, efficient, and robust fashion. [9 ].

2.1  Symbolic AI definition
Symbolic AI, also known as "classical AI" , is a branch of AI that has its roots in the early days of AI research. For some, 
it goes back in 1958 to John McCarthy’s idea of using logic to represent data and information a computer program 
must handle [10]. For others, it is associated with Newell’s and Simon’s hypothesis of a "physical, symbolic system" 
[11]. They believed intelligence can be modelled and represented symbolically, using explicit rules and symbols. The 
methods belonging to the category of symbolic AI have three core assumptions [8 ]:
1. A model representing an intelligent system can be defined explicitly, comprising clear, explicit rules and representa-
tions.
2. In such a model, knowledge is represented symbolically, using a formal language or notation that a computer can 
manipulate and process.
3. Mental or cognitive operations are formal operations over symbolic expressions and structures of a knowledge model.
Symbolic AI is often associated with expert systems, which use a knowledge base of facts and entailment rules to 
solve problems in a specific domain. It focuses on knowledge representation and rule-based reasoning. The systems 
are easy to understand and explain, but they have some limitations, mainly in handling uncertainty, incomplete data, 
ambiguity, and reasoning over vast amounts of data.
2.2  Numeric AI definition
Numeric AI, also known as "Statistical AI" or "Computational AI" , is a branch of AI that goes against the core assump -
tions behind symbolic AI. The central hypothesis is that human reasoning is too complex to be modelled using sym-
bols. Numeric AI emphasises numeric computation and data-driven approaches to model problem-solving. Numeric 
AI is a set of techniques and methodologies that allows knowledge extraction from data using mathematical methods 
and computation, and it’s mainly focused on the quantitative analysis and representation of knowledge.
The methods belonging to the category of numeric AI have three core features [8 ]:
1. Numeric information is essential in a knowledge representation, meaning that the information used to model an 
intelligent system is typically represented in terms of numbers and measurements, such as probabilities or likelihoods.
2. Knowledge processing is based mainly on numeric computation, meaning that mathematical methods, such as 
optimisation algorithms and machine learning (ML) techniques, are used to process and analyse the data.
3. Knowledge is not represented explicitly, meaning that it is not represented using symbols or rules, but rather, using 
patterns and relationships in the data.
For the sake of clarity, we list the following methods as belonging to numeric AI, even though they may not fully 
adhere to the defined characteristics above [8 ]: (artificial) neural networks, pattern recognition, cluster analysis, 
Bayesian inference, models based on fuzzy sets, models based on rough sets, evolutionary computing (genetic algo -
rithms, evolution strategies, evolutionary programming, and genetic programming), swarm intelligence, artificial 
immune systems.
2.3  Cybersecurity domain
Cybersecurity is the approach and actions associated with security risk management processes followed by organi-
sations and states to protect the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of data and assets used in cyberspace 
[5]. This concise definition of cybersecurity covers the collection of artefacts ranging from tools, policies, security 
concepts, security safeguards, guidelines, and technological infrastructure to processes, assurances, measures, best 
practices, and training.
Confidentiality is the first of the three objectives. In the context of this paper, it refers to protecting sensitive infor -
mation from unauthorised access or disclosure. Enabling confidentiality relies on implementing security controls to 
avoid unauthorised disclosure. Among those, we may cite encryption, access controls, and other security technologies

to comply with regulations, laws, and industry standards. Especially regarding international laws and regulations,

Additionally, experts also recommend ensuring data integrity. Integrity refers to the assurance that data has not 
been altered, tampered with, or corrupted in any unauthorised or undetected way. Following McBride et al. [12], 
generic means for enabling integrity include corruption testing, secure storage, backup capability and logging, for 
example, through security incident and event monitoring (SIEM) applications and network data capture systems. 
Integrity security controls also assure nonrepudiation, which means that the sender of the data cannot later deny 
having sent it. Ensuring the authentication of the sender or the receiver prevents user impersonation. Integrity in 
cybersecurity is about maintaining the trustworthiness of data and ensuring that it is accurate and reliable. Integrity is 
especially critical in some domains. For example, data integrity directly impacts patient care and safety in healthcare, 
as using altered or non-accurate patient data can lead to death. EU GDPR states in article 5 that "1. Personal data 
should be: […] processed in a manner that ensures appropriate security of the personal data, including protection 
against unauthorised or unlawful processing and against accidental loss, destruction or damage, using appropriate 
technical or organisational measures (’ integrity and confidentiality’)" .
While data can be private and accurate, it is not valuable if it cannot be timely accessed. Following ITU-T [1 ], general 
security objectives comprise availability, which refers to the ability of a system or network to be accessible and usable 
by authorised users when they need it. This includes ensuring that the system is running without interruption, that 
data and resources are accessible to users, and that any necessary services or functions are functioning correctly. 
It also implies that the system can sustain a certain level of workload and can manage and recover from potential 
failures, malicious attacks, natural disasters, or other incidents that might disrupt its regular operation. It is crucial 
to ensure that critical business processes can continue while the system can provide reliable services to its users. 
Additionally, international laws and regulations such as GDPR and HIPAA further state that only the necessary infor -
mation should be available. For example, article 5 in EU GDPR states that "1. Personal data should be: […] adequate, 
relevant and limited to what is necessary concerning the purposes for which they are processed (’data minimisation’)" .
Often called the 4th cybersecurity principle, accountability refers to the possibility of determining who is respon -
sible for information access and use, be it proper or improper. Accountability represents the intersection of the CIA 
principles: when accessing or entering data, an individual should ensure its integrity and confidentiality and leverage 
the system’s availability. According to existing compliance standards, accountability is considered a fundamental 
principle. For example, EU GDPR article 5 states, "2. The controller shall be responsible for, and be able to demon-
strate compliance with, paragraph 1 (’accountability’)" . Additionally, recital 85 in EU GDPR states that "as soon as 
the controller becomes aware that a personal data breach has occurred, the controller should notify the personal 
data breach to the supervisory authority without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 h after having 
become aware of it, unless the controller can demonstrate, following the accountability principle, that the personal 
data breach is unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. " An organisation must thus be 
able to demonstrate responsibility for the data and information it collects, stores and uses. Accountability includes 
tracking actions, e.g. logs and audits, and identifying responsible parties retrospectively after a cybersecurity inci-
dent or a data breach. Nonrepudiation is an essential aspect of accountability, which helps organisations provide 
evidence of their accountability. With specific regard to accountability, explainability is crucial when using AI for 
cybersecurity (as discussed in Sect. 2A).

3  AI for cybersecurity
Cybersecurity is a rapidly evolving field with an ever-increasing number of threats. The volume and complexity of these 
threats make it difficult for human operators to keep up with the sheer number of security incidents that need to be 
investigated. Organisations try to address this problem by using algorithms to improve the efficiency of the threat triage 
process. This involves using automated systems to perform an initial screening of security incidents so that only the most 
severe threats are passed on to human analysts for further investigation.
Using algorithms can significantly improve the speed and accuracy of threat triage, allowing security teams to identify 
and respond to potential threats quickly. However, algorithms alone may not be sufficient to keep pace with the rapidly 
evolving threat landscape.
AI is increasingly being used to augment the capabilities of cybersecurity professionals. AI-based systems can ana-
lyse large amounts of data and identify patterns humans might miss. This enables organisations to detect and respond 
to threats more quickly and effectively. There are three tasks where AI proves highly effective, especially compared to 
non-AI algorithms [6].
First, classification involves assigning a label or category to a given input based on pre-defined classes or categories. In 
identifying different types of attacks, such as malware or malicious information like spam, classification involves analysing 
the input features (e.g., the code or content of a file or message) and comparing them to the characteristics of known 
attack types. The input is then assigned the label of the class or category that it most closely matches.
Then clustering consists of grouping similar objects or data points based on their characteristics or features. When 
grouping similar assets, clustering algorithms analyse each asset’s features and assign them to groups or clusters such 
that the assets within a cluster are more like each other than those in different clusters. Clustering is particularly useful

Furthermore, effective management of AI-based cybersecurity tools and systems requires technical skills and a deep 
understanding of these tools’ underlying logic and workings. Cybersecurity professionals need to understand the algo-
rithms, models, and data used by AI systems and be able to interpret the results generated by these systems.
Additionally, being able to understand the logic of the AI systems involved in cybersecurity is critical to being able to

fine-tune the systems or suggest new features to make the system more effective. Furthermore, knowing the internal AI 
system helps understand decision points, uncertainty, and potential false positives/negatives.
Moreover, cybersecurity professionals must stay current with the latest developments in AI research, both in terms of 
new techniques and algorithms, as well as advances in related fields such as ML and natural language processing. This 
helps them understand the capabilities and limitations of the tools they are using and identify new opportunities for 
how these tools can improve cybersecurity.
3.1  Explainability
Even though explainability is not a new concept in AI, there is still a lack of consensus over defining precisely what 
explainability entails. Therefore, the approach has been to keep high-level definitions of features commonly found in the 
literature despite some differences in terminology or coverage. Most authors use "explainability" and "interpretability" 
as synonyms. In the next paragraphs, we provide some of the most precise definitions we found for these two concepts.
DARPA defines explainable AI as an AI system capable of explaining its reasoning to a human user, characterising 
its strengths and weaknesses while providing insights into it will behave in the future [13]. Following Ciatto et al. [14], 
explainability is the production of a more interpretable object from a less interpretable one. Gunning et al. [13] consider 
an explanation in a context depending on the task, capabilities, and expectations of the AI system’s user. They view 
explanations as complete or partial. Their definitions of interpretability and explainability are domain dependent. Miller 
[15] defines "explanation" as the answer to a question such as what, how, or why. An answer to a "what" question is a 
factual account of events. An answer to a "how" question indicates the causes of an event. An answer to a "why" ques-
tion is counterfactual because it requires explaining why other events did not occur. Authors consider this to be the 
most complex type of answer to produce. In [15] truly explainable AI models can provide contextual explanations. For 
example, while an event may have multiple causes, the recipient of the explanation may only be interested in a small 
subset of causes (relevant to a specific context); the AI system must be able to provide such an explanation, bounded 
to the contextual causes selected. Gilpin et al. [16], suggest a more restricted definition for an explanation, limiting it to 
"why" questions. They add that an explanation can be interpretable and exhaustive (completeness). The latter refers to 
the precise description of an AI system’s operations. Authors also define "interpretability" as a description of the internal 
functioning of an AI system, in a way that is understandable for humans. The descriptions produced by such a system 
are simple enough to be understood by a person and use a vocabulary that makes sense to this person. A difficult task 
is to have an explanation that is both interpretable and exhaustive.
Interpretability is closely related to the concepts of comprehensibility and understandability. It’s about explaining 
the system’s actions and decisions in a way easily understood by a human audience, depending on their level of under-
standing and expectations of the field [17]. Several definitions of "interpretability" are found in literature. According to 
Brian and Cotton, [18], systems are interpretable if their operations can be understood by a human being, either through 
introspection or through a produced explanation. Montavon et al. [19] define "interpretability" as mapping an abstract 
concept into a domain that humans can understand. For Calegari et al. [20], "interpretability" is a subjective relationship

associating a representation with a specific meaning in the problem domain. Ciatto et al. [ 14] define it as assigning 
subjective meaning to an object, done by a (human or non-human) agent, with little computational or cognitive effort.
In the context of this article, we adhere to the position argued by Russell and Norvig [21], stating that explainability 
is built upon interpretability. Interpretability addresses the issue of understanding why an AI system produced a given 
output, given a specific input. It relates to understanding the causes and the effects of an AI system. An AI system is said 
to be interpretable if one can examine the model and "why it got a particular answer for a given input, and how the 
answer would change if the input changed" . Examples of interpretable AI models are decision trees or linear regression 
models. Explainability goes beyond interpretability and provides a human-readable way of explaining how and why a 
model produces a given output. Following [21], while the inspection of the model provides interpretability, explainability 
is a separate process summarising what the model does. LIME 1 (Local Interpretable Model Agnostic Explanations) is an 
example of such a separate explanation module [22]. Whatever model is used, LIME creates an interpretable approxima-
tion of it in the form of a linear model, which provides explanations of each feature’s importance.
An AI system is considered comprehensible to humans when it can explain and provide the meaning or represent 
the knowledge learned in terms understandable for a human [23]. This means the explanations must be relevant and 
understandable for the specific people for whom the system is designed. For instance, the explanations provided to a 
non-technical audience may differ from those provided to a technical audience in detail and technicality. In the context of 
AI systems, there is a trade-off to be considered between models providing simple explanations and models with higher 
accuracy. Trust and confidence are two related underlying concepts, and explanations are insufficient to reach trust. As 
explained by [21], an explanation is "a story" about decisions made by the system considered. Simple explanations may 
be too appealing and fail to cover all underlying causes leading to the considered output. While a system is said "inter -
pretable" if one can examine its source code and its functioning, it is said "explainable" if it can provide explanations 
(stories about what it’s doing), even if it is an "uninterpretable black box" [21]. Explainable AI is the field of AI that focuses 
on creating systems that are transparent and interpretable [23].
When considering decision-making and society, one essential aspect of AI is allowing transparency and justifiability. 
These non-fully independent features allow for the definition of complementary requirements that an AI system needs 
to meet, at least partially, to be qualified as being explainable to some degree. We discuss them below.
• Transparency
An AI system is said to be transparent if it can be easily understood and described by humans without the need for 
extensive technical knowledge or detailed explanations of the internal processes and structure of the system. A transpar-
ent AI system is one that a human can easily understand without additional information or explanation [23]. One aspect 
of transparency is allowing users to know whether they are interacting with an AI system or a human [24].
• Justifiability
An AI system is justifiable if it can provide a rationale for its decisions. This means that the system should be able to 
expose the reasoning behind its decisions and the knowledge it used to reach them in a way that is comprehensible 
to humans by providing enough information for a human to understand why a decision was made [25], regardless of 
whether the decision itself is easy to understand. It requires the system to demonstrate the justification, or evidence, of its 
decision; it could be in logical reasoning, data, or any other evidence supporting it. No specific quality metric is imposed 
on the justification; it only needs to be enabled for the justifiability feature to be considered [17].
Following previous considerations, we adhere to the definition provided by [21] for a "good explanation": "it should 
be understandable and convincing to the user, it should accurately reflect the reasoning of the system, it should be 
complete, and it should be specific in that different users with different conditions or different outcomes should get 
different explanations. "

3.2  Performance
3.2.1  Suitability
When evaluating the performance of AI systems, quality performance measures are used to assess the system’s capabili-
ties and performance, and they can be evaluated or approximated even in the absence of real-world implementation 
and/or data. This allows for assessing a system’s potential performance before deploying it in the real world.
Moreover, no established framework exists for quantitative measures of heterogeneous AI approaches, such as sym-
bolic or numeric AI. This makes it challenging to compare and evaluate the performance of different AI systems, as no 
consistent set of metrics can be applied across different implementations and their associated datasets.
ISO/IEC TS 25011 provides the below table of quality characteristics for IT services [29] (Table  2):
In the context of this article and the publications found, one characteristic from Table 1 identified as crucial and meas-
urable is suitability. Suitability means the system meets stated and implied needs when used in the specified context 
[29]. Suitability ensures completeness (the system supports all the defined goals, objectives, and data), correctness (the 
system produces correct results with accurate data through a proper process), appropriateness (the results produced by 
the system are appropriate relative to the user’s needs), and consistency (the quality of the system is consistent across 
repeated use).
3.3  Accuracy and precision
The lack of a consistent set of quantitative metrics makes it difficult to compare the performance of different AI systems 
and to evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of various approaches. Therefore, to determine the overall performance 
of heterogeneous AI systems and identify the areas where they are performing well or poorly, the quantitative measures 
the most encountered in the literature are accuracy and precision.
ISO 5725-1 [30] provides its definition of "accuracy" being the composite of "trueness" and "precision" . These two 
terms are defined as follows:
• "Trueness: The closeness of agreement between the average value obtained from an extensive series of test results 
and an accepted reference value.
• Precision: The closeness of agreement between independent test results obtained under stipulated conditions. "
The above definition of "trueness" corresponds to the everyday use of "accuracy" in AI literature. Thus, in the 
context of this paper, we keep "accuracy" as the term of choice.

• A measurement method’s accuracy ("trueness") is essential when the measured property has a known or accepted 
true value. Even if this actual value cannot be determined precisely, a reference value may be established using 
reference materials or by comparison to another measurement method. "Trueness" can be evaluated by compar -

which can occur due to factors such as incomplete extraction of an element or interference from other elements 
during analysis.
• Precision refers to the variability between repeated measurements. Two types of precision, known as repeatability 
and reproducibility, are often used to describe the variability of a measurement method. Repeatability conditions 
assume that contextual factors (the operator, the equipment, the time elapsed between measurements, or envi-
ronment characteristics like temperature and humidity) do not affect the variability. In contrast, reproducibility 
conditions consider that these factors may contribute to the variability of the results. Repeatability represents 
the minimum variability, and reproducibility represents the maximum variability. Precision is typically measured 
in terms of standard deviations.
As one may suspect, in a cybersecurity context, an AI system’s accuracy and precision deeply impact its defensive 
capabilities, namely effective threat detection, risk management or incident response. Regarding threat detection, accu-
racy and precision bring an improved complex pattern and anomaly recognition [31], through higher detection rates 
(ranging from 80 to 92% and surpassing more than 50% the rates achieved by traditional signature-based malware 
systems [32] and a reduced number of false positives, thus reducing alert fatigue for security teams which can focus on 
real threats instead [33].

3.4  Robustness
AI robustness refers to the ability of an AI system to maintain its intended behaviour in the face of unexpected changes 
or perturbations in the environment in which it operates. These changes may be natural, such as drifts in the input data, 
or malicious, such as an adversary trying to gain unauthorised access to the system.
For an AI system to be robust, it must continue functioning safely and effectively even when faced with these unex -
pected changes. It means that the system should be able to adapt to new circumstances and maintain its intended 
behaviour without failure or compromise.
When dealing with system quality, robustness is closely related to "context coverage" [34], which measures how well 
an AI system can handle changes in the operating context. Context coverage is distinct from context completeness 
(defined simply as completeness in section II.B), which refers to the completeness of the information provided to the AI 
system, but it focuses on the ability of the system to handle unforeseen changes in input.
Three identified types of changes may challenge the robustness of an AI system [34] and are discussed below.
• Distributional change refers to changes in the input distribution over time, which can affect the behaviour of an AI 
system. It is a common challenge facing AI systems and related to the concept of generalisation in AI. Maintaining 
the system behaviour as the input distribution shifts over time is crucial to ensure the robustness of an AI system.
• Adversarial inputs are another type of challenge that an AI system may face. Unlike natural changes, these inputs have 
been manufactured by a malicious adversary to lead the AI system towards failure. An adversarial input is designed 
to manipulate the system’s behaviour and cause it to produce incorrect results.
• Maintaining explainability relates to changes in the input distribution, whether malicious or natural, which can lead to 
unexpected results that may not be easy to understand. Explainability can be used to detect whether an adversarial 
attack is the cause of that change. It allows an understanding of the decisions and actions of an AI system and helps 
detect any unexpected or anomalous behaviour that adversarial inputs may cause.
Concerning cybersecurity, a robust AI system presents several benefits but also comes with several challenges that 
need to be addressed, such as the expansion of attack surfaces, the potential for hiding malicious activities or making 
harmful decisions if compromised or the potential for developing malware capable of adapting to prevent detection by 
existing security systems [35]. In parallel, a robust AI system can improve an organisation’s defensive capabilities [36] by 
providing a shield against adversarial attacks and preventing the AI system from being compromised or misled, more 
specifically, from a regulatory point of view, under the AIA [26], while the concepts "cybersecurity" and "robustness" 
appear related (i.e. Article 15 "Accuracy, Robustness and Cybersecurity" in the AIA) as they both address an AI system’s 
resilience to performance disruptions or attacks [37]. But "robustness" is defined as an AI system’s ability to be "resilient 
in relation to harmful or otherwise undesirable behaviour that may result from limitations within the systems or the 
environment in which the systems operate (e.g. errors, faults, inconsistencies, unexpected situations)" . Addressing the 
above-mentioned challenges when designing AI systems is also enforced in the EU AIA, and specific solutions such as 
fail-safe plans are listed.
4  State of the art of the most relevant approaches
As identified by Parisi [6], four large cybersecurity domains are well-suited for AI-based systems. We present them below.
Network security protects a computer network and its associated infrastructure from unauthorised access, use, 
disclosure, disruption, modification, or destruction. It can include protection at all layers of the network stack, 
from the physical infrastructure and devices to the network itself to the applications and services that run on top 
of it. One of the primary concerns of network security is maintaining the integrity of the network perimeter, which 
refers to the logical and physical boundaries that separate a secure internal network from an untrusted external 
network, such as the Internet. This includes protecting against unauthorised access, intrusion, and other forms of 
cyberattacks that attempt to penetrate the network perimeter. Another critical aspect of network security is main-
taining the availability of the network, which means ensuring that authorised users have access to the resources 
they need when needed. It comprises protection against DoS attacks, which attempt to flood a network with traf-
fic to overwhelm it and prevent legitimate users from accessing it. It is also crucial to ensure the confidentiality

of transiting information and protect sensitive information from unauthorised disclosure as it flows across the 
network. It encompasses encrypting data flows across the network and implementing access controls to ensure 
only authorised users can access the information.
Endpoint security includes a set of security measures designed to protect devices used by end-users, such as 
computers, laptops, mobile devices, IoT devices, and portable storage devices, from various types of cyber threats. 
It is also known as "client security" as it protects all client devices that could connect to the network. Endpoint 
security includes a variety of technologies and techniques used to protect devices themselves, as well as data 
stored and exchanged among devices. It provides antivirus software, firewalls, intrusion detection and prevention 
systems, and malware scanners, which detect and block malicious software and other cyber threats. Additionally, 
endpoint security also includes measures that ensure the reliability and usability of the devices, such as software 
updates and patches that fix security vulnerabilities and improve the overall performance and stability of the device. 
Endpoint security also includes Device management and control, which allow IT administrators to centrally man-
age and monitor the security of all the endpoint devices. It comprises capabilities such as remote lock and wipe, 
inventory management, and deploying security policies and software across the organisation. Another essential 
aspect of endpoint security is to protect the devices from malicious alterations, which includes protection against 
firmware and BIOS-level malicious alteration and hardware-based attacks. Such attacks can consist of using digital 
signatures to ensure that firmware and software have not been tampered with and hardware-based security solu-
tions like Trusted Platform Module (TPM) that provide secure storage for encryption keys.
Application security targets the protection of all applications from various types of cyber threats, independent 
of the device or environment on which they are running. It encompasses the security of software, web, mobile, 
and cloud-based applications. The primary goal of application security is to ensure that applications operate as 
intended and are not compromised by unauthorised access, misuse, or alteration. Application security comprises 
various technologies and practices used to identify and mitigate application security vulnerabilities, such as code 
reviews, penetration testing, and runtime application self-protection. These measures identify and fix security vul-
nerabilities and weaknesses in the application code before the application is deployed and in runtime. Application 
security also includes measures that ensure data protection on the fly and at rest. It comes with encryption and 
secure communication protocols, such as HTTPS, to protect data as it is transmitted over a network and measures, 
such as secure data storage and access controls, to protect data when it is stored on the device or in the cloud. 
Additionally, application security includes measures to ensure that the application performs the intended tasks, 
including monitoring and analysing the application logs, using an Intrusion Detection System (IDS) to detect abnor -
mal behaviour, and using behaviour-based detection methods to identify unknown threats.
User security, or end-user security, is a set of security measures designed to protect users from various types of 
cyber threats and ensure that they use systems and applications safely and securely. This category focuses on the 
behaviour of users and their correct use of systems. It encompasses measures such as user education and aware -
ness training, which can help educate users on identifying and avoiding common security threats, such as phishing 
attacks and malware, and provide them with guidance on safe online behaviour and best practices for working 
securely. User security also includes measures designed to shield users from malicious actors. Among those we may 
cite are access controls, which ensure that only authorised users have access to sensitive information and systems, 
and multi-factor authentication, which allows users to verify their identity before allowing them to access systems 
and applications. Additionally, user security also focuses on protecting users from suffering harm from any failure; 
measures in this category are backup, disaster recovery and incident response plans. These measures help restore 
the system and data to normal in case of a failure or a malicious attack.

performed better than SVM, DNN, RF, and GRU methods.

5  Analysis—overwhelming approaches of numeric AI for cybersecurity
In cybersecurity, the shift from symbolic AI to numeric AI has been driven by the need for faster, more flexible, and more 
scalable solutions. Numeric AI allows for real-time analysis and better adaptation to new threats, making it well-suited 
for cybersecurity’s fast-paced and ever-evolving world. While symbolic AI has a rich history and has been used success-
fully in many applications [25], the growing demand for more powerful and efficient solutions has led to an increased 
focus on numeric AI in recent years.
5.1  Strengths
While numeric AI models can be computationally expensive to train, yielding significant investment in hardware and 
computational resources due to the large amounts of data they process, the benefits they deliver in terms of fast and 
accurate threat classification and response make them a valuable investment for organisations looking to protect them-
selves against cyber threats.
The studies referenced above have shown that numeric AI systems provide results with very high accuracy and preci-
sion in cybersecurity. The lowest accuracy found was 92.18% for phishing detection [59], with an average accuracy of 
97.48%. Meanwhile, the lowest precision was 81.63% for DGA attacks [54], with an average precision of 93.82%. These 
results demonstrate the effectiveness of numeric AI in accurately and precisely detecting cyber threats.
Furthermore, it’s worth noting that in 10 of the 21 studies, the accuracy and precision were at least 98%. It highlights 
the exceptional performance of numeric AI in detecting and preventing cyber threats.
The diversity of techniques and the large set of parameters used in numeric AI models enable them to address vari-
ous use cases in multiple settings. This is particularly important in cybersecurity, where the threat landscape constantly 
evolves.
In the studies, botnets have many categories and subcategories, and numeric AI models can be trained and fine-tuned 
to detect and respond to these threats [49– 52]. Similarly, malware families and variants are numerous, and numeric AI 
models can be used to classify and respond to these threats in real-time (less than a 1 μs [47]) [44– 46, 48].

Fraud schemes are also highly diverse, and numeric AI models can be used to detect and prevent fraudulent activi-

DGA is the backbone for many botnets and other swarm attacks, and numeric AI models can be used to detect and

The design of the systems in the studies is reported to be very high, with only one out of 21 having limited suitability

High-quality design is fundamental in cybersecurity, as numeric AI systems are being used to detect and respond to a 
wide range of threats. If a system is poorly designed, it may be unable to detect and respond to these threats effectively, 
putting the security of organisations and their data at risk.
In summary, the results of these studies demonstrate that numeric AI systems are highly effective in accurately and 
precisely detecting and preventing cyber threats. The broad range of techniques and parameters used allows them to 
address a wide range of challenges. Furthermore, the high quality of the studied systems is an essential factor that helps 
to ensure their effectiveness.
Given their accuracy, precision, diversity, and high quality, numeric AIs are vital for organisations looking to improve 
their cybersecurity defences.
5.2  Weaknesses
The quality of available datasets used to train and test numeric AI systems significantly impacts the output quality [43]. 
This is particularly important in cybersecurity, as accurately classifying and responding to threats is critical.
While generative systems and other mitigation techniques are being used to address the limited quantity of data 
available, studies have shown that this remains a limiting factor in the models’ confidence to address real-world, large-
scale issues [43].
Another challenge with numeric AI systems is their lack of transparency. By nature, these systems are complex and 
operate in a black-box manner, making it difficult to understand how they make decisions: no study has been found even 
to attempt to provide transparency as defined in section II.A.1). In cybersecurity, this can be problematic as organisations 
need to have confidence in the systems used to protect themselves against cyber threats.
Explainability is another weakness of numeric AI systems. In all but 3 studies [39, 44, 45], interpretability is added post 
hoc, as the systems were not designed to be transparent from the outset. In a field such as cybersecurity, where the ability 
to understand and trust the decision-making process is critical, this is a significant weakness that needs to be addressed.
In summary, while numeric AI systems offer many benefits in cybersecurity, the limitations of available datasets and the 
lack of transparency and explainability remain challenges that must be addressed. Further research is needed to improve 
these systems’ quality and reliability and ensure that they can be trusted to address real-world security challenges.
5.3  Shortcomings
Robustness is a critical aspect of numeric AI systems, particularly for cybersecurity, where the ability to respond to chang-
ing conditions and maintain accurate predictions is essential. However, few studies have addressed this issue.
Only eight studies address robustness against distributional changes, and only three focus on robustness against 
adversarial inputs [40, 41, 53]. Indeed, most numeric AI systems have not been tested or optimised to withstand changes 
in their operating environment, which can impact their performance and accuracy.
Regarding interpretations, some studies have added them post hoc, while most studies (10 out of 18) with some 
explainability level focus on maintaining it across changes, such as drift in the system’s malicious or natural inputs.
Many authors acknowledge that real-world, large-scale applications of numeric AI systems in cybersecurity are rife 
with limitations. These include a lack of real-world testbeds [57], doubts about scalability [49], limited datasets in terms 
of quality, freshness, or quantity [43], and the need sometimes to retrain to adapt to changing conditions [50].

6  The road towards hybrid AI for cybersecurity—challenges and benefits
6.1  Hybrid AI definition
The term "hybrid AI" is often used in the literature to describe a mix of methods within the same group of AI. The concept 
of "neuro-symbolic" AI has also been intensively mentioned in recent years as an approach addressing the issue of hav-
ing machines that do not just learn common sense but also understand it [61]. However, in the context of this paper, we 
use "hybrid AI" explicitly to refer to both symbolic and numeric AI methods to take advantage of the strengths of each 
approach and overcome their limitations.
Hybrid AI systems use symbolic and numeric AI methods to create a more robust and versatile AI system. Symbolic 
AI methods use logic and rules to formally represent knowledge and reason about it, making it possible to understand 
and explain the system’s decisions. On the other hand, numeric AI methods use statistical and ML techniques to analyse 
large amounts of data and make predictions or decisions based on that analysis.
Additionally, the hybrid nature is considered even when one group of methods is used more heavily than the other. 
Even in cases where a system is primarily based on symbolic or numeric AI methods, integrating complementary methods 
from the other group is still considered hybrid AI. Regardless of their relative contributions to the system’s performance, 
explainability, or robustness this approach is taken.
6.2  Expected benefits
One expected benefit of using hybrid AI in cybersecurity is that it should allow for the integration of multiple AI tech-
niques, each with its strengths and weaknesses. For example, rule-based systems are great at identifying well-known 
threats using explainable mechanisms, but they are not very effective at massively processing potentially malicious data, 
nor can they be quickly adapted to new or unknown threats. While machine and deep learning algorithms can quickly 
process vast amounts of data and treat new or unknown threats quickly with unlabelled or partial data by analysing pat-
terns, they are not as efficient at providing explainable feedback for their action or may not generalise as well as expected.
To the best of our knowledge, no current AI system in the cybersecurity domain provides complete, human-level 
natural language explanations of its decisions. Existing solutions typically rely on technical rule-based outputs or numeric 
feature importances, which may be comprehensible only to specialized analysts spending a fair amount of time on them. 
Introducing an automatically generated, natural language explanation of why a system has flagged a threat would enable 
any practitioners to more quickly trust and act upon the system’s guidance. This accelerated comprehension could lead 
to swifter response times, faster prevention measures, and a more robust level of security overall.
In our first example, the approach chosen by Yan, et al. [45] is first to train a supervised DNN, therefore processing 
massive amounts of network traffic data, with the critical ability to retrain if and when needed on new data, updating 
the model to detect new threats or improve on detecting existing ones. The major shortcoming of such models is that 
their black-box nature hinders the analysis of malware discriminations made by the DNN. For this reason, Yan, et al. 
[45] extracted rules from the trained DNN layers to build a decision tree compact enough to be fully interpretable and 
embedded on an FPGA14 to detect malware behaviour on mobile devices.
The second example is a work by Himmelhuber, et al. [42] who proposed using a GNN that embeds a knowledge graph 
and then filters the security alerts raised by their anomaly detection system, reducing false alerts by 66%, thanks to the 
interpretation generated both from the GNN and the knowledge graph.

6.3  Potential
Combining AI techniques in a hybrid AI system improves performance and adaptability to changing threat landscapes. 
Hybrid AI can benefit from the strengths of rule-based and ML algorithms to achieve higher accuracy and efficiency 
in detecting and preventing cyber threats. This makes it ideal for various cybersecurity applications, such as intrusion 
detection and prevention, malware detection, and threat hunting.
Additionally, the ability of hybrid AI systems to continue learning and adapting to new and evolving threats is a 
significant advantage over traditional rule-based systems, which rely on pre-defined rules that may become obsolete 
over time. With hybrid AI, the system can continuously update its understanding of the threat landscape and accord-
ingly adjust its behaviour, making it a valuable tool in the fight against cybercrime.
Indeed, Yan, et al. [45] obtained a decision tree built upon a trained DNN that is transparent, justifiable, and 
interpretable. Nonetheless, their system performed exceptionally well compared to other state-of-the-art malware 
detection approaches regarding accuracy, precision, recall, and F-Measure. Moreover, the lightweight nature of their 
model makes it incredibly fast and more scalable.
Also, Himmelhuber, et al. [42] provide interpretations without transparency by using explainable AI frameworks 
(GNNExplainer and DL-Learner) and use knowledge graphs to train their GNN and filter out a large part of the false 
alerts.
7  Conclusions
As the above review proves, combining numeric and symbolic AI approaches can only enhance the performances 
achieved so far when using only numeric AI systems. Still, the journey towards attaining such a combination comes 
with several challenges. These challenges are to be addressed soon, especially given the evolution of the regulatory 
context with initiatives such as the EU AIA and its specific articles pointing to High-Risk AI Systems, with a later entry 
into force by mid-2026 [26].
Coupled with the regulatory context, the increasing number of connected devices (expected to generate 79 zet -
tabytes of data by 2025 [32], a first challenge for hybrid AI relates to data integration, knowledge representation and 
rule-based reasoning. These relate to the hybrid AI system’s symbolic component, which is less addressed in existing 
research (as demonstrated above). Recent approaches such as Piplai, et al. [62] have discussed the issue of combining 
(e.g., network logs, threat intelligence feeds) with unstructured data (e.g., incident reports or security policies) into 
knowledge bases that can serve neuro-symbolic AI. The latter has also been identified as critical by Ghosh [63] for 
providing comprehensive and robust user analytics in cybersecurity. Creating a cohesive framework for represent -
ing numeric patterns and symbolic rules would allow seamless interaction between the two AI paradigms and, thus, 
explainable AI for security decision-making. The symbolic part enables the modelling of human-understandable rules. 
It provides logical (therefore clear) explanations for the AI system’s decisions, hence a high level of explainability and 
transparency, which we identified as crucial for ensuring trust and accountability in cybersecurity.
Another challenge for hybrid AI addresses the management of computation complexity. As discussed in earlier 
sections, numeric AI-based approaches have demonstrated performances in terms of scalability and efficiency in 
handling large-scale cybersecurity data. Finding the correct balance between the resource-intensive nature of deep 
learning and the complexities of symbolic AI’s rule-based reasoning would allow almost real-time threat detection 
and explainable decision-support. Still, developing the underlying algorithms and architectures that enable the 
hybrid processing of vast data remains an active challenge [64].
Finally, ensuring such hybrid AI systems’ adaptability and continuous learning is the ultimate challenge. Tackling 
it would allow the reuse of the explainable and efficient hybrid AI solutions developed for handling evolving threats, 
as common in the rapidly changing cybersecurity landscape. With such real-time updates of the numeric and sym-
bolic components, hybrid AI systems could detect and reason about new threats that fell outside their previous 
knowledge base.
In conclusion, hybrid AI allows for integrating different representation and reasoning methods, such as rule-based 
reasoning, decision trees, and neural networks, to create a more versatile and adaptive AI system. This can lead to 
significant improvements in performance, robustness, and scalability, as well as an increase in explainability and

transparency of the decisions made by the AI system. These advantages make hybrid AI attractive for organisations 
looking to enhance their cybersecurity defences. By addressing the different challenges discussed in this article, 
organisations can harness the full potential of hybrid AI to enhance their cybersecurity posture, combining numeric 
AI’s pattern recognition capabilities with symbolic AI’s reasoning and explainability. Furthermore, practitioners need 
to adopt urgent measures for industry adoption, such as cross-referencing global frameworks (e.g., NIST, ENISA) 
alongside recent approaches by Radanliev et al. [65, 66], embedding ethical-by-design checks from the outset, and 
implementing shared auditing tools for real-time adversarial and explanation testing. Embracing these steps across 
multiple standards and contexts will help foster greater trust, resilience, and ethical usage of AI-driven cybersecurity 
solutions worldwide.
