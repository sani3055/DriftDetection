Abstract: Large-scale database environments supporting digital government, 
financial services, healthcare, smart -city platforms, cloud applications, and 
national data services generate continuous streams of performance metrics, 
logs, query statistics, configuration events, and security signals. Static 
thresholds and manual dashboard inspection are increasingly inadequate 
because normal behaviour changes with workload cycles, application releases, 
data growth, infrastructure scaling, and distributed -system dependencies. This 
paper develops an artificial -intelligence-enabled framework for intelligent 
anomaly detection in large -scale database environments, with particular 
attention to Saudi Arabia's rapidly expanding digital infrastructure under Vision 
2030. A structured  narrative review synthesizes peer -reviewed work on KPI -
based database anomaly detection, multivariate time -series modelling, log 
analytics, deep learning, explainable anomaly detection, AIOps, and database 
root-cause analysis, together with Saudi policy s ources on data and AI, digital 
government, cybersecurity, and cloud adoption. The proposed framework 
integrates six layers: governed observability, context -aware data engineering, 
multi-model anomaly detection, multimodal correlation and root -cause 
analysis, risk -based alert orchestration, and human -in-the-loop learning. It 
distinguishes point, contextual, collective, compound, workload, resource, 
query, availability, configuration, and security -related anomalies, and 
recommends combining robust statistical baselines with unsupervised machine 
learning, autoencoders, recurrent or transformer models, and log -sequence 
analysis rather than relying on a single algorithm. Evaluation is defined using 
both machine-learning metrics and operational outcomes, including event-level 
precision and recall, false-alert burden, detection latency, time-to-diagnosis, and 
stability under concept drift. The framework is aligned with Saudi requirements 
for responsible AI, resilient digital infrastructure, data protection, and 
cybersecurity. The paper argues that intelligent anomaly detection should be 
deployed as an auditable decision-support capability before progressing toward 
automated remediation, enabling Saudi organizations to improve reliability 
while preserving governance and expert oversight.

1. INTRODUCTION 
Databases are a foundational layer of digital 
infrastructure. Government portals, national identity 
services, payment systems, health platforms, 
enterprise resource planning, telecommunications, 
smart-city applications, analytics platforms, and 
artificial-intelligence services all depend on 
databases that must remain responsive, correct, 
secure, and available. The operating challenge is no 
longer limited to keeping a small number of servers 
online. Large organizations increasingly operate 
heterogeneous esta tes containing relational 
databases, clustered systems, replicated platforms, 
cloud-managed services, data warehouses, data 
lakes, and application -specific data stores. These 
estates produce high -volume telemetry and interact 
with application, storage, net work, identity, and 
security layers. A failure that appears as elevated 
query latency may therefore originate from a 
changed execution plan, lock contention, a storage 
bottleneck, a network path, a deployment, a 
misconfigured parameter, abnormal authentica tion 
activity, or an upstream service. 
 
Traditional monitoring is dominated by fixed 
thresholds, rules, dashboards, and expert 
interpretation. Such mechanisms remain valuable, 
but they scale poorly when normal behaviour is 
seasonal, workload -sensitive, multivariate, and 
continuously changing. A CPU utilization of 80% may 
be normal during a known batch window yet 
abnormal during a quiet period. A modest increase in 
I/O latency may be harmless on one service but 
critical when it coincides with rising lock waits, 
replication delay, and transaction b acklog on a high -
priority platform. Static rules also generate alert 
storms because multiple dependent metrics often 
cross thresholds during a single underlying incident. 
The result is a mismatch between the volume of 
observable signals and the human capac ity to 
interpret them in real time. 
 
Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine 
learning (ML) provide a means to shift monitoring 
from rigid thresholding toward learned operational 
context. Research on deep anomaly detection shows 
that neural models can learn nonlinear 
representations of normal  and abnormal behaviour 
in complex data, while unsupervised approaches are 
particularly relevant when labelled failure data are 
scarce (Pang et al., 2021). Multivariate methods such 
as USAD, OmniAnomaly, and TranAD demonstrate 
approaches for modelling temporal dependencies 
and correlated signals in high-dimensional telemetry 
(Audibert et al., 2020; Su et al., 2019; Tuli et al., 2022). 
Database-specific research is also maturing. Gao et 
al., (2025) synthesize KPI -based performance 
anomaly detection in databases and emphasize the 
importance of database context, deployment 
conditions, and root-cause diagnosis. DBPA provides 
a benchmark for reproducible transactional database 
performance anomalies, while PinSQL demonstrates 
how anomaly detection can be combined with query-
level diagnosis in a cloud database environment 
(Huang et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2022). 
 
For Saudi Arabia, this topic is strategically 
relevant. The National Strategy for Data and AI aims 
to position the Kingdom among leading data -driven 
economies, while SDAIA's strategic objectives include 
advanced digital infrastructure, resilient 
cybersecurity, government cloud services, national 
platforms, and AI research and adoption (SDAIA, n.d.-
a; SDAIA, n.d.-b). Vision 2030 reporting also identifies 
digital infrastructure, cloud, AI, cybersecurity, and 
innovation as enablers of the digital and knowledge  
economy (Saudi Vision 2030, 2026). As digital public 
services and private -sector platforms scale, the 
reliability of the underlying database layer becomes a 
practical requirement for service quality, business 
continuity, trust, and national digital 
competitiveness. 
 
This paper develops a conceptual framework 
for intelligent anomaly detection in large -scale 
database environments that can be adapted to Saudi 
digital infrastructure. It addresses four questions: (1) 
which anomaly types and data sources are most 
relevant to large database estates; (2) which AI and 
ML techniques are suitable under real operational 
constraints; (3) how should detection be connected 
to diagnosis, prioritization, and human decision -
making; and (4) how can the framework align with 
Saudi digital, AI, data, and cybersecurity governance. 
The contribution is not a new benchmark result or 
production deployment. Instead, it is a structured 
synthesis that converts recent anomaly -detection 
and AI -for-database research into a deployment -
oriented architect ure with evaluation criteria, 
governance controls, and an implementation 
roadmap. 
 
2. Research Background and Literature Context 
2.1 Anomaly Detection in Database Operations 
An anomaly is an observation or pattern that 
deviates sufficiently from expected behaviour to 
warrant investigation. In database operations, that 
definition must be contextualized because not every 
deviation is harmful and not every incident produces 
a simple outlier. Database telemetry contains natural 
variability caused by users, batch processing, 
maintenance, backups, statistics collection, business 
calendars, software changes, elastic scaling, and data 
growth. The operational task is therefore to 
distinguish legitimate variation from behaviour 
associated with degradation, failure, abuse, or an 
emerging risk.

A useful taxonomy begins with point, 
contextual, and collective anomalies. A point anomaly 
is an isolated observation that is unusual relative to 
an expected distribution, such as a sudden spike in 
physical reads. A contextual anomaly is abnormal 
only with in a particular time, workload, or service 
context; for example, a response time that is 
acceptable during a known peak may be suspicious 
during a low -load window. A collective anomaly 
emerges from a sequence or group of observations 
that may appear indivi dually normal but form an 
abnormal pattern together. Database incidents often 
fall into the latter two categories because degradation 
develops through correlated trends rather than a 
single extreme measurement (Gao et al ., 2025; 
Schmidl et al., 2022).

2.2 KPI -Based and Multivariate Time -Series 
Detection 
Database monitoring systems produce 
hundreds or thousands of key performance 
indicators (KPIs). Examples include transactions per 
second, queries per second, average and percentile 
response time, CPU, memory, buffer -cache ratios, 
physical and logical reads , I/O latency, redo or 
transaction-log generation, active sessions, waits, 
lock duration, cache eviction, replication delay, 
storage utilization, and network throughput. The core 
technical challenge is to learn what combination and 
temporal pattern of these metrics represents normal 
operation for a particular service. 
 
Unsupervised machine learning is attractive 
because labelled incidents are scarce and biased 
toward previously known failure modes. Isolation -
based methods, clustering, one -class classifiers, and 
density approaches can model normal regions and 
flag outlier s. Deep representation learning extends 
this idea. Autoencoders learn to reconstruct normal 
multivariate behaviour and treat high reconstruction 
error as anomalous. USAD combines autoencoder -
based reconstruction with adversarial training for 
multivariate t ime-series anomaly detection 
(Audibert et al ., 2020). OmniAnomaly uses a 
stochastic recurrent neural -network architecture to 
model temporal dependencies and reconstruction 
probabilities in multivariate telemetry (Su et al.,  
2019). TranAD applies transformer -based attention 
and self -conditioning to capture wider temporal 
context and diagnose anomalous dimensions (Tuli et 
al., 2022). These methods are relevant to database 
estates because many incidents are preceded by 
gradual cross -metric changes rather than isolated 
threshold breaches. 
 
2.3 Log and Event-Sequence Anomaly Detection 
Metrics describe how a system behaves 
numerically, but logs describe events, errors, state 
transitions, and operational context. Database alert 
logs, audit logs, listener or connectivity logs, cluster 
logs, operating -system logs, backup logs, and 
application logs can reveal symptoms that do not 
appear clearly in KPIs. Log data are difficult because 
raw messages are semi-structured, high-volume, and 
change across software versions. 
 
DeepLog introduced the idea of modelling 
log sequences with long short -term memory 
networks so that deviations from learned normal 
event sequences can be detected (Du et al ., 2017). 
Drain provides an efficient online parser that 
converts raw log messages into templates, enabling 
scalable downstream analytics (He et al ., 2017). 
LogAnomaly adds semantic representation of log 
templates and detects both sequential and

applies a transformer language -model approach to 
learn patterns in log sequences and identify 
anomalous events (Guo et al ., 2021). These 
approaches suggest that database anomaly detection 
should not be limited to numerical KPIs. A strong 
architecture should fuse time -series signals with 
parsed logs, change records, query fingerprints, and 
topology information. 
 
2.4 Database-Specific Diagnosis and AI for DBMS 
The database community has increasingly 
explored the broader interaction between AI and 
database systems. Zhou et al. (2022) describe AI for 
databases as a research direction encompassing 
learned optimization, tuning, indexing, monitoring, 
and other data -management functions. Dritsas and 
Trigka (2026) similarly review ML integration into 
modern database systems and identify anomaly 
detection, self -tuning, query planning, and 
operational deployment as major areas of 
development. 
 
Detection alone is insufficient for operations. 
A system that announces "anomaly detected" without 
identifying the likely cause can increase workload 
rather than reduce it. PinSQL is important because it 
links anomaly detection to the identification of roo t-
cause SQL statements in cloud databases and then 
connects diagnosis to repair actions (Liu et al., 2022). 
DBPA is also significant because it formalizes 
reproducible performance -anomaly scenarios and 
provides data for studying diagnosis in transactional 
database systems (Huang et al ., 2023). Gao et al ., 
(2025) emphasize that root-cause analysis remains a 
central research challenge because generic time -
series methods often identify abnormal signals 
without explaining why the anomaly occurred. 
 
Explainability is therefore operationally 
important. Li et al ., (2024) review explainable 
anomaly detection and note the gap between high -
performing models and human -understandable 
reasons. In database management, Ouared et al ., 
(2023) argue for maintaining the DBA in the loop 
when ML is used for tuning, because acceptance of 
automated decisions depends on transparent 
reasoning. For anomaly detection, an explanation 
should identify the most influential signals, the time 
at whic h they diverged, related chang es or SQL, 
affected dependencies, model confidence, and 
evidence that supports the recommendation. 
 
2.5 AIOps, Drift, and Production Constraints 
AIOps research places anomaly detection 
inside a larger failure -management lifecycle that 
includes detection, prediction, diagnosis, and 
remediation. Notaro et al.,  (2021) show that AIOps 
methods address the scale and complexity of modern 
operational systems, while Diaz -De-Arcaya et al ., 
(2024) highlight the importance of lifecycle practices, 
monitoring, model maintenance, and integration 
between MLOps and AIOps. These perspectives are 
especially relevant to databases because an anomaly 
model that performs well during a pilot can degrade 
after infrastructure changes, workload evolution, 
software upgrades, or new application features. 
 
3. METHODOLOGY 
This study uses a structured narrative 
review combined with conceptual framework design. 
It does not claim a PRISMA systematic review or a 
statistical meta-analysis. The objective is to integrate 
representative peer -reviewed research on anomaly 
detection, AIOps, and AI-enabled database operations 
with official Saudi policy and governance documents, 
and to convert that evidence into a coherent 
architecture for large-scale database environments. 
 
The literature selection prioritized five 
evidence groups. The first group covers database -
specific anomaly detection, performance diagnosis, 
and AI -for-DBMS research. The second covers 
multivariate time -series anomaly detection and 
benchmarking. The third  covers log parsing and log -
sequence anomaly detection. The fourth covers 
explainable anomaly detection and AIOps lifecycle 
management. The fifth consists of Saudi official 
sources concerning Vision 2030, the National 
Strategy for Data and AI, responsible AI, digital 
government, cybersecurity, data governance, and 
cloud transformation. Sources were selected for 
relevance to operational database monitoring, 
recency where appropriate, methodological 
influence, and direct applicability to Saudi digital 
infrastructure. 
 
The conceptual synthesis followed five steps. 
First, database anomaly types were mapped to 
observable data sources and potential detection 
methods. Second, the strengths and limitations of 
statistical, classical ML, deep learning, sequence, and 
multimodal methods were compared. Third, the 
detection problem was connected to root -cause 
analysis, prioritization, and human operations 
workflows. Fourth, lifecycle risks such as drift, 
explainability, privacy, data quality, and unsafe 
automation were incorporated. Fifth, the architecture 
was aligned with Saudi digital and cybersecurity 
governance. The resulting framework should be 
interpreted as a deployment blueprint to be validated 
empirically in a target organization rather than as a 
claim of universal model superiority.

4. Proposed AI -Enabled Anomaly Detection 
Framework 
The proposed framework contains six 
interacting layers: governed observability, context -
aware data engineering, multi -model anomaly 
detection, multimodal correlation and root -cause 
analysis, risk-based alert orchestration, and human -
in-the-loop lifecycle learning. The design assumes 
heterogeneous database technologies and hybrid 
infrastructure rather than a single database product.

4.1 Layer 1: Governed Observability 
The first layer creates a reliable operational 
data foundation. It collects database KPIs, wait 
events, query statistics, execution -plan identifiers, 
lock information, session state, replication status, 
backup events, cluster state, operating -system and 
storage metrics, cloud monitoring signals, logs, audit 
records, configuration snapshots, deployment events, 
incident records, and service topology. Collection 
must be timestamp -synchronized so that changes 
across layers can be correlated. 
 
A key principle is that telemetry should be 
semantically normalized. Oracle, PostgreSQL, 
Microsoft SQL Server, MySQL, and cloud -managed 
databases expose different native metrics, but many

can be mapped into common concepts such as 
transaction pressure, read I/O stress, write I/O 
stress, concurrency pressure, memory pressure, 
availability state, replication health, and query 
efficiency. Product-specific detail should be retained 
for diagnosi s, while normalized features support 
cross-platform models and enterprise -wide 
dashboards. 
 
Governance begins at ingestion. Sensitive 
SQL text, audit events, identifiers, and operational 
logs may contain personal, confidential, or security -
relevant data. Access control, encryption, retention, 
masking or tokenization, and purpose limitation 
must be defined before telemetry is used for model 
training. Saudi Data Cybersecurity Controls 
emphasize protection across the data lifecycle, while 
SDAIA's data -governance and AI -ethics direction 
supports responsible processing and oversight (NCA, 
2022; SDAIA, 2023). 
 
4.2 Layer 2: Context-Aware Data Engineering 
Raw telemetry must be transformed into 
features that describe behaviour rather than isolated 
measurements. Useful transformations include 
rolling means, percentiles, robust dispersion, rate of 
change, lag features, seasonal residuals, workload -
normalized r atios, wait -class percentages, per -
execution SQL cost, cache efficiency, lock -wait 
intensity, replication -lag acceleration, and 
correlations between resource and query signals. The 
objective is to represent both absolute state and 
change dynamics. 
 
Context is essential to reduce false positives. 
Each observation should be enriched with service 
identity, environment, criticality, database role, 
business calendar, maintenance windows, 
deployment status, batch schedules, backup 
windows, expected growth, and known capacity 
changes. A model that ignores planned maintenance 
will incorrectly classify many legitimate events as 
failures. Conversely, the same metric level may 
deserve different treatment for a low -priority 
development da tabase and a national ser vice with 
strict availability requirements. 
 
4.3 Layer 3: Multi-Model Anomaly Detection Engine 
No single algorithm is optimal for all 
database anomalies. The framework therefore uses 
an ensemble of model families selected according to 
signal type and operational purpose. Transparent 
statistical baselines are retained for stable metrics 
and hard safe ty limits. Isolation or density methods 
can identify multivariate outliers with relatively low 
modelling complexity. Autoencoders can capture 
nonlinear normal patterns and use reconstruction 
error as an anomaly score. Recurrent or transformer 
models can le arn temporal dependencies and 
forecast expected behaviour. Log -sequence models 
can identify abnormal event patterns. Supervised 
classifiers can be used for recurring incident types 
when sufficiently reliable labels exist. 
 
The ensemble should produce calibrated 
evidence rather than a binary alarm from each model. 
For a signal vector x at time t, a simplified composite 
anomaly score may be written as: 
A(t) = w1Sstat(t) + w2Soutlier(t) + w3Srecon(t) + 
w4Sforecast(t) + w5Slog(t), 
 
Where each S term is a normalized score 
from a model family and the weights w are 
determined by validation, service context, and data 
quality. The formula is conceptual: production 
systems should calibrate scores using held -out 
operational periods and inci dent data rather than 
assigning arbitrary weights.

4.4 Layer 4: Multimodal Correlation and Root -
Cause Analysis 
The fourth layer turns detection into 
diagnosis. When an anomaly is detected, the system 
creates an incident window and gathers the changes 
that occurred before and during that period. It 
correlates affected metrics with SQL, execution plans, 
configuration changes, deployments, logs, topology, 
and dependent services. Temporal order is 
important: a storage-latency rise preceding database 
waits has a different interpretation from storage 
latency increasing after a query storm. 
 
A dependency graph can represent 
relationships among services, database instances, 
schemas, queries, hosts, storage volumes, network 
endpoints, and applications. Root -cause ranking can 
then combine temporal precedence, anomaly 
magnitude, graph proximity, k nown failure patterns, 
and change events. Query -level diagnosis should 
distinguish a genuinely causal SQL regression from a 
query that merely consumed resources after another 
component degraded. PinSQL illustrates the value of 
tracing root -cause SQL rather  than stopping at the 
identification of abnormal KPIs (Liu et al., 2022). 
 
Explanations should be concise and evidence 
based. A useful output is not "model score 0.91" but a 
statement such as: "High -confidence performance 
anomaly affecting service X; first divergence detected 
in read latency at 10:32, followed by buffer -cache 
misses and query latency; 68% of incremental I/O is 
associated with SQL fingerprint Q17 introduced after 
deployment D4; no corresponding CPU anomaly; 
recommended investigation: plan regression and 
storage read path." The numerical values in such an 
explanation would come from the live environment, 
not from a generic model. 
 
4.5 Layer 5: Risk-Based Alert Orchestration 
Anomaly severity should reflect more than 
model confidence. The framework proposes a risk 
score combining detection confidence, business 
criticality, expected service impact, persistence, blast 
radius, and evidence of an active change or security 
concern. A conceptual formulation is: 
R = f(A, C, I, P, B, G), 
 
Where A is anomaly confidence, C is service 
criticality, I is predicted impact, P is persistence, B is 
blast radius, and G is governance or security 
sensitivity. The function should be defined by 
organizational policy and validated through incident 
history. 
The orchestration layer groups correlated 
anomalies into a single incident, suppresses 
derivative alerts, and routes the event according to 
risk. Low-confidence anomalies can be recorded for 
learning without paging an operator. Medium -risk 
anomalies can create a ticket and attach diagnostic 
evidence. High-risk anomalies can trigger immediate 
notification, enhanced telemetry collection, and a 
predefined runbook. Automated remediation should 
be introduced only after detection and diagnosis have 
demonstrated s ufficient reliability and should 
initially be limited to reversible, low-risk actions. 
 
4.6 Layer 6: Human -In-The-Loop Learning and 
Model Governance 
DBAs, site -reliability engineers, security 
teams, and application owners remain part of the 
control loop. Operators should be able to confirm an 
anomaly, reject it as expected behaviour, change the 
root-cause category, identify a missed cause, and 
record the actual resolution. This feedback improves 
future models and allows false -alert patterns to be 
discovered. 
 
Model governance includes versioning, 
validation datasets, approval records, performance 
monitoring, drift indicators, retraining triggers, 
rollback procedures, and audit trails. Explainability 
should be treated as a control requirement rather 
than an opti onal visualization. SDAIA's AI Adoption 
Framework and National AI Risk Management 
Framework emphasize governance, reliability, risk 
identification, treatment, and ongoing monitoring, 
which supports a lifecycle approach for operational 
AI (SDAIA, 2024; SDAIA, 2026a). 
 
5. Detection Strategy for Large -Scale Database 
Environments 
5.1 Statistical and Rule-Based Layer 
Statistical methods should not be discarded 
when advanced AI is introduced. They provide low -
cost, explainable baselines and enforce non -
negotiable limits such as storage exhaustion, failed 
backup status, or replication states that require 
immediate attent ion. Robust statistics are 
particularly useful when distributions contain 
occasional spikes. Seasonal baselines can represent 
known hourly and weekly workload patterns. 
Change-point methods can identify structural shifts 
after deployments or configuration changes. 
 
5.2 Unsupervised and Semi-Supervised Learning 
Unsupervised learning should be the default 
starting point for rare and poorly labelled incidents. 
Isolation methods can detect unusual combinations 
of metrics; clustering can identify operating regimes; 
one-class models can learn boundaries around 
healthy periods; and autoencoders can model high -
dimensional normal patterns. Semi -supervised 
learning can improve performance when a limited set 
of trustworthy incident labels exists.

5.3 Deep Temporal Models 
Recurrent networks and transformers are 
useful when sequence and long-range context matter. 
They can learn that a gradual increase in sessions 
followed by lock waits and rising latency represents 
a different state from a short independent spike. 
Transformer attention can capture relationships 
across longer windows, while recurrent architectures 
may be more economical for some streaming 
deployments. Model selection should consider 
inference latency, training cost, memory, 
explainability, and data volume, not only F1-score. 
 
5.4 Log and Change-Event Models 
Logs provide discrete signals that 
complement metrics. A log pipeline should parse 
messages into stable templates, normalize variable 
fields, preserve important parameters, and detect 
new or unusual sequences. DeepLog, LogAnomaly, 
and LogBERT demonstrate d ifferent ways to model 
event sequences and semantics (Du et al., 2017; Meng 
et al., 2019; Guo et al., 2021). In a database context, 
the log model should be correlated with patch, 
deployment, failover, backup, configuration, and 
security events so that a new message pattern can be 
interpreted against operational changes. 
 
5.5 Cross-Database and Distributed Anomalies 
Large Saudi enterprises may operate many 
database nodes and multiple geographic or cloud 
environments. A single -node model can miss 
anomalies that are visible only through comparison. 
Peer-group baselines can compare replicas, cluster 
nodes, or similar ser vices. Distributed anomalies 
include uneven load, network partitions, replication 
divergence, region -specific latency, or failures that 
propagate through shared storage or identity 
services. 
 
Compound anomalies are particularly 
difficult because several faults or symptoms can 
occur simultaneously. Gao et al ., (2025) identify 
compound and distributed scenarios as important 
future directions for database anomaly detection. 
Graph-based correlation and topology-aware models 
are promising because they can represent 
dependencies rather than treating every metric a s 
independent. 
 
6. Evaluation and Validation 
Machine-learning metrics are necessary but 
not sufficient for operational anomaly detection. 
Point-wise precision and recall can be misleading 
when an incident lasts many minutes and produces 
hundreds of anomalous timestamps. Evaluation 
should therefore include event -level metrics that 
assess whether the system detected the incident, how 
early it detected it, how many duplicate alerts were 
generated, and whether the diagnosis was useful. 
 
Precision measures the proportion of 
detected events that were truly anomalous. Recall 
measures the proportion of known incidents that 
were detected. F1 -score balances precision and 
recall, but it should be supplemented by precision -
recall curves when anomalies are rare. False-positive 
rate must be translated into operator burden, such as 
false alerts per database per week. Detection latency 
measures the time between the earliest meaningful 
anomaly and alert generation. Time -to-diagnosis 
measures how quickl y the system identifies a 
plausible root cause. Root -cause top -k accuracy 
evaluates whether the actual cause appears among 
the highest-ranked explanations. 
 
Operational metrics should include mean 
time to acknowledge, mean time to diagnose, mean 
time to recover, number of alerts per incident, 
percentage of incidents with useful early warning, 
and percentage of model recommendations accepted 
by experts. Stabili ty under concept drift should be 
tested across upgrades, workload shifts, seasonal 
periods, and infrastructure changes. Resource 
overhead is also important: telemetry collection and 
model inference must not degrade the database 
systems being protected. 
 
Evaluation datasets should combine three 
sources. First, historical incidents provide realistic 
failure patterns but may contain incomplete labels. 
Second, controlled fault injection or replay can 
reproduce known issues such as lock contention, 
resource sa turation, plan regression, or replication 
delay. DBPA demonstrates the value of reproducible 
anomaly scenarios for transactional databases 
(Huang et al ., 2023). Third, extended normal -
operation periods are necessary to measure false -
alert rates under real workload variability. 
 
A staged validation design is preferable. 
Models should first run in shadow mode without 
paging operators. Their detections are compared 
with incident records and expert review. The next 
stage enables advisory alerts but no automated 
actions. Only after st able performance is 
demonstrated should selected low -risk responses be 
automated. This progression reduces the risk that an 
inaccurate model creates operational disruption.

7. Application to Saudi Digital Infrastructure 
7.1 Government and Citizen Digital Services 
Saudi digital -government platforms depend 
on reliable data services and integrated 
infrastructure. The Digital Government Authority's 
standards link digital -transformation planning with 
Vision 2030 and emphasize coordinated architecture 
and measurable perf ormance (DGA, 2024). 
Intelligent database anomaly detection can support 
these objectives by identifying degradation before it 
affects beneficiaries, correlating incidents across 
application and database layers, and reducing the 
dependence on manual monitoring for large estates. 
 
For citizen-facing services, the most valuable 
use case is early warning rather than autonomous 
repair. A model can detect rising transaction latency, 
replication stress, abnormal connection patterns, or 
query regressions before service-level thresholds are 
breached. The orchestration layer can prioritize 
issues by service criticality and attach a diagnosis 
package for the responsible team. 
 
7.2 Cloud and Hybrid Data Platforms 
Saudi government and private -sector 
organizations are increasing cloud adoption. DGA 
guidance identifies cloud computing as an enabler of 
efficiency, integration, digital services, and secure 
data processing, while the National Cybersecurity 
Authority has updated cloud cybersecurity controls 
for cloud providers and tenants (DGA, 2025; NCA, 
2025b). Cloud databases introduce elasticity and 
managed-service abstraction but also create new 
observability challenges because infrastructure 
control and telemetry differ across providers. 
 
The proposed framework supports hybrid 
environments by separating semantic operational 
features from product -specific metrics. The model 
can learn common signals such as transaction 
pressure and replication health while retaining 
provider-specific details for root -cause analysis. 
Cloud scaling events should be treated as context so 
that legitimate elasticity is not mistaken for an 
anomaly. 
 
7.3 Financial, Healthcare, Industrial, and Smart -
City Platforms 
Financial databases require low latency, 
transaction integrity, auditability, and strong control 
over automated actions. Healthcare platforms 
require availability and data protection. Industrial 
and smart -city environments may integrate 
operational technol ogy, sensors, geographic 
platforms, and real -time analytics. The same 
anomaly-detection architecture can be adapted by 
changing feature sets, risk policies, retention, and 
response thresholds. 
 
8. Governance, Security, and Responsible AI 
Saudi Arabia's policy environment makes 
governance a core component of operational AI. 
SDAIA's National Strategy for Data and AI positions 
data and AI as national economic and social assets. Its 
AI Ethics Principles emphasize responsible use, 
privacy, gove rnance, and mitigation of negative 
impacts, while the AI Adoption Framework and 
National AI Risk Management Framework provide 
lifecycle-oriented guidance for adoption and risk 
treatment (SDAIA, 2023; SDAIA, 2024; SDAIA, 
2026a). Anomaly detection used in cr itical 
infrastructure should therefore be auditable and risk-
aware. 
 
Data governance must cover collection, 
processing, storage, access, retention, and deletion of 
operational telemetry. SQL text and audit data can 
contain personal or commercially sensitive 
information. Training datasets should use the 
minimum information necessary, and sensitive fields 
should be masked or tokenized where detailed values

Cybersecurity requirements also intersect

Data Cybersecurity Controls address protection 
across the data lifecyc le and the Cloud Cybersecurity

root cause. Operators should see confidence, 
contributing signals, model version, data -quality

should requir e human review until the system has 
demonstrated appropriate reliability. All automated 
actions should be logged with preconditions, 
rationale, approval path, execution result, and 
rollback information. 
 
9. Implementation Roadmap 
A practical implementation can be organized 
into four phases. Phase 1 establishes observability 
and baseline quality. The organization inventories 
databases, services, criticality, monitoring coverage, 
log sources, incident records, and data -governance 
constraints. Fixed rules are rationalized, telemetry is 
normalized, and missing monitoring gaps are 
addressed. 
Phase 2 introduces AI -assisted detection in 
shadow mode. Statistical baselines, an unsupervised 
multivariate model, and a log -anomaly model can be 
deployed for a limited set of representative 
databases. Detections are evaluated against historical 
incidents and expert review. The objective is to 
quantify false -alert burden, detection latency, and 
data-quality limitations before operational paging is 
enabled. 
 
Phase 3 adds multimodal correlation, root -
cause ranking, and advisory alerting. Models are 
connected to SQL fingerprints, changes, topology, and 
incident workflows. Explanations and recommended 
investigation steps are shown to DBAs. Human 
feedback is captured systematically. Drift monitoring, 
retraining governance, and model rollback 
procedures become mandatory. 
 
Phase 4 enables selective closed -loop 
actions. Only reversible, low -risk operations with 
strong evidence should be automated initially, such 
as increasing telemetry, collecting diagnostics, 
creating tickets, routing incidents, or executing 
preapproved vali dation queries. Higher -risk 
database changes should remain approval -based 
unless rigorous production evidence supports 
further autonomy. 
 
Success should be measured as an 
operational transformation, not merely a model -
development exercise. A technically accurate 
detector that operators do not trust or that creates 
too many alerts is unsuccessful. The target outcomes 
are fewer noisy alerts, e arlier detection, faster 
diagnosis, improved service reliability, and better use 
of expert time.

10. Limitations and Future Research 
This paper is conceptual and does not 
present an empirical deployment in a Saudi 
production environment. The proposed architecture 
must therefore be validated using organization -
specific telemetry, workload patterns, incident 
histories, and governance requirements. 
Performance results from public anomaly datasets 
cannot be assumed to transfer directly to enterprise 
databases.

© 2026: Global Academic Journal’s Research Consortium (GAJRC)                                                                                                           1275 
 
A second limitation is the scarcity of high -
quality labelled database incidents. Incident tickets 
are often incomplete, root causes may be ambiguous, 
and "normal" periods may contain hidden 
degradations. Future research should develop 
privacy-preserving be nchmark datasets, 
reproducible fault -injection environments, and 
cross-platform anomaly taxonomies that reflect 
Oracle, PostgreSQL, SQL Server, cloud databases, and 
distributed data platforms. 
 
Third, root -cause analysis remains harder 
than detection. Temporal correlation does not prove 
causality, and several symptoms may share an 
upstream cause. Future work should evaluate graph 
neural networks, causal discovery, knowledge 
graphs, and retrieval -augmented diagnostic models 
while maintaining strict evidence controls. 
Generative AI may help summarize incidents and 
retrieve runbooks, but it should not be allowed to 
fabricate causes or execute changes without 
verification. 
 
Fourth, concept drift and operational change 
require continued study. Models should learn from 
legitimate changes without normalizing emerging 
failures. Adaptive thresholds, online learning, drift 
detectors, and change-aware retraining policies need 
evaluation under realistic database lifecycles. Finally, 
research is needed on Arabic -language operational 
interfaces, national -sector datasets, and Saudi -
specific governance patterns so that intelligent 
operations can support local capability development 
as well as technical reliability. 
 
11. CONCLUSION 
Large-scale database environments are 
becoming too dynamic and data -intensive for 
monitoring strategies based only on static thresholds 
and manual interpretation. AI -enabled anomaly 
detection can improve operational awareness by 
learning normal behaviour, identifying multivariate 
and sequential deviations, correlating metrics with 
logs and changes, and prioritizing incidents 
according to service risk. The literature shows that 
statistical baselines, unsupervised learning, deep 
temporal models, log analytics, and database-specific 
diagnosis each contribute useful capabilities, but no 
single model solves the full operational problem. 
 
The framework proposed in this paper 
integrates six layers: governed observability, context-
aware feature engineering, multi -model detection, 
multimodal root -cause analysis, risk -based 
orchestration, and human -in-the-loop lifecycle 
governance. Its central principle is that anomaly 
detection should produce actionable and explainable 
evidence rather than isolated scores. Machine -
learning metrics must be supplemented by 
operational measures such as false -alert burden, 
detection lead time, diagnosis speed, and expert 
acceptance. 
 
For Saudi Arabia, intelligent database 
anomaly detection aligns with Vision 2030 priorities 
around digital and knowledge infrastructure, data 
and AI leadership, resilient cloud services, 
cybersecurity, and efficient digital government. The 
appropriate adop tion path is progressive: establish 
trusted telemetry, validate models in shadow mode, 
introduce advisory diagnosis, and automate only low-
risk actions after evidence of reliability. This 
approach allows AI to strengthen the resilience of 
Saudi digital inf rastructure while preserving 
accountability, cybersecurity, data governance, and 
expert control.
