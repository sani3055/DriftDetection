Multi-cloud adoption has widened the enterprise attack surface to a degree 
that perimeter-based defence can no longer address. Traffic is now flowing 
continuously across AWS, Azure, and GCP, and the majority of deployed Zero 
Trust Architecture (ZTA) system s are still using static rule tables, with no 
ability to provide an audit trail of the reasoning behind decisions, and with 
logs stored in datastores that can be modified by an insider without detection. 
This paper proposes ZT-ChainGuard, a framework that overcomes these three 
limitations in one architecture that integrates an ensemble machine learning 
trust-scoring engine, ZTA policy enforcement and a blockchain -based audit 
trail. The trust-scoring engine is a two-layer stacking ensemble, with XGBoost 
and Random Forest as base learners, and Logistic Regression as a meta -
learner, and it returns a continuous trust score, P(Attack | flow), for each 
network flow, which is then used to trigger the ZT policy decision at a 
threshold of 0.5. The explanation of each decision is provided by SHAP values 
at both the global and per -flow level, and each decision is stored as an 
immutable, SHA-256 hash-chained block. On CICIDS2017 (2.83 million flows, 
14 attack classes) the framework achieves 99.90% accuracy, 99.71% F1 -
score, and 99.99% ROC -AUC; on ToN-IoT (2.23 million IoT records, 9 attack 
types) it achieves 99.81% accuracy, 99.88% F1 -score, and 100% ROC -AUC. 
The latency of inferences is 0.006ms per sample, and the overhead of auditing 
the blockchain is 0.019ms per block. This performance is not just a quirk of a 
particular split, as it is shown to be stable across the three folds of three -fold 
cross validation.

1. Introduction 
The tenets of enterprise network security that have been in place for decades are no longer true. 
Security teams used to think that traffic within a specific network perimeter was more reliable than 


Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science

traffic coming from outside the network, which is the basis of firewall architectures. The boundary 
has been broken down by multi-cloud adoption. 
The percentage of companies that now run workloads on two or more cloud providers at once is well 
over three-quarters, according to a recent industry survey, and it's not just about scale. Resources 
are spun up, moved, and turned off in timescales that sta tic firewall rules can't keep up with, and 
lateral-movement paths that used to exist within a single-tenant data centre show up again, albeit in 
a different form, within a shared multi-cloud fabric. 
Zero Trust Architecture, codified in NIST SP 800-207 [1] and further institutionalized by the 2022 U.S. 
Executive Order on Cybersecurity, is the right answer to this change: don't trust the network, verify 
everything, including requests from anywhere. The idea is good, but the actual implementations are 
often not so good. The majority of deployed ZTA systems still rely on rule tables or static identity 
attributes for access decisions. They don't learn new attack patterns, they provide no justification for 
a compliance officer to review, and if they do have audit logs, they reside in mutable datastores that 
can be easily modified by an insider threat. This paper tackles all three deficiencies in one 
architecture. 
ZT-ChainGuard redefines the ZTA trust score as a machine learning output, namely the posterior 
probability P(Attack | flow) generated by a stacking ensemble on the feature vector of a network 
flow. The reframing is conceptually simple but practically significant: a probability is interpretable, it 
can be adjusted to suit an organisation's risk appetite, and it can be cleanly decomposed into the 
contributions of the features by using SHAP. None of these can be said of a rule -table lookup. Each 
policy decision is then recorded as a cryptographically chained block in a blockchain ledger, either as 
an ALLOW or DENY. If a decision is modified after logging, the hash chain is broken after that point 
and the audit trail is much more difficult to alter than a standard log file. 
The selection of evaluation datasets is based on the multi -cloud scenario that the framework is 
designed for. CICIDS2017 [2] is the traffic observed at cloud provider egress points, TCP/IP dominated 
enterprise flows covering a taxonomy of 14 attack categories, and ToN-IoT [3] is the IoT and IIoT layer 
that is growing at the edge of cloud deployments, with Zeek -extracted connection records across 
MQTT, HTTP, and DNS for 9 attack types. Testing the same architecture on both, without any cross -
dataset feature engineering, will determine if the framework generalises to truly heterogeneous 
environments. 
The contributions of this work are as follows. 
● A redefinition of the ZTA trust score as a continuous machine learning probability, P(Attack | 
flow), derived from a stacking ensemble and used directly to drive ZT policy enforcement in place of 
static rule tables. 
● Two-layer stacking architecture of XGBoost and Random Forest base learners and a Logistic 
Regression meta-learner, trained separately for binary and 14-class multi-class detection, with 
explicit consideration of the SMOTE application order to avoid a mismatch between the number of 
labels in the binary oversampled arrays and the number of classes in the multi-class labels.

2. Related Work 
2.1 Zero Trust Architecture 
The original Zero Trust concept by Kindervag was not a technical specification but a design 
philosophy. Rose et al. [1] put it into practice in NIST SP 800 -207, which outlines the need for 
continuous verification, least-privilege access, and micro-segmentation that most contemporary ZTA 
systems try to achieve. Later, ZT ideas have been expanded to more specialized fields: ZTA is 
extended to 6G network architectures by Chen et al. [4] and a more critical perspective is given by 
Fernandez and Brazhuk [5] who point out that there is a gap between the principles of ZTA and the 
systems that organisations deploy. The most prevalent failure they report is replacing static identity-
based policy with the behaviour-driven trust scoring that the Zero Trust model requires. 
This gap is starkly demonstrated by Khan's survey [6] which found that 72% of government 
organisations had active ZT programmes in 2023, but the vast majority of these programmes were 
based on identity verification only, rather than behavioural analytics. This is important because 
identity can be spoofed; what ZTA is designed to provide is ongoing behavioural verification that is 
always skeptical even of an authenticated session. Weinberg et al. [7] place this gap in the context of 
the evolution of emerging technologies, and say that anomaly detection and Extended Detection and 
Response (XDR) are the logical next steps for ZTA. The first step towards this is taken by Wu et al. [8] 
who propose a privacy-domain-theoretic dynamic security framework, but their threat model is more 
limited than the multi-cloud intrusion detection scenario considered here. 
2.2 Machine Learning for Intrusion Detection 
The literature on ML-based intrusion detection is large enough that a comprehensive review would 
constitute a paper of its own. The thread most relevant here concerns stacking ensembles applied to 
standard benchmarks. Shtayat et al. [9] combined Random For est, XGBoost, and Extra -Trees with a 
Logistic Regression meta -model and achieved near -perfect accuracy on both CICIDS2017 and NSL -
KDD, a result that independently supports the choice of base learners adopted in this paper, although 
their framework includes  neither ZTA integration nor audit logging. El Gemayel et al. [10] report 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(6s), (2026) 1773-1788 
1776 
99.32% accuracy on CICIDS2017 using ensemble deep learning, and Karatas et al. [11] show that 
dimensionality reduction combined with multi -stacking consistently outperforms single -model 
approaches on large, imbalanced datasets. 
The SmartTrust system [12] is perhaps the closest prior work in spirit to ZT -ChainGuard, targeting 
cloud threat detection under ZTA principles via a hybrid CNN -LSTM-Transformer architecture. Its 
detection rates are competitive, but it incorporates no ensemble stacking, no SHAP-level explanation, 
and no per-decision audit trail. Pelletier and Abualkibash [13] establish that standard ML classifiers 
achieve 96 –98% accuracy on CICIDS2017, a useful lower bound against which any serious system 
should be measured.  Pawlicki et al. [14] are notably more cautious, demonstrating through 
adversarial perturbation experiments that XGBoost trained within an ensemble maintains detection 
reliability better than several alternatives under attack, a finding that partly motivat ed its selection 
as a base learner in the present work. 
2.3 Explainable AI for Cybersecurity 
The literature on intrusion detection using ML is too vast to be reviewed in this paper. The most 
relevant thread here is the stacking ensembles on standard benchmarks. Shtayat et al. [9] achieved 
near-perfect accuracy on both CICIDS2017 and NSL-KDD datasets using a combination of three base 
learners (Random Forest, XGBoost and Extra -Trees) with a Logistic Regression meta -model, further 
supporting the selection of base learners used in this paper, but without the addition of ZTA or audit 
logging. Ensemble d eep learning has been used to achieve 99.32% accuracy on CICIDS2017 by El 
Gemayel et al. [10] and dimensionality reduction and multi-stacking has been shown to consistently 
outperform single-model approaches on large, imbalanced datasets by Karatas et al. [11]. 
Inspired by ZTA, the closest work in spirit to ZT-ChainGuard is the SmartTrust system [12] which aims 
to detect threats in the cloud in a hybrid CNN -LSTM-Transformer system. It has detection rates that 
are competitive, but it does not include any ensemble stacking, any SHAP-level explanation, and any 
audit trail per decision. The standard ML classifiers are found to be 96–98% accurate for any serious 
system as a lower bound in the CICIDS2017 [13] dataset. Pawlicki et al. [14] are more reserved, 
showing that XGBoost trained as a base learner in an ensemble is more reliable at detecting attacks 
than several other alternatives in their adversarial perturbation experiments, which partly inspired 
their choice of XGBoost as a base learner in the present work. 
2.4 Blockchain for Security Audit 
Blockchain-based audit logging for security systems has drawn sustained attention since 2019. In the 
IEEE Access survey, Khan et al. [19] provide a comprehensive overview of the space, and conclude 
that immutability and decentralisation are the properties that make blockchain -based logging 
different from signed log files. The BCE -IoT framework [20] integrates blockchain with intrusion 
detection based on SHAP for IoT networks, and provides tamper -resistance results that are directly 
comparable to those provi ded here, where the use of lightweight cryptographic primitives (XXTEA, 
ECC) is appropriate for constrained devices. Sharma et al. [21] adopt a more holistic approach by 
integrating blockchain into a Zero Trust network for Industry 5.0 with Federated Trans fer Learning,

does in this work. 
The most ambitious prior combination of these components is the Digital Twin ZTA framework [22] 
which combines NSL -KDD, CICIDS2017, IoT -23, hash -chained ledgers and differential privacy, and 
results in 89–91% accuracy with MLP and CNN -BiLSTM models. For po sitioning purposes, there are

in-line deployment.

3. Datasets, Statistics, and Preprocessing 
3.1 Why These Two Datasets 
The choice of CICIDS2017 and ToN -IoT is not a conventional selection. Most intrusion detection 
papers are based on one dataset, typically NSL -KDD (1999) or CICIDS2017. The two here are used 
because the multi-cloud scenario studied includes at least two qualitatively different types of traffic: 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(6s), (2026) 1773-1788 
1778 
enterprise IT workloads, which are typically deployed on compute instances and load balancers, and 
IoT edge deployments, which are typically sensors, gateways, and lightweight devices that connect 
to cloud backends via MQTT and other similar protocols. CIC IDS2017 and ToN -IoT are these two 
environments, having different feature spaces, different protocol mixes, and different class -
imbalance ratios. The same framework is run on both, with no cross-dataset feature engineering, and 
this is a meaningful test of generalisability. 
3.2 CICIDS2017 
The Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity Intrusion Detection Evaluation Dataset 2017 [2] was created 
during five working days in July 2017 at the University of New Brunswick. It was generated using the 
B-Profile system, which simulated 25 abstract user -behaviour profiles using HTTP, HTTPS, FTP, SSH 
and email. The traffic was captured by a mirror -port tap and processed with CICFlowMeter to 
produce 78–80 numerical features per flow: packet counts, byte totals, inter -arrival times, TCP flag 
counts, and various statistical aggregates over the flow duration. 
The raw CSV files contain a pre-processing trap that is easy to fall into. Label names and label values 
include quote characters and whitespace, and the literal string ‘BENIGN’ is not equal to BENIGN, 
leading to label -encoding failures in naive pipelines t hat are not detected: no label matches the 
stripped target string. This was not recorded in several related works that were reviewed for this 
paper and was the cause of sub -50% ROC-AUC in our preliminary experiments prior to the fix. It is 
reported here because it is directly related to reproducibility.

3.4 Data Fusion Strategy 
The two data sets are treated independently. This may seem like a missed opportunity for joint 
learning, but it is the right thing to do with the data. Their feature spaces are incommensurable: 
CICIDS2017 contains CICFlowMeter-derived statistical aggregates, including Init_Win_bytes_forward 
and Bwd Packet Length Std, while ToN-IoT contains Zeek connection-level attributes like dst_pkts and 
dns_qclass. If the datasets were simply concatenated, there would be a need to align features, and 
the imputation decisions made to fill in the gaps would be a confounder in the results.  
We rather consider CICIDS2017 and ToN-IoT as two different cloud zones, enterprise IT and IoT edge, 
and train and evaluate one model for each zone. The blockchain audit layer mimics this by logging a 
cloud_zone attribute on each decision recorded, with AWS -US-EAST for decisions originating from 
AWS and GCP -ASIA-SOUTH for decisions originating from ToN -IoT, as if a real multi -cloud ZT 
deployment would do so. The SHAP analysis then produces two distinct importance profiles, one for 
each zone, with the most di scriminative features being very different for enterprise traffic and IoT 
traffic, as one would expect. 
3.5 Preprocessing Pipeline 
The order of the transformations is important: scaling should be done before SMOTE and SMOTE 
should be applied to the binary training split only, otherwise the label -size mismatch problem 
described below will occur. 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(6s), (2026) 1773-1788 
1780 
1. Quoting and stripping of whitespaces. Leading and trailing spaces and single-quotes are 
removed from column names and label values. This step is necessary for CICIDS2017 and is applied 
to all for consistency. 
2. Stratified sampling (CICIDS2017 only). A 30% stratified random sample is taken, maintaining 
the class proportions, and producing approximately 850,000 flows, without introducing bias in the 
class distribution and with a manageable memory footprint. 
3. Data cleaning. Approximately 0.3-0.5% of records are removed by replacing infinite values 
with NaN and dropping them, as well as by removing duplicate rows 
4. Label encoding. All attack classes are assigned 1, and BENIGN/normal is assigned 0. All 14 
CICIDS2017 classes and all 10 ToN-IoT classes are preserved using scikit-learn's LabelEncoder for 
multi-class labels. 
5. Stratified 80/20 split, stratified on the binary label, ensuring proportional attack 
representation in the test set even for rare classes. 
6. MinMax normalisation. All features are standardized to [0,1] based on statistics from the 
training set only, and the test set features are transformed with the same scaler to avoid 
information leakage from the test set. 
7. Oversampling using SMOTE (only for binary training split). The pre-SMOTE scaled training 
matrix and multi-class training labels are saved separately as 𝑋_𝑡𝑟𝑎𝑖𝑛_𝑜𝑟𝑖𝑔 and ym_train before 
SMOTE runs. This is an important step: SMOTE adds 𝑁𝑜𝑟𝑖𝑔𝑖𝑛𝑎𝑙 + 𝑁𝑠𝑚𝑜𝑡𝑒 − 𝑁_𝑜𝑟𝑖𝑔𝑖𝑛𝑎𝑙 rows to the 
binary training set, while the multi-class label vector has only 𝑁_𝑜𝑟𝑖𝑔𝑖𝑛𝑎𝑙 entries, so the 
combination of the SMOTE expanded matrix and the multi-class label vector results in a shape 
mismatch, which causes the model to fail silently or crash depending on the version of the 
framework. 
4. ZT-ChainGuard Architecture 
4.1 Overview 
ZT-ChainGuard consists of six functional stages that trace the data flow from raw network traffic to 
logged audit decision.ZT -ChainGuard consists of six functional stages that trace the data flow from 
raw network traffic to logged audit decision. It is helpful to separate the training-time path (stages 1-
3) from the inference -time path (stages 3 -6). During the training phase, the ensemble is trained to 
map the vectors of the flow features to the posterior attack probabilities, while during the inference 
phase, each new flow is scored, the score is used to make a ZT policy decision, and the decision is 
added to the blockchain ledger with its corresponding SHAP explanation.

4.2 The Trust Scoring Ensemble 
The stacking ensemble is a two-layer ensemble. In Layer 1, XGBoost and Random Forest are trained 
separately on the SMOTE -balanced binary training set, obtaining class -probability vectors 
𝑃(𝐵𝑒𝑛𝑖𝑔𝑛 | 𝑥) and 𝑃(𝐴𝑡𝑡𝑎𝑐𝑘 | 𝑥)  for each flow x. The two probability values from each base learner 
are then combined into a meta -feature vector of length four, which is the input to Layer 2. In Layer 
2, a Logistic Regression meta -learner is trained on these meta -features to produce the f inal trust 
score: 
𝑇𝑟𝑢𝑠𝑡𝑆𝑐𝑜𝑟𝑒(𝑥) = 𝑃(𝐴𝑡𝑡𝑎𝑐𝑘|𝑥) = 𝑀𝑒𝑡𝑎𝐿𝑅(𝑋𝐺𝐵𝑜𝑜𝑠𝑡(𝑥), 𝑅𝐹(𝑥)) 
This particular combination is not for accuracy, but for diversity in the ensemble. XGBoost constructs 
trees one after another, with each new tree focusing on the errors of the previous ensemble, which 
is well suited to learning non-linear interactions between features but can overfit if the regularisation 
hyperparameters are not optimised. Random Forest constructs trees independently on bootstrap 
samples, resulting in good variance reduction for some increase in bias. Stacking is beneficial over 
either of the two methods since they are not effective on all types of examples. The Logistic 
Regression meta-learner is designed to be simple: its purpose is to reweight the outputs of the base-
learner, not to add to the complexity of the model.

where 1.0 corresponds to ‘definitely benign’ and gives ROC-AUC close to 0.5 when the convention of 
‘greater is more suspicious' is applied to setting thresholds.

Given the trust score, the ZT policy decision follows directly:

𝐴𝐿𝐿𝑂𝑊, 𝑇𝑟𝑢𝑠𝑡𝑆𝑐𝑜𝑟𝑒(𝑥) < 𝜏 
where 𝜏 = 0.5 is the default decision threshold, which is the maximum -likelihood decision boundary

reported at 𝜏 = 0.5, and would be adjusted on a held -out validation set during operational 
deployments.

Two types of output are produced. Global importance plots show the mean |φj| over the test set, 
which represents the features that are most important to the model's decisions overall. Local 
waterfall plots break down individual decisions, which is great fo r auditing: If a flow is denied, the 
waterfall will display exactly which features were most responsible for the denial, and by how much. 
The five most globally important features on CICIDS2017 were Init_Win_bytes_forward (mean 
|SHAP| = 1.94), Destination Port (1.71), Bwd Packet Length Std (1.14), Init_Win_bytes_backward 
(1.08), and Average Packet Size (0.98). On ToN -IoT the ranking was dst_port (1.99), dst_pkts (1.73), 
src_ip_bytes (1.15), duration (1.04), and dns_qclass (1.03). These differences align with the two types 
of traffic: enterprise traffic is most evident in TCP window and packet -length statistics, while IoT

traffic is most evident in destination -port and packet -count statistics, which is consistent with the 
features extracted by Zeek for ToN-IoT. 
4.5 Blockchain Audit Layer 
Each access decision (ALLOW or DENY) is written as a block in a SHA -256 hash-chained ledger. Each 
block contains: (i) a sequential block index; (ii) a unix timestamp; (iii) a flow_id which is a hash of the 
flow's source IP, destination IP, port, and unix t imestamp; (iv) the trust score scaled to an integer in 
[0, 10000]; (v) the ZT decision; (vi) the ground -truth label (for offline evaluation purposes only); (vii) 
a cloud_zone string describing the logical cloud zone from which the flow originated; (viii) t he top-5 
SHAP feature attributions for that flow; and (ix) the SHA -256 hash of the contents of the current 
block. The hash of the previous block is part of the contents of the current block before hashing, 
creating the chain dependency that makes it easy t o detect tampering: If the hash of the previous 
block is changed, the hash of the current block will also change, thus making it impossible to find a 
block with the same prev_hash, and the inconsistency will cascade up the chain. 
The implementation is based on an in -process EVM -compatible ledger (eth_tester backend) that 
offers realistic hash computation and gas accounting without the need to run a node. The Solidity 
smart contract ZTAuditLog.sol is designed to be efficient in term s of throughput, allowing for the 
batch logging of up to 50 decisions per transaction, while also triggering a ThreatAlert event when 
the flow's trust score exceeds 0.90, which can be used to integrate with external alerting pipelines. 
Real deployments of consortium blockchain networks (such as Hyperledger Fabric or Ethereum 
Sepolia) would impose consensus latency in addition to the hashing cost measured here; calculating 
this additional cost is a future research goal outlined in Section 6.

5.7 On the Near-Perfect Metrics: What They Mean and What They Do Not 
The results reported above, accuracy above 99% and ROC-AUC at or near 100% on both datasets, will 
reasonably prompt a reviewer to ask whether something has gone wrong. That question deserves a 
direct answer rather than a footnote. 
5.7.1 These Results Are Normal for These Benchmarks 
CICIDS2017 and ToN-IoT are controlled testbed datasets. Their attack traffic was generated by a small 
number of machines executing well-defined attack scripts, and their benign traffic by a profile-based 
simulator, so the statistical signatures of attacks are strong and consistent: DoS Hulk generates 
systematically high SYN flag counts and anomalous byte rates, SSH -Patator produces characteristic 
burst connection patterns, and so on. The learned decision boundary uses these signatures and the 
test set comes  from the same distribution as the training set. In our trust -score analysis, the 
separation is calculated directly: benign flows have a mean of 0.0014, attack flows have a mean of 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(6s), (2026) 1773-1788 
1785 
0.9979, and the separation in probability space is 0.9965 on a [0,1] scale, which means that the two 
classes barely overlap. This is not anomalous, the accuracy of CICIDS2017 has been reported by 
multiple independent groups to be above 99%, such as Shtayat  et al. [9] (99.9%) and El Gemayel et 
al. [10] (99.32%) and a comprehensive survey [24] states that ensemble methods on CICIDS2017 
routinely achieve accuracy above 99%. The results found here are in the middle of this range. 
5.7.2 The Architecture Contributes Incrementally but Verifiably 
The performance boost over simpler baselines is due to three design decisions. Firstly, SMOTE for the 
binary training split guarantees that the model is exposed to balanced classes during training, which 
would otherwise result in a trivial majority-class bias and reduce the recall on minority attack classes 
while increasing the accuracy. Second, the stacking architecture leverages the diversity of error 
patterns of XGBoost and Random Forest, which leads to a 0.01 percentage -point gain in Recall and 
F1 over XGBoost alone, and is consistent across both datasets and all three folds of the cross 
validation. Third, MinMax normalisation will give equal scales to feature contribution which is 
important for the Logistic Regression meta-layer. 
5.7.3 What the Results Do Not Imply 
High benchmark performance on controlled testbed data does not imply equivalent performance on 
live production traffic. Real networks have encrypted flows, protocol polymorphism, and zero -day 
attacks which are not represented in CICIDS2017 or ToN -IoT. The results reported here are near -
perfect, and provide an upper bound: the architecture works correctly, and its components integrate 
as designed. They do not replace the need for evaluation on production captures, adversarial 
perturbation testing, or concept-drift analysis, all of which are listed as future work in Section 6. 
6. Discussion 
6.1 What Is Actually Novel 
It is important to distinguish what ZT -ChainGuard achieves technically from what its system -level 
novelty consists of. There's not much new on the component level: Stacking ensembles is a common 
approach, SHAP is already known, and blockchain logging has b een used in security applications 
before. The novelty is in the architecture and how it works. First, the ZT trust score is not a rule-table 
output but rather a probability of attack, P(Attack | flow), which is continuous, probabilistic, and 
interpretable, and can be tuned directly to an organisation's risk tolerance; no previous ZTA system 
reviewed in this paper uses a posterior probability as a trust signal. Second, the per -decision SHAP 
decomposition is integrated into the ZT enforcement outcome itself: the explanation is a part of the 
enforcement record, not a separate artefact, as it is produced as part of the decision itself. Third, the 
per-flow blockchain log contains the SHAP attribution as block payload, which means that the 
reasoning behind a decision is carried along with the decision itself. The combination of that decision, 
trust score and feature attribution in a single tamper evident block has not been seen in previous 
work reviewed here.

6.2 Practical Considerations 
The latency figures matter for the deployment argument. ZT-ChainGuard generates a trust score and 
makes a policy decision in 0.006 ms per flow, and logs the policy decision to the blockchain in 0.015-
0.019 ms, with a total per -flow overhead of less than 0. 03 ms, which is well within the bounds of 
what can be done in-line at typical cloud-egress bandwidths. The ZT-ChainGuard overhead is several 
orders of magnitude below operational significance, for example, the round -trip latency of a cloud 
API call is typically measured in tens to hundreds of milliseconds. 
The SHAP explainability layer addresses two regulatory requirements at once. GDPR Article 22 
prohibits automated decisions that have a significant impact on the individual, and allows the 
individual to ask for an explanation. Automated network access contr ol is likely to be considered a 
high-risk AI system under the EU AI Act and is required to comply with certain transparency 
requirements. The waterfall plots of the per-decision information, generated by ZT-ChainGuard, is a 
natural fit for that explanation  artefact. In reality, this is important because regulatory compliance 
continues to be a major obstacle to the use of ML in regulated industries like financial services and 
healthcare. 
6.3 Limitations and Future Work 
Three limitations deserve honest acknowledgment. The first is the evaluation gap discussed in 
Section 5.7.3: controlled testbed results are not production results, and the framework needs to be 
evaluated on real or more realistic synthetic traffic before p roduction claims can be made with 
confidence. The second is that the current architecture relies on a centralised trust -scoring engine, 
which is a single point of failure in a true multi-cloud deployment; the obvious next step is to federate 
the training of the model across the cloud zones, with each cloud zone training its own model based 
on its local data, while contributing to a global model without sending the raw traffic. The third is 
that the blockchain part is deployed on an in -process EVM simulator;  if it is deployed on a live 
permissioned blockchain, for enterprise deployments Hyperledger Fabric, or for consortium 
deployments Ethereum Sepolia, the consensus latency will not be present in our measurements. 
Two further directions seem particularly worthwhile beyond these. The adversarial robustness 
evaluation would involve perturbing the trust -scoring engine with FGSM and PGD, as detailed by 
Pawlicki et al. [14] and measure the degradation in detection performance by an attacker who knows 
the architecture of the trust -scoring engine. If the attack patterns are continually changing, then a 
deployment that runs for a long time would require concept -drift monitoring, which would monitor 
the distribution of the fe atures over time and trigger a retraining of the model when the detection 
performance drops. 
7. Conclusion 
This paper set out to address three specific deficiencies in current Zero Trust implementations: static 
rule-based trust scoring, opaque decision reasoning, and mutable audit trails. ZT-ChainGuard tackles 
all three with a single architecture that maps netw ork-flow features to a probabilistic trust score 
using a stacking ensemble, decomposes each decision into feature attributions using TreeSHAP, and 
records both the decision and the feature attributions as an immutable hash-chained block.

The empirical results on CICIDS2017 and ToN -IoT are promising: 99.90% accuracy and 99.99% ROC -
AUC on CICIDS2017, 99.81% accuracy and 100% ROC -AUC on ToN -IoT, and 0.006 ms inference 
latency, and less than 0.02 ms blockchain overhead per decision. We have go ne to great lengths to 
make these numbers clear and to make them clear that they are not ready for unmodified 
deployment on live enterprise traffic. The journey from here to production involves adversarial 
robustness testing, live-traffic evaluation and deployment on a real consortium blockchain. 
The deeper contribution, in our view, is the architectural pattern itself: treating the ZT trust score as 
a continuous ML posterior, linking explainability to enforcement at the per -decision level, and 
persisting the reasoning inside the audit record. This  pattern is not specific to the classifiers or the 
blockchain platform used here and can be used with other base learners, other XAI methods, or other 
distributed ledger technologies, depending on operational constraints.
