Abstract 
Security operations centres receive far more alerts than analysts can review, and the probabilities, rankings and 
explanations that machine learning attaches to those alerts are rarely checked for reliability. This article designs 
a data-driven analytics framework that makes alert scores mean what they say, carries uncertainty alongside 
them, and explains them in a way whose quality is measured, and it evaluates the framework on a transparent 
synthetic testbed: 1,200 alerts per day from eight families, a ra re and drifting incident class, four asset -value 
tiers, a fixed review capacity of 12.5%, and a stylised analyst model. Raw scores from deep boosted trees ranked 
incidents well but under-predicted risk by a factor of five (mean predicted probability 0.84% against a true rate 
of 4.2%); every post -hoc calibrator cut the expected calibration error from 0.034 to between 0.006 and 0.009, 
drift raised it to 0.017, and a sliding-window recalibrator using delayed labels held it at 0.008. Calibration did 
not change a ranking by probability alone, but it changed the queue once probability was multiplied by asset 
loss: loss captured rose from 59.7% to 68.3%, Tier -3 incidents missed fell from 27.6% to 6.9%, and net value 
rose from 29.4 to 34.5 thousand USD per day. Conf ormal sets, ensemble spread and explanations behaved less 
favourably: spread did not signal drift, explanation stability (0.65) was unrelated to uncertainty, and in the 
analyst model uncertainty flags and explanations traded recall against precision without raising net value. In a 
2,000-draw Monte Carlo analysis the calibrated queue beat the raw -score baseline in every draw, and Sobol 
indices attribute the gain mainly to loss scale and attack prevalence. All results describe the testbed, and the 
analyst findings are hypotheses for studies with analysts, not findings about them. A governance record and a 
four-phase roadmap are proposed.

Introduction 
A security operations centre  (SOC) is a queueing system with an adversary. Detectors, rules and 
analytics emit alerts; analysts investigate as many as their time allows; and the incidents that matter 
must be found among a stream in which most alerts are false. The imbalance is old. A xelsson showed 
that when attacks are rare, even a detector with a low false-positive rate produces mostly false alarms 
(Axelsson, 2000), and studies of working analysts describe alert volumes, false positives and tool sprawl 
as central sources of frustration and burnout (Alahmadi et al., 2022; Kokulu et al., 2019; Sundaramurthy 
et al., 2015 ; Vielberth et al., 2020 ). Machine learning is the usual response. Classifiers, clustering and 
provenance analysis reduce false positives an d rank what remains ( Julisch, 2003 ; Pietraszek, 2004 ; 
Hassan et al., 2019; Veeramachaneni et al., 2016), and explainable AI (XAI) is added so that the analyst 
can see why an alert scored highly ( Ribeiro et al., 2016 ; Lundberg & Lee, 2017 ; Capuano et al., 2022 ; 
Nadeem et al., 2023). 
What the analyst sees, however, is usually a n umber, a rank and an explanation, and each is a claim 
that can be checked. The number is read as a probability, but classifier scores are often not probabilities: 
modern classifiers are miscalibrated, boosted trees distort the scores they emit, and calibration degrades 
when the data shift (Guo et al., 2017; Niculescu-Mizil & Caruana, 2005; Ovadia et al., 2019; Minderer et 
al., 2021). A rank hides how sure the model is, and an alert the model has never seen anything like looks 
the same as a routine one. An explanation is a second mod el of the first, and attributions can be 
unstable, unfaithful or manipulable ( Adebayo et al., 2018 ; Ghorbani et al., 2019 ; Slack et al., 2020 ; 
Krishna et al., 2022). Meanwhile the human and the machine form a team whose performance depends 
on how much the analyst relies on the machine; reliance that is too high produces automation bias and 
complacency, and reliance that is too low wastes the machine ( Parasuraman & Riley, 1997 ; 
Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010 ; Lee & See, 2004 ; Skitka et al., 1999 ). Calibrated probabilities, honest 
uncertainty and measured explanations are the machine's side of that bargain. 
This article asks what an analytics framework for alert prioritization should contain if the probability, 
the uncertainty and the explanation are all to be reliable, and what such a framework is worth. Four 
research questions guide it. RQ1: how miscalibrated are alert scores, how much do calibrators repair, 
and how do the repairs hold up under drift, across alert families and with few labels? RQ2: what do 
uncertainty signals (ensemble spread and conformal sets) add, in coverage, abstention and error? RQ3: 
in a capacity-limited queue with asset values, what is the decision value of calibrated and uncertainty-
aware prioritization? RQ4: how do explanations behave, and what, under an explicit and stylised 
analyst model, do calibrated displays, uncertainty flags and explanations do to decisions and to net 
value, and which assumptions matter most? 
The method is design science with a simulation study ( Hevner et al., 2004; Peffers et al., 2007; Gregor 
& Hevner, 2013): requirements are drawn from the literature, a four-layer framework is designed from 
them, and the framework is exercised on a transparent synthetic testbed whose every parameter is 
stated, because no real SOC data were used  (Ashik & Shabbir, 2022 ; Hasan & Adnan, 2022 ; Imran & 
Jannatul, 2021). The testbed has eight alert families, a rare incident class with a hard subset, four asset-
criticality tiers, a c ovariate and prior shift in the test period, delayed labels, and a review capacity of 
12.5% of daily alerts (Akter & Sumaiya, 2022; Mashfiqur & Md, 2022; Md & Yeasin, 2022). Five detectors, 
six calibrators, class -conditional conformal sets, ensemble spread, Shapley attributions and a noisy -
Bayesian analyst model are evaluated with three seeds, and the decision value is propagated through 
2,000 Monte Carlo draws with Sobol indices and a metamodel (Sobol', 2001; Saltelli et al., 2010). 
The contribution is threefold. It states six requirements for reliable, explainable alert prioritization and 
a framework that meets them. It reports, on one transparent testbed, where calibration changes 
decisions (when probability is combined with value or compared with a cost) and where it does not 
(when a single score is merely rank ed), and where uncertainty signals and explanations disappoint. 
And it provides a governance record, a set of reliance and calibration metrics, and a phased roadmap 
for a SOC that wants to test the ideas on its own alerts. The findings are about the testbe d. The 
orderings, rather than the numbers, are what a reader should carry to other settings, and the analyst 
results in particular are hypotheses to be tested with people.

Background and Related Work 
Alert fatigue and the work of the SOC 
The practice literature on SOCs is mostly qualitative. Interviews with analysts and managers describe 
a work system in which tools generate more alerts than staff can handle, in which false positives 
dominate, and in which the metrics used to manage the ce ntre (volume closed, time to close) do not 
reward careful investigation ( Kokulu et al., 2019 ; Alahmadi et al., 2022 ). A human -capital model of 
analyst burnout treats alert load and monotony as drivers of turnover ( Sundaramurthy et al., 2015 ), 
and a systematic study of SOC research lists people, process and technology challenges, among them  
the integration of analytics with analyst workflow (Vielberth et al., 2020). Parallel evidence from health 
care, where alarm fatigue and alert override are well documented, shows how repeated, most ly 
irrelevant alerts erode attention and trust ( Cvach, 2012; Ancker et al., 2017 ), and the old warning of 
Breznitz about the psychology of false alarms describes the same mechanism (Breznitz, 1984). The base-
rate arithmetic is the quantitative core: when incidents are a few per cent of alerts, precision is low 
unless discrimination is very high (Axelsson, 2000; Sommer & Paxson, 2010; Arp et al., 2022). Burnout 
is also a cost to the organisation, and the cost of the queue is therefore not only the cost of the time 
spent but the cost of the attention lost ( Maslach et al., 2001 ). These sources describe perceptions and 
organisational patterns; they do not pro vide measurements of the reliability of the numbers on the 
analyst's screen, which is the gap this article addresses. 
Alert correlation, triage and learning 
Reducing and ranking alerts has a long history. Alert correlation merges related alarms into attack  
scenarios (Cuppens & Miège, 2002; Valdes & Skinner, 2001); clustering finds root causes that generate 
most false alarms (Julisch & Dacier, 2002; Julisch, 2003); adaptive classifiers learn from analyst feedback 
to suppress false positives ( Pietraszek, 2004; Spathoulas & Katsikas, 2010 ); belief propagation over 
enterprise logs ranks suspicious early-stage infections (Oprea et al., 2015); analyst-in-the-loop systems 
combine unsupervised and supervised models and learn from analyst labels ( Veeramachaneni et al., 
2016); and provenance-based triage scores alerts by the rarity of the causal paths behind them (Hassan 
et al., 2019; Hassan et al., 2020). Evaluations of intrusion detection have themselves been criticised for 
unrepresentative data and experimental bias (McHugh, 2000; Tavallaee et al., 2009; Engelen et al., 2021; 
Arp et al., 2022). Most of this work reports ranking quality, precision and recall. It seldom asks whether 
the scores are probabilities, which is what an expected-value queue, a cost-based threshold or a trusting 
analyst needs. 
Probability calibration 
A classifier is calibrated when, among the cases it scores at 0.2, about one in five is positive. The concept 
is old in forecasting, where reliability diagrams and decompositions of the Brier score were developed 
(Brier, 1950; Murphy, 1973; DeGroot & Fienberg, 1983; Dawid, 1982; Bröcker & Smith, 2007), and proper 
scoring rules explain why the Brier score and log loss reward calibrated, sharp forecasts ( Gneiting & 
Raftery, 2007). For classifiers, Platt scaling and isotonic regression map scores to probabilities ( Platt, 
2000; Zadrozny & Elkan, 2002), and Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana showed that boosted trees need such 
mapping because boosting pushes predicted probabilities away from the extremes (Niculescu-Mizil & 
Caruana, 2005). Beta calibration and Dirichlet calibration extend the family (Kull et al., 2017; Kull et al., 
2019), and temperature scaling, a single -parameter rescaling of the logit, repairs much of the 
miscalibration of neural networks ( Guo et al., 2017 ). Expected calibration error (ECE) is the usual 
summary, but binned estimators are biased and sensitive to b inning, and calibration needs tests and 
not only a number ( Naeini et al., 2015 ; Nixon et al., 2019 ; Vaicenavicius et al., 2019 ; Widmann et al., 
2019; Kumar et al., 2019 ). Newer architectures are not uniformly worse calibrated than o lder ones 
(Minderer et al., 2021 ), and per -group calibration (multicalibration) can fail even when overall 
calibration holds ( Hébert-Johnson et al., 2018 ; Pleiss et al., 2017 ). For security, the relevant questions 
are practical: how much labelled data a calibrator needs, whether calibration survives drift, and 
whether one calibrator serves all alert families. 
Uncertainty estimation 
Predictive uncertainty has an aleatoric part (noise in the data that no model can remove) and an 
epistemic part (ignorance that more data or a better model could reduce) ( Kendall & Gal, 2017 ; 
Hüllermeier & Waegeman, 2021 ; Depeweg et al., 2018 ). Deep ensembles, Monte Carlo dropout,

Bayesian approximations and evidential methods estimate it ( Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017 ; Gal & 
Ghahramani, 2016; Maddox et al., 2019; Sensoy et al., 2018; Wilson & Izmailov, 2020; Fort et al., 2019), 
and surveys compare them (Abdar et al., 2021; Gawlikowski et al., 2023). Their behaviour under dataset 
shift is the test that matters for security, and the evidence is that quality degrades with shift and that 
ensembles are a strong baseline ( Ovadia et al., 2019 ). The maximum softmax probability is a simple 
baseline for flagging misclassified and out-of-distribution inputs (Hendrycks & Gimpel, 2017). Whether 
the spread of an ensemble rises on drifted security alerts, and whether it distinguishes hard incidents 
from easy ones, is an empirical question that the testbed can answer for itself  (Ashfaq & Mohammad, 
2022; Imran & Jannatul, 2022; Morshedul & Islam, 2022). 
Conformal prediction and risk control 
Conformal prediction wraps any predictor in sets with a fin ite-sample coverage guarantee under 
exchangeability (Vovk et al., 2005; Shafer & Vovk, 2008; Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023). Split (inductive) 
conformal prediction uses a held-out calibration set (Papadopoulos et al., 2002; Lei et al., 2018), class-
conditional or Mondrian variants give coverage within each class, which matters when one class is rare 
(Vovk et al., 2005 ; Vovk, 2012), and a daptive or weighted variants address covariate shift and non -
exchangeable data ( Tibshirani et al., 2019 ; Gibbs & Candès, 2021 ; Barber et al., 2023 ). Set -valued 
classifiers, adaptive sets and risk -controlling sets extend the idea ( Sadinle et al., 2019 ; Romano et al., 
2020; Angelopoulos et al., 2021 ; Bates et al., 2021 ; Angelopoulos et al., 2024 ). Conditional coverage 
cannot be guaranteed in distribution-free settings (Foygel Barber et al., 2021). In malware classification, 
conformal evaluation has been used to detect concept drift ( Jordaney et al., 2017; Barbero et al., 2022). 
For alert triage, a conformal set with both classes in it is a principled version of "the model does not 
know", and this article uses it as an escalation signal.  
Selective prediction, deferral and human-AI teams 
A classifier with a reject option abstains when its confidence is low, and the trade-off between coverage 
and risk goes back to Chow (Chow, 1970; El-Yaniv & Wiener, 2010; Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017; Geifman 
& El-Yaniv, 2019). Learning to defer extends abstention by training the model jointly with the decision 
about which cases a human should handle (Madras et al., 2018; Mozannar & Sontag, 2020; Okati et al., 
2021; Raghu et al., 2019 ), and complementarity is the goal: the team should do better than either 
member (Wilder et al., 2020; Bansal et al., 2019; Kamar et al., 2012; Horvitz, 1999). Empirically, human 
decisions can be improved by machine predictions in some settings ( Kleinberg et al., 2018 ), but the 
labels on which such systems are trained and evaluated are selective, because outcomes are observed 
only for cases that humans acted on ( Lakkaraju et al., 2017 ). That problem is acute in a SOC, where 
unreviewed alerts are rarely labelled. 
Dataset shift and drift in security 
Security data drift because adversaries adapt, software changes and the mix of traffic changes  (Kaniz 
& Jahanara, 2022; Sadia & Mostari, 2022; Stabak & Shahid, 2022). Sommer and Paxson explained why 
machine learning for intrusion detection is hard, among other reasons because of the open -world 
problem and the cost of errors ( Sommer & Paxson, 2010 ); Arp and coll eagues catalogued pitfalls in 
learning-based security research (Arp et al., 2022); TESSERACT showed how temporal and spatial bias 
inflate malware-classifier results (Pendlebury et al., 2019); and concept drift in classifiers is a standing 
problem (Gama et al., 2014; Lu et al., 2019; Quiñonero-Candela et al., 2009; Moreno-Torres et al., 2012). 
Label-shift methods adjust probabilities when class priors change ( Saerens et al., 2002 ; Lipton, 2018). 
For calibrated alert probabilities, drift is a first-order concern: a calibrator fitted on last quarter's alerts 
may be wrong this  week, and the corrections available (recalibration on recent labelled alerts, prior 
correction, adaptive conformal updates) depend on labels that arrive late (Mohammad, 2023; Adnan & 
Iftekhar, 2023; Feroz & Pervaz, 2022). 
Explainable AI for security and its quality 
Post-hoc explanation methods attribute a prediction to features, through local surrogates, anchors, 
Shapley values or counterfactuals ( Ribeiro et al., 2016 ; Ribeiro et al., 2018 ; Lundberg & Lee, 2017 ; 
Štrumbelj & Kononenko, 2014 ; Mothilal et al., 2020 ), and there are surveys, taxonomies and critiques 
(Guidotti et al., 2018 ; Barredo Arrieta et al., 2020 ; Adadi & Berrada, 2018 ; Miller, 2019; Rudin, 2019; 
Doshi-Velez & Kim, 2017; Lipton, 2018). Shapley values have a clean axiomatic basis ( Shapley, 1953), 
efficient algorithms for trees ( Lundberg et al., 2020 ), and well -known limitations when features are

dependent or when used as importance measures ( Kumar et al., 2020 ; Janzing et al., 2020 ; Aas et al., 
2021; Covert et al., 2021 ; Fisher et al., 2019 ). Surveys of explainable AI in cybersecurity and of 
explainable intrusion detection identify the analyst as the intended reader and the lack of evaluation 
with analysts as a gap (Capuano et al., 2022; Nadeem et al., 2023; Neupane et al., 2022; Mahbooba et al., 
2021; Wang et al., 2020). The quality of an explanation is not given. Saliency maps can fail sanity checks, 
attributions can be changed by small perturbations, explanation methods disagree, and classifiers can 
be built to fool LIME and SHAP (Adebayo et al., 2018; Ghorbani et al., 2019; Alvarez-Melis & Jaakkola, 
2018; Krishna et al., 2022 ; Slack et al., 2020 ; Hooker et al., 2019 ; Yeh et al., 2019 ; Bhatt et al., 2020 ). 
Uncertainty has been proposed as a form  of transparency, and explanations can themselves carry 
uncertainty (Bhatt et al., 2021; Slack et al., 2021; Zhao et al., 2021), which makes attribution spread and 
stability natural diagnostics. 
Human reliance on automation and the effect of explanations 
The human-factors literature treats reliance as a decision that should track the automation's reliability 
(Ahmed & Sadia, 2023 ; Islam & Md, 2023 ; Asheke, 2023): the vocabulary of use, misuse, disuse and 
abuse; levels of automation; complacency and automa tion bias; trust calibration; and the ironies of 
automating the easy part and leaving the human the hard part ( Parasuraman & Riley, 1997 ; 
Parasuraman et al., 2000; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010; Lee & See, 2004; Hoff & Bashir, 2015; Skitka et 
al., 1999; Bainbridge, 1983; Cummings, 2004; Goddard et al., 2012). Algorithm aversion and algorithm 
appreciation show that reliance can err in both directions (Dietvorst et al., 2015; Logg et al., 2019), and 
situation awareness frames what the operator needs to keep ( Endsley, 1995 ). Studies with people 
suggest that explanations do not reliably improve joint performance and can inc rease reliance on 
incorrect advice (Gazi Yeasin, 2023; Jahanara & Kaniz, 2023; Sheak & Tahmedur, 2023), that displaying 
a model's confidence can help align trust with correctness, that forcing deliberation can reduce 
overreliance, and that stated accuracy shapes trust (Bansal et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020; Buçinca et al., 
2021; Lai & Tan, 2019; Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al., 2021; Vasconcelos et al., 2023; Wang & Yin, 2021; Yin 
et al., 2019; Kocielnik et al., 2019; Green & Chen, 2019). These are findings about particular tasks and 
populations, mostly not security analysts, and they are used here only to motivate the analyst model's 
structure and to flag what must be tested. 
Cost-sensitive decisions, queues and governance 
Decisions under asymmetric costs have a classical form: act when the expected loss avoided exceeds 
the cost of acting, which for a binary decision is a threshold on the probability that depends on the costs 
(Elkan, 2001; Provost & Fawcett, 2001 ). Threshold choice, cost curves, decision curves and expected -
loss views of performance metrics make the d ependence on costs and prevalence explicit ( Adams & 
Hand, 1999; Drummond & Holte, 2006 ; Vickers & Elkin, 2006 ; Hand, 2009; Hernández-Orallo et al., 
2012). When capacity binds, the problem is one of scheduling: ordering jobs by weight over processing 
time minimises weighted completion on a single resource ( Smith, 1956; Pinedo, 2016), and priority 
queues formalise waiting ( Cobham, 1954 ; Harchol-Balter, 2013 ). In alert triage, the weight is the 
expected loss avoided, which is the calibrated probability times the asset's value, and the processing 
time is the review time (Kaium, 2023; Pervaz, 2023; Badrul & Mominul, 2023). Governance adds records 
and oversight: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework, ISO/IEC 42001 and 23894 for AI 
management and risk, ISO /IEC 27035 and NIST SP 800 -61 for incident management, and the EU AI 
Act, whose human -oversight provisions apply to high -risk systems ( Tabassi, 2023; ISO/IEC, 2023a; 
ISO/IEC, 2023c ; ISO/IEC, 2023b ; Cichonski et al., 2012 ; European Parliament and Council, 2024b ; 
National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2024). NIST SP 800-61 Revision 2 is cited here; I believe 
a later revision has since been issued, so the current edition should be checked before the guidance is 
applied (Nishat, 2023; Sadia, 2023; Stabak & Md Siam, 2023). Whether an alert-prioritization tool falls 
under the EU AI Act's high-risk categories depends on its use, and legal advice, not this article, should 
settle it; application dates under the Act are phased and may have changed (Mohammad, 2024; Feroz, 
2023; Tahmedur & Rifat, 2023).

(Table 2). R1, probabilities that mean what they say: the score shown to the analyst and used in the 
queue must be calibrated on recent labelled data, and calibration must be reported by alert family and

period. R2, uncertainty beyond the point estimate: the framework must carry a measure of what the 
model does not know and a r ule for escalating alerts in that region. R3, adaptation under drift with

Methods 
Study design 
The study follows the design -science logic of Hevner and colleagues: an artefact (the framework) is 
built from requirements and evaluated against stated criteria ( Hevner et al., 2004 ; Peffers et al., 2007 ; 
Gregor & Hevner, 2013). Evaluation is by simulation, because the quantities of interest (calibration error 
under a known drift, the value of a queue ordering, the effect of a display on a decision) need ground 
truth that a real SOC does not have. Simulation conventions follow Law and Sargent: common random 
numbers, verification checks, a results file from which every table and figure is produced, and honest 
statements of validity ( Law, 2015; Sargent, 2013). Figure 3 gives the design: a synthetic alert testbed 
with drift; five detectors and six calibrators; verification; experiments on calibration, conformal sets, 
triage, explanations and analyst conditions; and a Monte Carlo and Sobol analysis of the decision value. 
The four research questions map to the experiments: RQ1 to the calibration experiments, RQ2 to 
conformal sets and spread, RQ3 to the triage experiments and RQ4 to the explanation, analyst and 
Monte Carlo experiments. Every random element is seeded, th ree seeds are run, and results are 
reported as means with standard deviations over seeds.

Testbed: alerts, drift, labels and value 
The testbed generates 1,200 alerts per day from eight families (Table 3). Days 0 to 29 train the detectors, 
days 30 to 39 are the calibration window, and da ys 40 to 59 are the test period, which contains a drift 
episode from its ninth day. Each alert has 32 features: a family indicator, eight detector scores, eight 
context features (user risk, peer deviation, asset criticality, hour of day, indicators-of-compromise hits, 
geographic rarity, volume z-score and one spare) and eight nuisance features. Noise is Student t with 
five degrees of freedom, scaled by family. Incidents occur at family -specific rates between 1.2% and 
10%, for a mean of about 4%; they raise their own family's detector score, user risk, peer deviation and

10 
 
indicator hits; and 30% of incidents are hard (low-and-slow), with signals about a quarter as strong, so 
that part of the uncertainty is irreducible. Six per cent of benign alerts are look -alikes that raise their 
family's detector. Labels are complete and correct in every experiment; the selective, delayed and noisy 
labels of real SOCs are discussed as a limitation and not simulated (Figure 4(b), (c) and (d) show the 
prevalence by day, one detector's shift and the asset tiers). 
The drift is the kind security teams meet: a variant lowers detector 3's response to family 3 incidents, a 
new scanner makes benign alerts look like family 0 incidents, nuisance features shift, and a seven-day 
campaign multiplies the prevalence of family 5. Asset criticality is a feature, observable to the model 
and the policy, and sets the loss avoided if a true incident is investigated the same day (Table 4). The 
value of an alert to the queue is therefore the probabili ty of an incident times the asset's loss, and the 
cost of reviewing it is analyst time. Both the loss scale and the review cost are stylised and are varied 
in the Monte Carlo analysis. Labels for day d become available on day d + 2, which is the delay used  
by the adaptive methods.

Detectors, calibrators and uncertainty 
Five detectors (Table 5) span the usual choices: logistic regression, a deliberately flexible boosted -tree 
model that overfits the rare class (one member and a five -member bagged ensemble), a regularised 
shallow boosted tree, and an ensemble of five small neural networks (Ke et al., 2017; Friedman, 2001; 
Hastie et al., 2009; Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017). The flexible tree is the main object of study, because 
it ranks well and is poorly calibrated, which is the situation in which calibration matters. Calibrators 
are fitted on the calibration window and applied to the test period: temperature scaling, Platt scaling, 
beta calibration, isotonic regression and histogram binning with 15 equal -mass bins (Guo et al., 2017; 
Platt, 2000; Kull et al., 2017; Zadrozny & Elkan, 2002). A per-family variant fits one Platt model per alert 
family, and an adaptive variant refits Platt scaling each test day on the labelled alerts of days d − 7 to d 
− 2 together with the last four days of the calibration window. A calibration -set size sweep refits each 
calibrator on the most recent 500 to 12,000 calibration aler ts. Calibration is reported as ECE with 15 
equal-mass bins, the Brier score and log loss, and by reliability curves over deciles (Naeini et al., 2015; 
Brier, 1950; Gneiting & Raftery, 2007). Because binned ECE is biased and depends on binning, ECE is 
read alongside the proper scores and the reliability curves (Nixon et al., 2019; Vaicenavicius et al., 2019). 
Two uncertainty signals are used. The first is the spread (standard deviation) of the predicted 
probabilities of the five bagged deep trees, an epistemic proxy. The second is p(1 − p) of the calibrated 
probability, an aleatoric proxy. Conformal sets use Mondrian (class -conditional) split conformal 
prediction on the isotonic-calibrated probability: half of the calibration window fi ts the isotonic map, 
the other half supplies the quantile of the nonconformity score 1 − p of the true class, separately for 
incidents and benign alerts, at α = 0.05, 0.10 and 0.20. A set can be {benign}, {malicious}, {both} or empty; 
{both} is the ambiguous outcome used as an escalation signal (Vovk et al., 2005; Angelopoulos & Bates, 
2023; Papadopoulos et al., 2002). An adaptive variant lowers or raises the working miscoverage level 
for each class by a step of 0.05 in proportion to the error observed on labels two days old ( Gibbs & 
Candès, 2021). Selective prediction keeps the alerts with the lowest uncertainty and reports the error 
rate of the kept alerts at a threshold of 0.5 as coverage falls (Chow, 1970; Geifman & El-Yaniv, 2017).

Prioritisation and economics 
The queue reviews, each day, the 150 alerts (12.5%) with the highest priority score. Ten orderings are 
compared: random; the raw score; the calibrated probability; the raw score times asset loss; the 
calibrated probability times asset loss; the same with per-family and adaptive calibration; the calibrated 
probability plus or minus one ensemble spread, times loss (an upper and a lower confidence variant);  
and an oracle that knows the labels. Ordering by expected loss avoided per unit of review time is the 
weighted-shortest-processing-time rule, optimal for a single resource when review time is constant 
(Smith, 1956; Pinedo, 2016). A capacity sweep from 3% to 25% shows how the ordering matters as the 
constraint tightens. A second experiment drops the capacity limit and applies the cost -optimal 
threshold rule, revie w if p × loss exceeds the review cost, with each probability source, including a 
prior-shift correction using the test period's prevalence odds, which is an oracle quantity used only to 
bound what correction could achieve ( Elkan, 2001; Saerens et al., 2002). Net value is the loss avoided 
on confirmed incidents less the cost of the reviews and of false escalations. All values are in USD per 
day of the testbed. 
Explanations 
Shapley attributions use TreeSHAP for each of the five bagged members on a sample of 1,000 test alerts 
per seed (400 incidents and 600 benign alerts), with the mean over members as the explanation and the 
spread as its uncertainty (Lundberg et al., 2020; Lundberg & Lee, 2017; Štrumbelj & Kononenko, 2014). 
Four properties are measured. Stability is the mean Jaccard index of the top -three features between 
member pairs. Agreement with ground truth is the share of the top -three features, by mean absolute 
attribution, that belong to the four truly informative features of the alert's family (its own detector, user 
risk, peer deviation and indicator hits), which a real SOC cannot know. Faithfulness is the deletion 
ratio: the mean absolute change in the ensemble logit when the three top -attributed features are 
replaced by their training means, divided by the same change for three random features; a ratio of one 
means the attribution is no better than chance ( Yeh et al., 2019; Hooker et al., 2019; Bhatt et al., 2020). 
Surrogate fidelity is the R-squared and AUROC of a depth-three regression tree fitted to the calibrated 
member probability on calibration alerts and evaluated o n the test period (Bastani et al., 2017; Rudin, 
2019). Each property is reported by tercile of ensemble spread, by easy and hard incidents, and before 
and after drift. 
Analyst model 
No analysts were studied, so the human side is a model whose assumptions are explicit and varied. 
For each reviewed alert the analyst forms own evidence as a log -likelihood ratio: +2.2 for an easy 
incident, +0.6 for a hard incident and −1.0 for a benign alert, with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 
1.5. The decision combines this with the machine's displayed log-odds, weighted by a reliance weight 
w (0.7 at baseline) and shrunk towards the base rate otherwise: posterior log-odds equal own evidence 
plus (1 − w) times the base-rate log-odds plus w times the displayed log-odds, and the analyst escalates 
when the posterior probability exceeds 0.2 (Lee & See, 2004; Parasuraman & Manzey, 2010). Conditions 
differ in what is displayed and in the queue. An uncertainty flag (mid-range probability or high spread) 
halves w for flagged alerts. An explanation raises w by 0.15 and raises own evidence by 25% when the 
top-three attribution is stable, and it costs 20% more review time; both effects are assumptions 
motivated by the finding that explanations raise acceptance of advice and can help only when they are 
informative (Bansal et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2020; Buçinca et al., 2021). Nine conditions are run on the 
same test alerts and the same noise draws: the analyst alone with a random queue and with the value 
queue; the raw score shown with the raw queue and with the value queue; the calibrated probability 
shown; the calibrated probability with a flag, wi th an explanation, and with both; and the raw score 
with an explanation. Reliance is measured as the share of machine advice (the upper half of each day's 
queue by displayed probability) that the analyst follows when the advice is wrong and when it is right.

explanation gain (0 to 0.4), explanation trust (0 to 0.3), flag shrinkage (0 to 0.8) and explanation time

Verification 
Checks were run before the experiments were interpreted. The random queue found 12.7% of incidents 
at a capacity of 12.5%, as it should; the oracle queue found all of them and gave the highest net value; 
the logistic regression, which is calibrated by construction on this data, had an ECE of 0.005 and a mean 
probability equal to the prevalence; the drift raised the test prevalence from 4.2% to 5.6% and lowered 
the AUPRC of every tree and  neural model, and Figure 4(c) shows the intended detector shift; 
conformal sets reached at least the nominal coverage on the calibration half; and the two analyst-alone 
conditions, whose decisions ignore the display, confirmed the same share of incidents under a random 
queue and under the value queue (53.4% and 52.8%). One error found during verification is worth 
recording: an early version computed the benign nonconformity score as 1 − (1 − p), which breaks ties 
through floating-point rounding and made co verage depend on isotonic plateaus; the score is now 
computed directly, and all results here use the corrected code. 
Results 
All results come from the synthetic testbed and are means with standard deviations over three seeds 
unless stated. Absolute values (ECE, recall, USD) describe the testbed and should not be read as 
estimates for any real operation. The orderings and mechanisms are the findings, and even those hold 
only under the assumptions in Tables 3 and 4. 
Detector quality and raw calibration 
Table 6 shows ranking quality and calibration for the five detectors. The shallow boosted tree ranks 
best (AUROC 0.802 and AUPRC 0.237 before drift), the bagged deep trees next (0.779 and 0.213), and 
the logistic regression and MLP ensemble lowest (0.765 and 0.7 58). Ranking quality is modest 
throughout, because 30% of incidents are hard by design and part of the signal is irreducible. 
Calibration does not follow ranking. The logistic regression (ECE 0.005) and the shallow tree (0.007) are 
close to calibrated befo re drift. The MLP ensemble is moderately off (0.021). The deep trees are the 
worst (0.034 for a single tree and for the bagged ensemble) and are wrong in a particular direction: their 
mean predicted probability is 0.84% against a true incident rate of 4.21 %, so they under -predict by a 
factor of five, and the error grows after drift to 0.049 and 0.048. Raw scores of the deep trees therefore 
rank well and mislead as probabilities, which is the case in which calibration can matter. Figure 5(a) 
and (b) plot the two measures by detector and drift period.

Table 7 and Figure 5(c) give the effect of each calibrator on the deep boosted tree. Every post -hoc 
method removes most of the error before drift: ECE falls from 0.034 to between 0.006 (temperature 
scaling) and 0.009 (histogram binning), a reduction of 72% to 82%, and log loss falls from 0.218 to 0.153, 
a reduction of 30%. The methods are hard to separate at this sample size; temperature, Platt, beta and 
isotonic differ by less than one standard deviation. Brier scores improve little (0.0394 to 0.0374), because 
the Brier score is dominated by the rare -event variance that calibration cannot change. AUROC is 
unchanged by the monotone parametric calibrators and falls slightly with isotonic regression (0.759) 
and histogram binning (0.755), whose plateaus create ties. After drift every static calibrator degrades 
to an ECE of about 0.017, so the benefit shrinks from about 82% to about 66% of the raw error. Adaptive 
Platt scaling, refitted daily on labels two days old, holds ECE at 0.0078, which is 55% below static Platt 
scaling and 84% below the raw score. Figure 5(d) shows the cost of having few labels. With the most 
recent 500 calibration alerts, which contain about 20 incidents, temperature scaling has an ECE of 0.011, 
Platt scaling 0.017, isotonic regression 0.021 and histogram binning 0.026. At 1,000 alerts the values are 
0.007, 0.009, 0.011 and 0.017. The non -parametric methods need roughly 4,000 alerts or more to 
approach the parametric ones (0.006, 0.007, 0.008 and 0.010), and at 12,000 alerts all four lie be tween

deep tree before and after drift, and Fi gure 6(c) shows the MLP ensemble, whose mean probability

matters inside families, as Table 8 shows.

Conformal sets and uncertainty signals 
Table 9 gives the Mondrian conformal results on isotonic-calibrated scores. Class-conditional coverage 
was at or above its nominal level before drift: 0.969 and 0.980 at α = 0.05 (target 0.95), 0.931 and 0.928 
at α = 0.10 (target 0.90) and 0.835 for both classes at α = 0.20 (target 0.80). The excess over target is 3 to 
4 points at the two looser levels. I suspect it comes from  the finite-sample correction and the plateaus 
of the isotonic map, but I did not test that. After drift, coverage of incidents fell by 1.5 points at α = 0.10 
(0.916) and by 4.6 points at α = 0.20 (0.789, below the 0.80 target), while coverage of benign alerts barely 
moved. The adaptive update left incident coverage at 0.910 and brought benign coverage to 0.908 at α 
= 0.10, so both classes sat within about a point of target; but static sets were already close, and the 
adaptive variant gave more single -label sets that were less accurate (77.0% against 80.1% after drift).

static method, so day-to-day variation is as large as the drift effect (Figure 7(a)).

statement about the accuracy of single-label sets. An ambiguous set is therefore a weak escalation signal 
in this testbed, since it applies to roughly two-thirds of alerts at the 10% level (Figure 7(b)).

Table 13 and Figure 10 give the results of the nine analyst conditions. Every statement in this section is

Discussion 
Answers to the research questions 
RQ1 asked how well alert scores are calibrated and how the repairs hold up under drift, across families 
and with few labels. The raw scores of the deep trees under-predicted incidents by a factor of five, and 
any post-hoc calibrator removed 72% to 82% of t hat error on a window from the same period. Under 
drift the static calibrators kept about two -thirds of the benefit, daily recalibration with delayed labels 
kept most of it, and a global calibrator left a family-level error three to eleven times larger in the family 
hit by the campaign (0.077 against 0.007 to 0.025). With few labels a one -parameter method did best. 
RQ2 asked what uncertainty signals add. Conformal sets kept class-conditional coverage close to target, 
but were ambiguous for most alerts; ensemble spread was a modest improvement over probability for 
choosing alerts to set aside, did not rise with drift, and was unrelated to explanation stability. RQ3 
asked about the decision value of calibrated, uncertainty -aware prioritisation under capacity. 
Calibration alone did not change a ranking. When the probability was multiplied by asset loss, the 
value of the queue rose 17%, and under a cost -optimal rule the calibrated probability about doubled 
the net value. Adding a spread bound or per-family and adaptive calibration changed the net value by 
between −1.8 and +1.5 thousand a day around 34.5 thousand. RQ4 asked about explanations and about 
displays for analysts. Explanations were moderately stable (0.65) and better than chance on faithfulness 
(2.4), and their stability was not tied to uncertainty. Under the stylised analyst model, the calibrated 
display was worth much more than the raw display, whereas flags and explanations moved the balance 
between recall and precision without raising net value. 
Why calibration matters only when probabilities are used as probabilities 
The triage result is stated more precisely as follows. A calibrated probability and a raw score give the 
same queue when the queue is a ranking by probability alone. They differ when the number is 


American Journal of Scholarly Research and Innovation, December 2024, 01– 40 
 
26 
 
combined with something else: multiplied by an asset loss, compared with a review cost, shown to a 
person, or averaged across families (Ahmed, 2024; Islam, 2024; Asheke, 2024). A constant factor would 
not reorder a product, so the effect needs error that varies across the score range, and the raw deep-tree 
scores are compressed and uneven in that way: an alert on a cheap asset with a high raw score and one 
on a critical asset with a moderate score trade places differently under raw and true probabilities  
(Ashrafuzzaman, 2024 ; Jannatul, 2024 ; Kynatun & Saba, 2024 ). Against a fixed review cost even a 
constant factor matters, since a score five times too small puts most alerts below the threshold, which 
is the doubling seen under the cost-optimal rule (Elkan, 2001; Vickers & Elkin, 2006; Van Calster et al., 
2019). Both mechanisms need the number to be used as a number. I did not run the queue with the 
shallow tree, whose raw score is already close to calibrated (ECE 0.007), so the inference that a well -
calibrated raw score would do as well as a recalibrated one is plausible but untested here  (Mashiat, 
2024; Sumaiya, 2024; Feroz, 2024). The practical point is that ECE should be reported for the model that 
will be used with a loss or a cost, and that the value of calibration should be ev aluated in that setting, 
not on the accuracy or AUROC that calibration leaves unchanged ( Gneiting & Raftery, 2007; Nixon et 
al., 2019). 
Calibration under drift and with few labels 
The drift in this study was one moderate episode, and static calibrators lost about a third of their benefit 
in it. Adaptive recalibration with labels two days old recovered most of that, which supports a practice 
of scheduled recalibration with explicit monitoring of ECE and prevalence by week. Two caveats apply. 
The recalibration labels were complete and correct, whereas real SOC labels exist mainly for alerts that 
were reviewed, and the reviewed alerts are the highest-ranked ones; a model recalibrated on them will 
tend to learn the selection and not the truth, which is why a randomised audit sample of unreviewed 
alerts is among the requirements (Lakkaraju et al., 2017; Ovadia et al., 2019; Gama et al., 2014). And the 
benefit of any calibration depends on the amount of data: with 500 recent alerts, containing about 20 
incidents, only temperature scaling stayed near 0.01. For rare families this suggests pooling alerts across 
families with a family indicator or a shared temperature, and refining only when sample sizes allow, 
which is consistent with the modest and uneven gains of per-family models in Table 8 (Kull et al., 2017; 
Guo et al., 2017). 

American Journal of Scholarly Research and Innovation, December 2024, 01– 40 
 
27 
 
Uncertainty signals 
The conformal sets were valid and uninformative, because t he detector was weak. Validity here has 
two limits. Coverage was class-conditional and marginal over alerts, so it said nothing about coverage 
for a single family or asset tier, and under drift coverage of incidents fell below target at α = 0.20, as 
one would expect when a shift breaks exchangeability, while the same shift had only a small effect at α 
= 0.10 (Tibshirani et al., 2019; Barber et al., 2023; Gibbs & Candès, 2021). The spread gave a risk-ordered 
abstention that was a little better than the probability, but it did not behave as an epistemic signal: it 
was highest for easy incidents,  lowest for benign alerts, and flat across the drift. This is a caution for 
practice. Ensemble disagreement is often described as model uncertainty, and in a boosted -tree 
ensemble trained on the same data it may mostly reflect the score level, so a team th at wants a drift 
alarm should monitor calibration error and feature distributions directly and not rely on the spread 
(Hüllermeier & Waegeman, 2021; Lakshminarayanan et al., 2017; Ovadia et al., 2019). 
Explanations as a control 
The explanations in this study were neither useless nor trustworthy by default. They were moderately 
stable, better than chance at finding the informative features and at changing the output when the 
attributed features were removed, and a short surrogate tree explained little. The finding that stability 
did not depend on uncertainty matters for how a display is designed: an uncertainty flag cannot stand 
in for an explanation -quality flag, so any note on stability must be computed from the members 
directly, as it was for the display in Figure 2. The deletion test replaces features with training means, 
and that creates off-distribution inputs, so the faithfulness ratio is an indicator and not a proof (Hooker 
et al., 2019 ; Ghorbani et al., 2019 ; Krishna et al., 2022 ). The NISTIR 8312 principles of explainable AI 
separate the accuracy of an explanation from the limits of the system's knowledge, and the results 
support the separation: faithfulness and stability are properties of the explanation, spread and coverage 
are properties of the prediction, and they vary independently (Phillips et al., 2021; Adebayo et al., 2018). 
Human decision-making 
The analyst-model results are hypotheses. They follow from assumed weights, and I make no claim 
that SOC analysts behave this way. Within those assumptions they show three things. First, a display 
can do harm: the raw display lowered the net value below the analyst working alone, because its 
miscalibration was imported into the analyst's decision, in line with the observation that people over -
rely on advice that appears confident and under -rely on advic e that appears weak ( Parasuraman & 
Manzey, 2010; Lee & See, 2004; Skitka et al., 1999). Second, a calibrated probability avoided that harm 
(12.4 thousand above the raw display) and added 4.3 thousand over the analyst alone, a gain about the 
size of that condition's variation over seeds, and it did so without any added review time. Third, flags 
and explanations traded one error for another: the flag reduced both following of wrong advice and 
confirmation of true incidents, the explanation did the opposite, and neither raised net value, in line 
with studies in which explanations increased acceptance of advice whether or not the advice was right 
(Bansal et al., 2021 ; Buçinca et al., 2021 ; Poursabzi-Sangdeh et al., 2021 ). Whether a real flag lowers 
reliance only when the advice is wrong depends on how well the flag separates wrong from right 
advice; in the model it lowered both. Controlled studies with analysts, using real task s and the 
measures of reliance in Table 13, are needed before any of these effects are used for design ( Green & 
Chen, 2019; Kocielnik et al., 2019; Alahmadi et al., 2022). 
Implications for operators 
Five implications follow, each with its limit. Measure calibration for the score that feeds a loss or a 
threshold, and report it by family, since a global figure hid a family in which the error was up to eleven 
times larger. Rank by expected value, not by probability or count: on this testbed the calibrated value 
queue found 24.5% of incidents against 39.4% for the probability ranking, yet captured 68 .3% of the 
loss against 37.1%, so teams that report alerts closed or incidents found will mistake it for a regression, 
and the metric that follows the objective is loss captured or net value at the capacity actually available 
(Smith, 1956; Pinedo, 2016; Kleinberg et al., 2018 ). Use the cheapest calibrator that the labelled data 
supports, refresh it on a schedule, and keep an audit sample of unreviewed alerts so that the labels are 
not only those of the alerts that were ranked highest. Show a calibrated probability to analysts and test 
any flag or explanation as a separate intervention, measuring both recall a nd precision, since each 
moved them in opposite directions. And treat the base -rate arithmetic as part of the design: at an 

American Journal of Scholarly Research and Innovation, December 2024, 01– 40 
 
28 
 
incident rate of about 4% the precision of even a good queue is low, as the 9.9% to 15.9% in Table 10 
shows, which is why alert fatigue is a property of the situation and not only of the detector (Axelsson, 
2000; Cvach, 2012; Sundaramurthy et al., 2015). 
Implications for standards, regulation and governance 
The results connect to the standards in four ways, and some of those standards have changed or may 
change, so the citations should be checked against current versions. The incident-handling guide cited 
here, NIST SP 800 -61 Revision 2, treats prioritisation, as I read it, as a function of the functional and 
informational impact of an incident and the recoverability from it, which is the role asset criticality 
plays in the value queue (Cichonski et al., 2012; ISO/IEC, 2023b). I believe that NIST has since replaced 
that revision with a new one aligned to the Cybersecurity Fra mework 2.0, but I have not verified its 
current status or content, and a reader applying this work should consult the NIST publication page

Implications for analytics practice 
The study illustrates five practices. Evaluate on the period the model will face and not on the period it 
was trained on: drift raised the prevalence from 4.2% to 5.6% and degraded every calibrator, and the 
check can be run from the same label store ( Sommer & Paxson, 2010; Arp et al., 2022). Report proper 
scores and reliability curves next to ECE, which depends on binning ( Brier, 1950; Naeini et al., 2015 ; 
Vaicenavicius et al., 2019 ). Separate the three questions of ranking, probability and decision, because 
the tools that answer each are different and a gain on one does not carry to the others. Treat the human 
part of a pipeline as a component to be measured, with reliance on wrong and right advice reported 
next to accuracy (Bainbridge, 1983; Amershi et al., 2019). And propagate the decision into money under 
uncertainty with Sobol indices, so that the team knows which input to measure first: in this study the 
loss scale and the attack rate, not the explanation parameters (Sobol', 2001; Saltelli et al., 2010; Herman 
& Usher, 2017). 
Limitations 
Table 15 lists the limitations with their consequences and the way each could be addressed. The most 
important are three. The data, the drift, the costs and the analyst model are synthetic, so the orderings 
are conditional findings about a model world and absolute values, such as the ECE of 0.034 or the USD 
figures, carry no empirical weight. The analyst model has assumed parameters, and the effects of 
displays, flags and explanations are consequences of those assumptions. And labels are complete and 
correct, which real SOC labels are not; selection bias in labels is a known problem for models trained 
on reviewed alerts (Lakkaraju et al., 2017; Pendlebury et al., 2019). The study used three seeds and 20 
test days, so intervals for rare families and for top -tier incidents are wide, and only differences much 
larger than the standard deviation (calibrated against raw values, display calibration, the do ubling 
under the cost-optimal rule) should be taken as supported; the differences of about one thousand USD 
among the calibrated queues are not. Review is a single step without queue dynamics or fatigue 
(Harchol-Balter, 2013), explanations are TreeSHAP with an independence assumption that correlated 
features violate (Aas et al., 2021; Slack et al., 2020), and costs are expected values with no regulatory or 
reputational terms. Finally, the reference list was checked against publisher and registry records, but a 
small number of fields (page spans for some articles, print years inferred from online -first dates, and 
some proceedings details) could not be confirmed and should be checked before citation. 
Further research 
Five directions follow. First, replay on real alert histories: a SOC's labelled alerts with their review 
outcomes, a randomised audit sample of unreviewed alerts and measured loss tiers would replace the 
generators, and would test whether the orderings found here survive selective labels (Lakkaraju et al., 
2017; Pendlebury et al., 2019). Second, studies with analysts, with the reliance measures of Table 13, to 
estimate the real effects of a calibrated probability, a flag and an explanation, including the cost of the 
extra review time ( Bansal et al., 2021 ; Buçinca et al., 2021 ). Third, stronger detectors and richer 
uncertainty: sequence models on raw event streams, one -class and anomaly detectors for unseen 
attacks, and uncertainty methods designed to separate epistemic from aleatoric terms, with conformal 
methods designed for shift and delayed labels (Gawlikowski et al., 2023; Angelopoulos & Bates, 2023; 
Gibbs & Candès, 2021). Fourth, decision models beyond a single review step: learning to defer, queues 
with fatigue and shift change, and optimisation that is trained on the decision itself and not on 
prediction error ( Madras et al., 2018 ; Mozannar & Sontag, 2020 ; Elmachtoub & Grigas, 2022 ). Fifth, 
explanations under adversaries and dependence: attacks on attributions, and attribution methods that 
respect correlated features (Slack et al., 2020; Aas et al., 2021). 
Conclusion 
This study examined, in a transparent simulation, whether calibrating alert scores, adding uncertainty 
signals and attaching explanations help prioritisation under limited analyst capacity. Four conclusions 
are supported within the limits of the testbed. A  detector that ranks well can still be badly calibrated, 
and a one-parameter post-hoc calibrator repaired most of the error, with daily recalibration holding it 
under drift and parametric methods being safer with few labels. Calibration did not change a ranking 
by probability, but it raised the value of a queue by 17% when probability was multiplied by asset loss 
and about doubled the value of a cost -optimal review rule, which is the sense in which it matters. 
Uncertainty signals were less useful than they appear: conformal sets were valid and mostly
