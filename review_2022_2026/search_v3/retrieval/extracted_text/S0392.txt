An Integrated Machine Learning Framework for Adaptive Cybersecurity Threat Detection 
 
Maniru Malami Umar1, Sadiq Aliyu Ahmad2, Bello Alhaji Buhari3 and Asma’u Shehu4 
 
Abstract 
The rapid growth of digital technologies, cloud computing, Internet of Things (IoT), and 
interconnected networks has significantly increased the frequency and sophistication of cyber 
threats. Traditional cybersecurity solutions that rely on signature -based detection mechanisms are 
increasingly ineffective against advanced persistent threats (APTs), zero -day attacks, ransomware, 
and polymorphic malware. However, individual machine learning (ML) models have limitations, 
including high false -positive rates, li mited adaptability and difficulty detecting previously unseen 
attacks. This paper proposes an Integrated Adaptive Cybersecurity Framework (IACF) that 
combines supervised learning (Random Forest, XGBoost, SVM), unsupervised anomaly detection 
(Isolation Forest, k-means, Autoencoder) and deep learning (CNN, LSTM) within a seven -layer 
architecture that also incorporates threat intelligence integration, concept -drift-based adaptive 
learning, automated response and explainable AI (SHAP, LIME). The framework was evaluated on 
four benchmark datasets ; CIC-IDS2017, UNSW -NB15, NSL -KDD and CIC -DDoS2019 using 
accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score and false positive rate (FPR). Detection accuracy ranged from 
98.87% (UNSW-NB15) to 99.85% (CIC-DDoS2019), with corresponding FPR between 0.12% and 
0.42%. Under controlled drift -injection experiments, the framework recovered detection accuracy 
to between 97.6% and 98.9% after concept, feature and adversarial drift. Explainability evaluation 
with a small panel of security analysts (n = 15) indicated a 43.8% increase in reported trust and a 
46% reduction in alert investigation time when SHAP/LIME explanations were provided, and the 
pipeline processed 1,000 packets in approximately 422 ms on the reported hardware con figuration. 
The principal contribution of the study is the integration of supervised, unsupervised and deep -
learning detection with concept -drift adaptation and explainability within a single closed -loop 
architecture, and an empirical demonstration of its performance on standard intrusion -detection 
benchmarks.

1.0 Introduction 
The rapid expansion of digital transformation 
technologies such as cloud com puting, Internet 
of Things (IoT), edge computing, and 5G 
networks has significantly increased the attack 
surface of modern cyber infrastructures ( Alaba 
et al., 2017; Zeadally et al., 2020). As 
organizations become more interconnected , 
cyber threats have evolved in complexity, scale, 
and sophistication, targeting both enterprise and 
critical national infrastructures. 
Traditional cybersecurity systems rely heavily 
on signature -based and rule -based detection 
mechanisms, which are effective only against 
previously known threats. However, they are 
largely ineffective in detecting zero-day attacks, 
polymorphic malware, and Advanced Persistent 
Threats (APTs) (Sommer and Paxson, 2010). 
These limitations have necessitated the adoption 
of intelligent, data-driven approaches capable of 
learning and adapting to evolving attack 
patterns. 
Machine Learning (ML) and Artificial 
Intelligence (AI) techniques have emerged as 
promising solutions for enhancing cybersecurity 
resilience by enabling automated detection, 
classification, and prediction of cyber threats 
(Buczak and Guven, 2016; Liu and Lang, 2019). 
Supervised learning algorithms such as Random 
Forest, Support Vector Machines (SVM), and 
Gradient Boosting Machines have demonstrated 
high effectiveness in intrusion detection tasks 
when trained on labelled datasets (Shone et al., 
2018). Simila rly, unsupervised learning 
techniques such as clustering and anomaly 
detection methods are widely used to identify 
previously unseen attack patterns (Chandola et 
al., 2009). 
Despite these advancements, single -model 
machine learning approaches are often limited 
by high false -positive rates, dataset imbalance, 
lack of generalisation, and inability to adapt 
dynamically to evolving threats (Ring et al., 
2019). To address these challenges, hybrid 
machine learning frameworks have been 
proposed to integrate multiple learning 
paradigms, thereby improving robustness, 
accuracy, and adaptability in cybersecurity 
systems (Ahmed et al., 2016). 
Beyond the technical limitations of individual 
models, cybersecurity itself has become a 
critical concern in the digital age due to the 
increasing frequency and sophistication of 
cyberattacks targeting individuals, enterprises, 
and governments. Modern cyber threats include 
ransomware, phishing, botnets, insider threats, 
and Advanced Persistent Threats (APTs), all of 
which are capable of bypassing traditional 
security defences (Verizon, 2023). Conventional 
Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) rely on 
predefined signatures and heuristics to identify 
malicious activities; while effective for known 
threats, these systems struggle to detect novel or 
evolving attack vectors, particularly those that 
exhibit low and slow attack patterns designed to 
evade detection (Sommer and Paxson, 2010), 
leaving organisations to face significant 
challenges in maintaining real -time situational 
awareness and proactive defence capabilities. 
The exponential growth of network traffic and 
security logs further complicates manual threat 
analysis, as Security Operations Centres (SOCs) 
are often overwhelmed by the volume of alerts 
generated by traditional systems, leading to alert 
fatigue and an in creased risk of missed threats 
(ENISA, 20 22). This highlights the need for 
automated, intelligent, and scalable 
cybersecurity solutions. Although ML -based 
IDS solutions have shown improved detection 
performance, they still face challenges such as 
concept drift, adversarial attacks, and a lack of 
explainability (Biggio and Roli, 2018), and

many ML models degrade in performance when 
deployed in real -world dynamic environments 
due to changes in attack patterns over time 
(Gama et al., 2014). There is, therefore, a critical 
need for an adaptive cybersecurity framework 
that combines multiple mach ine learning 
techniques, incorporates continuous learning 
mechanisms, and integrates real -time threat

against evolving cyber threats. 
1.1 Research Gap and Contribution 
As the literature review in Section 2 shows, 
several recent studies already combine two or 
more of these elements individually: hybrid 
ensembles of supervised and unsupervised 
learners (Mills et al., 2024), drift -adaptive 
frameworks such as HDDAF (Lara-Gutierrez et al., 
2025), and explainability-augmented ensembles such 
as the Consensus Hybrid Ensemble Model (Ahmed et 
al., 2025). What remains absent from this literature is 
a single architecture that combines supervised, 
unsupervised and deep-learning detection, concept-
drift-based continuous adaptation, threat-intelligence 
enrichment, automated response and explainable AI 
within one closed-loop pipeline, and that reports all 
seven capabilities under a common experimental 
protocol on the same benchmark datasets. The 
contribution of this study is accordingly 
integrative rather than purely algorithmic: it 
proposes and empirically evaluates such an 
architecture (IACF), and reports, under one protocol, 
its detection performance, its behaviour unde r 
simulated concept, feature and adversarial drift, 
and the effect of SHAP/LIME explanations on 
analyst-reported trust and investigation time.  
This study therefore proposes an Integrated 
Machine Learning Framework for Adaptive 
Cybersecurity Management and Intelligent 
Threat Detection that integrates supervised 
learning, unsupervised anomaly detection, deep 
learning models, and adaptive feedback 
mechanisms to enhance real -time threat 
detection and response capabilities. The aim of 
the study is to develop such a framework, 
capable of detecting both known and unknown 
cyber threats while continuously adapting to 
emerging attack patterns. Specifically, the study 
sets out to analyse the limitations of traditional 
cybersecurity management systems; investigate 
machine learning techniques used in intelligent 
threat detection; design an integrated machine 
learning architecture combining supervised, 
unsupervised, and deep learning models; 
develop an adaptive learning mechanism for 
continuous threat intelligence updates; evaluate 
the effectiveness of the framework using 
standard cybersecurity performance metrics; and 
propose automated response mechanisms for 
real-time cybersecurity management. 
2.0 Literature Review 
2.1 Machine Learning in Cybersecurity 
Machine learning has become a cornerstone of 
modern cybersecurity research due to its ability 
to analyse large-scale datasets and detect hidden 
patterns in network traffic (Buczak and Guven, 
2016). ML -based intrusion detection systems 
(IDS) are generally categorised into supervised, 
unsupervised, and semi -supervised learning 
approaches. 
Supervised learning techniques require labelled 
datasets and are widely used for classification 
tasks. Algorithms such as Random Forest, 
Support Vector Machines (SVM), k -Nearest 
Neighbours (k-NN), and Gradient Boosting have 
demonstrated strong performance in detecting 
known cyber threats (Shone et al., 2018). 
However, their effectiveness is highly dependent 
on the quality and availability of labelled data. 
Unsupervised learning techniques are used to 
detect anomalies in unlabelled datasets. Methods 
such as k -means clustering, DBSCAN, and 
Isolation Forest are commonly used to identify 
deviations from normal behaviour (Chandola et 
al., 2009). These techniques are particularly 
useful for detecting zero -day attacks and 
unknown threats.  Deep learning approaches 
have further enhanced cybersecurity capabilities 
by automatically extracting high -level features 
from raw data. Convolutional Neural Networks

(CNNs), Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), 
and Long Short -Term Memory (LSTM) 
networks have been applied successfully in 
intrusion detection and malware classification tasks 
(Kim et al., 2020). Despite their effectiveness, deep 
learning models often require large datasets and 
significant computational resources. 
2.2 Hybrid Machine Learning Models 
Hybrid machine learning models combine 
multiple learning techniques to overcome the 
limitations of individual approaches. Ensemble 
methods such as bagging, boosting, and stacking 
improve detection accuracy and reduce variance 
in predictions (Dietterich, 2000). 
Recent studies have shown that hybrid IDS 
frameworks integrating supervised and 
unsupervised learning outperform standalone 
models in terms of accuracy and robustness 
(Ahmed et al., 2016; Ring et al., 2019). For 
example, combining anomaly detection with 
classification models allows systems to detect 
both known and unknown threats effectively.  
Furthermore, the integration of deep learning 
with traditional machine learning techniques has 
shown significant improvements in detection 
performance, especially in high-dimensional network 
environments (Shone et al., 2018). Hybrid approaches 
also support better generalisation and adaptability in 
dynamic cybersecurity environments where attack 
patterns continuously evolve. 
However, challenges such as model interpretability, 
computational overhead, and adversarial 
manipulation remain major concerns in h ybrid 
ML-based cybersecurity systems (Biggio and Roli, 
2018). These limitations highlight the need for more 
adaptive, explainable, and resilient hybrid 
frameworks capable of operating in real-time security 
environments. 
2.3 Machine Learning in Intrusion Detection 
Systems 
Intrusion detection systems (IDS) have evolved 
significantly from signature-based approaches to 
machine learning -driven solutions. Early ML -
based IDS relied on supervised learning 
techniques including Support Vector Machines 
(SVM), Decision Trees (DT), and Random 
Forests (RF), achieving moderate success in 
detecting known attack patterns. Surveys of 
intrusion detection systems have documented 
the progression from single -classifier 
approaches to more sophisticated ensemble and 
hybrid methods. 
A comprehensive survey by researchers in 2024 
examined machine learning strategies and 
considerations in intrusion detection, highlighting the 
growing complexity of attack vectors and the 
corresponding need for more sophisticated 
detection mechanisms. The survey identified 
key challenges including data imbalance, feature 
selection, and the need for real -time processing 
capabilities. Similarly, studies focusing on deep 
learning-based intrusion detection in IoT 
environments have emphasised the importance 
of lightweight architectures capable of operating 
on resource-constrained devices. 
2.4 Ensemble and Hybrid Approaches 
The limitations of single-model approaches have 
driven significant research into ensemble and 
hybrid methods. Mills et al. (2024) presented a 
hybrid intrusion detection system combining 
supervised and unsupervised learning models 
through an ensemble stacki ng model. Their 
framework integrated a multilayer perceptron 
neural network, a modified self-organising map, and 
a decision tree, achieving detection accuracies of 
99.84% on NSL -KDD and 99.90% on CIC -
DDoS2019 datasets. However, the system showed 
limitations in detecting infrequent attack types such as 
User-to-Root (U2R) attacks, with an F1 score of 
only 62.5%. 
Sharma et al. (2024) proposed an evolutionary 
machine learning algorithm for intrusion 
detection, employing genetic algorithm -based 
feature selection with a hybrid DT -SVM 
classifier. Their approach was validated on BoT-
IoT and UNSW -NB15 datasets, demonstra ting 
effective adaptability to new threats and 
scalability across diverse network environments.

The integration of evolutionary optimisation 
with traditional classifiers represents a 
significant advancement in adaptive threat 
detection. 
The Consensus Hybrid Ensemble Model 
(CHEM) introduced by Ahmed et al. (2025)  
combined linear, nonlinear, and ensemble 
methods with neural networks and probabilistic 
models through a metaclassifier approach. The 
model employed random forest and decision tree 
as metaclassifiers in a voting classifier, using

various base classifiers. CHEM demonstrated 
impressive performance across multiple attack

The integration of SHAP and LIME

2.5 Deep Learning for Threat Detection 
Deep learning has emerged as a powerful tool 
for cybersecurity applications. The SentinelAI -
IDS architecture, introduced in 2025, leverages 
enhanced Bayesian optimisation to optimise a 
random forest and XGBoost ensemble classifier. 
This deep learning -based hybrid intrusion 
detection architecture demonstrated multi -
dataset training capability on CICIDS_2017, 
UNSW_NB15, and NSL -KDD datasets, 
achieving 97.10% accuracy and a 95.50% F1 -
score. The integration of mutual information -
based feature selection with pri ncipal 
component analysis significantly reduced 
dimensionality while maintaining detection 
performance. 
SmartTrust, a hybrid deep learning framework 
proposed for cloud environments, integrates  
CNN architectures within a Zero -Trust 
Architecture framework for real -time threat 
detection. The framework addresses insider 
threats, privilege escalation, and data breaches 
through continuous verification and adaptive 
security policies.  The GA -CNN-BiGRU-IDS 
framework, introduced in late 2025, addresses 
class imbalance through advanced data 
augmentation techniques combined with a 
hybrid CNN -BiGRU model for spatiotempora l 
feature extraction. This approach overcomes 
limitations of deep learning-based IDS related to 
acute class imbalance and the difficulty of 
capturing discriminative spatiotemporal patterns 
in complex network traffic. 
2.6 Adaptive Frameworks and Drift Management 
The challenge of maintaining model performance 
over time has driven research into adaptive 
frameworks. The Hybrid Drift Detection and 
Adaptation Framework (HDDAF), introduced by 
Lara-Gutierrez et al. (2025), provides a multi-layered 
AI system specifically designed to mitigate concept 
drift, feature drift, and adversarial attacks. HDDAF 
integrates Hoeffding drift detection, feature 
selection, adversarial training, and incremental 
learning, dynamically adapting through a Mixed -
Drift Handling Module that balances fine-tuning 
and full retraining. On the CIC-IDS2017 dataset, 
HDDAF achieved a macro F1 score above 99%. 
An adaptive security framework for multi -
environment networks using ensemble data drift 
detection and incremental deep learning was 
proposed i n 2025. This framework employs an 
ensemble data drift detection mechanism combining 
ADaptive WINdowing and autoencoders, enabling 
adaptive model updates. A deep neural network 
is incrementally retrained only when data drift is 
detected, ensuring adaptability to evolving attacks 
while conserving computational resources. The 
framework incorporates replay -based memory, 
regularisation, and an interpolation mechanism to 
avoid catastrophic forgetting. Experimental results 
demonstrated mean accuracy of 0.999 while 
maintaining low memory usage of 
approximately 32.1 MB. 
The ADAPT algorithm, introduced in 2025, 
employs a novel pseudo -labelling semi -
supervised approach to combat concept drift in 
malware detection. This model-agnostic method 
can be applied to various machine learning 
models, paving the way for more effective  
adaptation to concept drift.

2.7 Explainable AI in Cybersecurity 
The integration of explainable AI (XAI) 
techniques has become increasingly important in 
cybersecurity applications. A 2024 study 
presented a novel method for XAI -integrated 
hybrid ensemble learning for improving real -
time anomaly detection in network data.  The 
model combined Random Forest, deep learning 
(CNN, Bi -LSTM), and boosting techniques, 
achieving accuracy rates of 99.98 % on CICIDS 
2017 and 97.71  % on UNSW -NB15 datasets 
with false positive rates as low as 0.02% and 
0.01 %. XAI techniques rendered model 
decisions in clear and understandable ways, 
encouraging confidence and comprehension 
among cybersecurity specialists. 
MetaStack-APTNet, an ensemble deep learning 
framework for APT detection, incorporated 
SHapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) for 
model interpretability. The framework achieved 
98.92 % accuracy with detection and mitigation 
times of 200 ms and 700 ms respectively. 
2.8 Addressing Data Imbalance and Zero -
Day Threats 
Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have 
been employed to address class imbalance in 
cybersecurity datasets. An innovative intrusion 
detection framework using GAN -augmented 
Deep Ensemble Neural Network for cross -
domain IoT -cloud security was proposed in  
2025. The framework combined multi -source 
learning architecture with GAN -based 
oversampling to address class imbalance, 
achieving 97.22 % overall accuracy. 
Zero-day attack detection remains a critical 
research area. Studies have explored 
autoencoder-based anomaly detection for 
identifying zero-day attacks in unseen data. The 
SKT-IDS framework, based on Sigmoid Kernel 
Transformation and encoder -decoder 
architecture, demonstrated effective zero -day 
attack detection by training solely on known 
traffic using cosine similarity loss. Research 
employing Microsoft Azure Auto Machine 
Learning has also explored automated 
approaches to zero-day threat mitigation.

3.3 Proposed Framework Architecture 
The proposed Integrated Adaptive Cybersecurity 
Framework (IACF) consists of seven major layers, 
organised as a closed -loop security ecosystem 
capable of continuous improvement, as 
illustrated in Figure 1. 
 
Figure 1: Architecture of the Proposed Integrated Adaptive Cybersecurity Framework (IACF). Each layer 
of the architecture performs a distinct function within the overall detection and response pipeline 
3.3.1 Data Collection (Layer 1): Raw security 
data is continuously ingested from heterogeneous 
sources, including network traffic, firewalls, routers 
and switches, endpoint devices, cloud infrastructure, 
SIEM/log repositories, and external threat intelligence 
feeds. This layer establishes broad visibility across the 
monitored environment. 
3.3.2  Data Preprocessing (Layer 2): Collected 
data is cleaned, and relevant features are 
extracted and selected. Numerical attributes are 
normalised, and class imbalance is corrected 
through data balancing, producing a consistent, 
model-ready dataset for the detection engine. 
3.3.3  Integrated Detection Engine (Layer 3): 
Preprocessed data is analysed in parallel by three 
complementary modules: a supervised learning 
module (Random Forest, XGBoost, and SVM) 
that classifies known attack patterns; an 
unsupervised learning module (Isolation Forest, 
k-means clustering, and Autoencoder) that flags 
anomalous behaviour indicative of previously 
unseen threats; and a deep learning module 
(CNN for feature extraction an d LSTM for 
temporal analysis) that captures complex spatial 
and sequential patterns in traffic. The outputs of 
the three modules are combined by an Integrated 
Decision Fusion module using ensemble, voting, 
and stacking strategies to produce a final 
benign/malicious threat prediction. 
3.3.4  Threat Intelligence Integration (Layer 4): 
This layer maintains bidirectional communication 
with the detection engine, enriching predictions 
with global threat feeds, malware signatures, 
IP/domain reputation data, and vulnerability

databases, thereby improving detection context 
and reducing false alarms for emerging threats. 
3.3.5 Adaptive Learning Engine (Layer 5): 
Feedback from alerts, logs, and analyst

updated, affected models are retrained or fine -
tuned, and updated models are redeployed to the

3.3.6 Automated Response System (Layer 6): 
Confirmed threats are intended to trigger

3.3.7 Reporting and Monitoring Output

Together, these layers are designed to provide a 
closed-loop security ecosystem: detection

3.4 Evaluation Metrics 
The framework is evaluated using standard 
cybersecurity performance metrics, defined in 
terms of true positives (TP), true negatives (TN), 
false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN). 
Accuracy: the proportion of correctly classified 
instances out of all instances. 
Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN) (1) 
Precision: the proportion of true positives among 
all instances predicted as positive. 
Precision = TP / (TP + FP) (2) 
Recall (True Positive Rate): the proportion of 
actual positive instances correctly identified. 
Recall = TP / (TP + FN) (3) 
F1-Score: the harmonic mean of precision and 
recall, balancing both metrics. 
F1-Score = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + 
Recall) (4) 
False Positive Rate (FPR): the proportion of 
actual negative instances incorrectly classified 
as positive. 
FPR = FP / (FP + TN) (5) 
In addition to these classification metrics, 
Detection Time (the interval from data ingestion 
to threat identification) and Resource Utilisation 
(CPU, memory, and storage consumption) are 
recorded to assess the framework's suitability for 
real-time operation.

4.7 Discussion 
The results demonstrate that the integrated 
approach performs well against the limitations 
reported for single -model detection systems in 
prior studies. The integration of ensemble 
learning, deep learning, and unsupervised 
anomaly detection provides complementary 
detection capabilities; however, the present 
study did not include a formal ablation 
comparing the full IACF against each standalone 
component or sub-combination (Random Forest 
alone, the supervised module alone, supervised 
+ unsupervised, and so o n), so the claim that 
integration itself produced the observed 
accuracy cannot yet be attributed to integration 
specifically rather than to the individual strength 
of the underlying models. 
The controlled drift experiments indicate that the 
framework can recover detection performance 
after the introduction of the tested drift 
scenarios; however, long -term operational 
effectiveness requires validation using 
continuously evolving real-world traffic. 
The inclusion of XAI techniques appears to have 
enhanced operational utility in this small 
evaluation, with security analysts reporting 
greater understanding of and trust in automated 
detection decisions; a larger, statistically 
powered study would be needed to confirm this 
effect generalises. 
However, several limitations warrant 
acknowledgement. The computational 
requirements of the deep neural network 
component may preclude deployment on 
resource-constrained edge devices. The 
framework's performance on highly imbalanced 
datasets, while strong, shows some degradation 
for extremely rare attack types. In addition, 
results are reported from single experimental 
runs without standard deviations or confidence 
intervals across repeated runs, the analyst panel 
is small (n = 15) and was evaluated descriptively 
(Sections 3.7 and 4.4), the threat -intelligence,

5.0 Summary and Conclusion 
5.1 Summary 
This research presented an Integrated Adaptive 
Cybersecurity Framework (IACF) for intelligent 
threat detection and adaptive cybersecurity 
management. The framework integrates ensemble 
learning, deep neural networks, and unsupervised 
anomaly detection within a unified architecture, 
supported by adaptive drift management, intelligent 
response mechanisms (specified conceptually), and 
explainable AI capabilities. The integration of 
multiple learning paradigms, namely ensemble, deep 
learning, and unsupervised me thods, achieved 
accuracies of 98.87% –99.85% across the four 
benchmark datasets (99.42% on CIC -IDS2017, 
99.71% on NSL-KDD, 98.87% on UNSW-NB15 
and 99.85% on CIC -DDoS2019, all within this 
range); whether this reflects a genuine integration 
effect over single-model approaches, as opposed to 
the strength of the individual models used, remains to 
be confirmed by the ablation study noted in Section 
4.7. The framework further demonstrated the ability 
to detect and adapt to concept drift, feature drift, and 
adversarial attacks under the tested experimental 
conditions, with post-adaptation accuracy recovering 
to 97.6 % - 98.9 %; this indicates controlled adaptation 
rather than necessarily continuous learning under real-
world conditions. The incorporation of SHAP and 
LIME explanations enhanced the transparency of the 
system, with a small panel of security analysts (n = 15) 
reporting, in a small descriptive evaluation without a 
significance test, a 43.8% relative increase in trust and 
a 46 % reduction in investigation time, while the 
framework's measured processing time of 422 
milliseconds for 1,000 packets indicates 
potential suitability for real-time detection under 
the tested computational and traffic conditions; 
operational deployment requires further 
validation under realistic network loads. Finally, 
experimentation across four benchmark datasets 
provides empirical evidence for the framework's 
performance on standard intrusion -detection 
benchmarks, offering a basis for its further 
evaluation toward adoption in adaptiv e 
cybersecurity management. 
5.2 Conclusion  
The escalating sophistication of cyber threats 
demands intelligent, adaptive security solutions that 
transcend the limitations of traditional approaches. 
This research indicates that  integrated machine 
learning frameworks, combining multiple learning 
paradigms with adaptive mechanisms and 
explainability, offer a promising path  toward 
effective cybersecurity in complex, evolving 
environments. 
The IACF framework is designed to address the 
critical challenges identified in current literature: the 
limitations of single-model detection, performance 
degradation due to drift, high false positive rates, lack 
of interpretability, and class imbalance . By 
integrating complementary detection approaches, 
adaptive management, and transparent explanations, 
the framework provides an integrated architecture 
that contributes to ongoing work in intelligent 
cybersecurity. 
The empirical results support the framework's 
effectiveness on the benchmark datasets, with 
detection accuracies exceeding 99 % on three of the 
four datasets, false positive rates below 0.5 %, and 
successful adaptation to drift scenarios. These 
findings contribute to the growing body of evidence 
supporting integrated, adaptive approaches to 
cybersecurity. 
5.3 Recommendations 
Based on the findings of this research, the 
following recommendations are offered: 
For researchers, several directions merit further 
investigation. Model compression and pruning 
techniques should be explored to enable 
deployment on resource -constrained edge 
devices and IoT endpoints, while more 
sophisticated adversarial training and dete ction 

24                                      Researchers Journal of Science and Technology (2026) 6(9): 12 - 25 
 
mechanisms should be developed to counter 
increasingly sophisticated adversarial attacks. 
Federated learning approaches also warrant 
investigation as a means of enabling privacy -
preserving, distributed model training across 
multiple organisations. In addit ion, extended 
evaluations conducted over months or years 
would help to fully characterise drift patterns 
and long -term adaptation effectiveness, and 
closer collaboration with industry practitioners 
would help validate frameworks in operational 
environments and identify practical deployment 
challenges. 
For practitioners, it is recommended that 
integrated ML frameworks be implemented in 
phases, beginning with non -critical detection 
functions and gradually expanding to automated 
response capabilities. Workflows should be 
designed to leverage XAI explanatio ns in 
support of, rather than as a replacement for, 
human analysts, an approach reinforced by the 
descriptive improvements in trust and efficiency 
observed with XAI integration  in this study . 
Monitoring systems should also be established 
to track model performance over time and to 
trigger drift detection and retraining processes as 
needed. Because the framework's performance is 
ultimately limited by the quality and 
representativeness of trainin g data, investment 
in high-quality, labelled datasets is essential, as 
is testing frameworks across diverse network 
environments before production deployment, 
given the heterogeneity that characterises 
modern networks. 
For policymakers, the findings point to the need 
for standards and best practices governing the 
evaluation and certification of AI -based 
cybersecurity systems, alongside consideration 
of mandatory explainability requirements for AI 
systems used in critical  security applications. 
Initiatives that support secure data sharing 
would further enable collaborative training and 
validation of cybersecurity ML models, while 
sustained investment in workforce training 
programmes is needed to develop practitioners 
capable of designing, deploying, and operating 
AI-based cybersecurity systems. Finally, ethical 
guidelines for autonomous security systems 
should be developed to address issues of 
accountability, transparency, and bias. 
5.4 Future Work 
Building on the findings of this study, several 
avenues for future research are proposed. These 
include the integration of digital twin technology 
to support simulated threat environments and 
enhanced training, and the application of large 
language models for threat intelligence 
processing and natural language explanation 
generation. Further work should also pursue more 
sophisticated automated response capabilities, 
including adaptive policy enforcement and self -
healing mechanisms, as well as multi -agent 
reinforcement learning approaches for coordinated 
defence across distributed environments. Finally, 
specialised techniques for improved zero-day attack 
detection should be developed to address the 
limitations of current anomaly-based approaches.
