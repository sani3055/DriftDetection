ADAWU-IDS: a dynamic adaptive weight update mechanism
for concept drift-aware network intrusion detection systems
Jinliang Yuan 1 · Yu Yang 1 · Mingqi Wang 1
Received: 22 March 2026 / Accepted: 14 June 2026
© The Author(s) 2026
Abstract
Network intrusion detection systems deployed in dynamic environments are vulnerable to concept drift, where changes in
trafﬁc distributions can degrade detection performance over time. This paper proposes ADAWU-IDS, an interpretable and
validation-calibrated drift-response framework for concept drift-aware network intrusion detection. The framework combines
Multi-Scale Drift Index (MSDI)-based drift-severity estimation, Adaptive Drift-Aware Weight Updating (ADAWU), and
validation-selected response control within a chronological evaluation pipeline. Unlike ﬁxed-response adaptive designs,
ADAWU-IDS does not assume that stronger intervention is always beneﬁcial. Candidate response conﬁgurations are calibrated
on a held-out validation stream and then ﬁxed before ﬁnal chronological testing. MSDI is used as an auxiliary drift-severity
signal rather than as a standalone binary drift detector, while ADAWU adjusts ensemble contributions according to recent
predictive behavior, drift severity, and temporal relevance. Under a chronological CICIDS2017 protocol, the validation-
selected conﬁguration, denoted as ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated, shows modest improvements over Static LSTM in Weighted F1
and Attack F1. However, these improvements are not statistically signiﬁcant at the p < 0.05 level, and the calibrated framework
does not consistently improve Attack Recall or reduce the false-negative rate. Classical online ensemble baselines, including
Dynamic Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, and Leveraging Bagging, achieve stronger raw predictive performance under the
same protocol. The ablation results show that mandatory hierarchical retraining can over-adapt to short-term chunk behavior
and harm attack detection. Therefore, hierarchical response is reformulated as an optional response action selected through
validation calibration. Overall, ADAWU-IDS provides a transparent and conﬁgurable framework for analyzing drift-response
behavior in intrusion detection, with practical value in drift-severity interpretation, response calibration, and component-level
diagnosis. The evaluation reports paired statistical tests, ablation analysis, segment-wise drift-window results, computational
cost, and drift-detection diagnostics. The implementation is available at: https://anonymous.4open.science/r/ADAWU-IDS-
800B.

Existing drift-aware learning methods can be broadly cat-
egorized into passive adaptation, active drift detection, and
adaptive ensemble learning (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thock-

models as new data arrive, but they may overﬁt tempo-
rary ﬂuctuations or noisy labels. Active methods monitor
performance or distributional indicators and trigger adapta-
tion when a change is detected, but their effectiveness often
depends on threshold choices and the detectability of the drift
event (Zhang et al. 2026; Abdulrahaman et al. 2026). Adap-
tive ensembles, such as Dynamic Weighted Majority, Online
Bagging, and related online learning methods, adjust the
inﬂuence of base learners over time and have shown strong
performance under non-stationary data streams (Swarnalatha
et al. 2025; Thockchom et al.2023;Y ue ta l .2024). Neverthe-
less, many adaptive IDS studies still face three limitations.
First, drift is often characterized using a single indicator,
such as prediction error, window-level statistics, or a one-
dimensional distributional distance. Such indicators may be
insufﬁcient for describing heterogeneous changes in network
trafﬁc, where feature distributions, class-conditional struc-
tures, and attack composition may evolve differently (Cai
et al. 2025; Chu et al. 2023). Second, ensemble adaptation is
frequently driven mainly by recent predictive performance,
while the severity of distributional change and temporal
relevance of models are not always explicitly represented
(Cai et al. 2025;Y ue ta l . 2024; Chen et al. 2025). Third,
many adaptive systems use ﬁxed response rules once drift is
detected. However, unconditional or overly aggressive adap-
tation can be harmful when short-term chunk labels are noisy,
imbalanced, or not representative of future trafﬁc. Therefore,
response control should be calibrated rather than assumed to
be universally beneﬁcial.
To address these issues, this paper proposes ADAWU-
IDS, an interpretable and validation-calibrated drift-response
framework for concept drift-aware network intrusion detec-
tion. The motivation of ADAWU-IDS is not to replace
strong accuracy-oriented online ensemble classiﬁers such as
Dynamic Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, or Leverag-
ing Bagging. These methods are highly effective when the
primary objective is raw predictive performance under non-
stationary streams. However, in practical intrusion detection
deployment, prediction accuracy alone is often insufﬁcient.
Security operators may also need to understand whether a
performance change is associated with distributional drift,
how severe the drift is, whether an adaptive response should
be triggered, and whether stronger intervention may actually
harm later detection due to noisy labels, short-term imbal-
ance, or non-representative attack patterns.
From this perspective, ADAWU-IDS is designed to
address a complementary deployment need: transparent
and conﬁgurable drift-response analysis. The framework
combines a Multi-Scale Drift Index (MSDI), an Adaptive
Drift-Aware Weight Update mechanism (ADAWU), and a
validation-selected response policy within a chronological
IDS evaluation pipeline. MSDI summarizes feature-group
and class-conditional distributional changes as an inter-
pretable drift-severity signal. ADAWU adjusts ensemble
contributions by jointly considering recent predictive behav-
ior, drift-severity information, and temporal relevance. The
response policy is not treated as a ﬁxed or mandatory inter-
vention; instead, candidate conﬁgurations are calibrated on a
held-out chronological validation stream before ﬁnal testing.
This design allows the framework to examine when adap-
tive components are useful, when they are harmful, and how
response actions should be selected before deployment.
The practical value of ADAWU-IDS therefore lies in its
diagnostic and calibration-oriented role rather than in claim-
ing universal predictive superiority. Strong online ensemble
baselines may achieve better attack-detection metrics in some
settings, especially when the objective is minimizing false
negatives or maximizing aggregate F1 scores. ADAWU-
IDS, by contrast, provides additional information about drift
severity, model temporal relevance, component interaction,
and response-policy sensitivity. Such information is useful
in deployment scenarios where interpretability, auditabil-
ity, response control, and failure-case analysis are important
alongside predictive performance.
The methodological novelty of ADAWU-IDS lies in the
explicit coupling of drift-severity estimation, adaptive weight
control, and validation-selected response policy in a tempo-
rally ordered IDS pipeline. First, unlike methods that rely
mainly on prediction error or a binary drift alarm, MSDI pro-
vides a severity-oriented view of distributional deviation by
integrating feature-level and class-conditional information.
Second, unlike conventional online ensemble methods that
primarily update learners according to recent classiﬁcation
performance, ADAWU incorporates drift severity and tem-

response-policy selection interact under chronological IDS
evaluation. These results suggest that ADAWU-IDS is most
useful in deployment settings where drift interpretation,
response calibration, and component-level diagnosis are
required in addition to raw predictive accuracy.

2 Related work
This section reviews related studies from four perspectives:
static network intrusion detection, concept drift detection and
adaptation, adaptive ensemble learning for non-stationary
streams, and recent distribution-shift-aware anomaly detec-
tion studies. The purpose is to clarify the methodological
context of ADAWU-IDS and to distinguish the proposed
framework from prior adaptive weighting and drift-response
approaches.
2.1 Static network intrusion detection
Conventional NIDS predominantly rely on signature-based
detection, employing pattern matching against established
attack proﬁles. Although highly effective for identifying
documented threats, these systems are inherently vulnera-
ble to zero-day exploits and rapidly evolving attack variants.
To mitigate this vulnerability, machine learning approaches
have been widely adopted to model normal and malicious
trafﬁc patterns from historical data. Traditional algorithms,
such as Support V ector Machines, decision trees, and ran-
dom forests, have demonstrated considerable efﬁcacy in
NIDS applications (Chinnasamy et al. 2025; Hozouri et al.
2025). Furthermore, deep learning architectures, notably
Long Short-Term Memory networks and Convolutional Neu-
ral Networks, have shown strong capability in extracting
complex temporal and spatial dependencies within network
trafﬁc (Hore et al. 2024; Bamber et al. 2025; Hochreiter and
Schmidhuber 1997).
Despite these advancements, the majority of existing
methods are constrained by a “train-once-deploy” paradigm
predicated on the assumption of stationary data distributions
(Shyaa et al. 2024; Seth et al. 2024; Cai et al. 2025). In real-
world network environments, this assumption is frequently
violated, inevitably leading to severe model degradation as
underlying data concepts drift over time (Lara-Gutierrez et al.
2025; Luo et al. 2024; Jemili et al. 2024).

2.2 Concept drift detection and adaptation
Concept drift refers to changes in the underlying data distri-
bution over time, which may degrade the predictive perfor-
mance of models trained on historical data (Lara-Gutierrez
et al. 2025; Abdulrahaman et al. 2026; Cai et al. 2024).
Passive adaptation methods continuously update the learn-
ing model without explicit drift detection mechanisms,
typically employing sliding windows or instance weighting
techniques (Jemili et al. 2024). While computationally efﬁ-
cient, these approaches are prone to overﬁtting temporary
data ﬂuctuations and noise during stable periods.
Active detection methods explicitly monitor performance
or data distributions to trigger model updates only when
a change is conﬁrmed (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Mahdi
et al. 2024;H ue ta l . 2025). Representative algorithms, such
as the Drift Detection Method (DDM), Early Drift Detec-
tion Method (EDDM), and Adaptive Windowing (ADWIN),
attempt to balance stability and adaptability (Gama et al.
2004; Baena-Garcıa et al. 2006; Bifet and Gavalda 2007).
However, they frequently struggle with signiﬁcant detection
delays and extreme sensitivity to predeﬁned thresholds (Cai
et al. 2024).
Ensemble learning approaches maintain a dynamic com-
bination of multiple base classifiers, adjusting their influence
through weight updates (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thock-
chom et al. 2023;Y ue ta l . 2024). Algorithms like Dynamic
Weighted Majority (DWM) and Online Bagging have demon-
strated robust performance in non-stationary environments.
Nevertheless, a major limitation of current ensemble tech-
niques is their reliance on oversimpliﬁed weight update
mechanisms—often based exclusively on recent predictive
accuracy—thereby neglecting critical factors such as spe-
ciﬁc drift characteristics and historical model staleness (Cai
et al. 2025; Guerrero Cano et al. 2026; Chen et al. 2025).
More recent advances have explored model-internal drift
detection via parameter monitoring and probabilistic frame-
works for distinguishing real drift from virtual drift (Mahdi
et al. 2024). While theoretically promising, these meth-
ods currently lack seamless integration with comprehensive
drift quantiﬁcation metrics and adaptive ensemble structures
(Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Cai et al. 2025; Guerrero Cano
et al. 2026).
2.3 Adaptive ensemble learning under
non-stationary data
Adaptive ensemble learning is a major direction for han-
dling non-stationary data streams. Instead of relying on a
single static classiﬁer, ensemble methods maintain multi-
ple base learners and adjust their contribution over time.
Dynamic Weighted Majority (DWM) reduces the inﬂuence
of poorly performing classiﬁers and can add or remove learn-
ers when the stream changes. Online Bagging approximates
bootstrap aggregation in an online setting, while Leveraging
Bagging strengthens diversity through resampling and drift-
aware updating. These methods are widely used because they
provide strong predictive performance and efﬁcient online
adaptation under many non-stationary settings.
However, classical adaptive ensembles usually update
model weights mainly according to recent prediction errors
or online classiﬁcation performance. This design is effective
for accuracy-oriented adaptation, but it may provide limited
interpretability regarding why a speciﬁc response is triggered
and how drift severity affects model behavior. In IDS applica-
tions, such interpretability is important because operational
decisions often need to distinguish between mild trafﬁc ﬂuc-
tuation, severe attack-distribution shift, and temporary class
imbalance.
Another challenge is response control. Stronger interven-
tions, such as retraining or aggressive model replacement,
are not always beneﬁcial. If the current chunk contains noisy
labels, short-term imbalance, or a non-representative attack
pattern, unconditional adaptation may overﬁt the current
chunk and harm later detection. Therefore, adaptive IDS
frameworks should not assume that all response components
are always beneﬁcial. Instead, response actions should be
calibrated on validation data and evaluated through ablation
analysis, statistical testing, and failure-case discussion.
2.4 Drift-aware and dynamic learning frameworks
for NIDS
In the speciﬁc context of NIDS, streaming learning algo-
rithms have been deployed to process sequential data without
retaining extensive historical logs (Jin et al. 2024; Singh
et al. 2024). For instance, Hoeffding Trees and their vari-
ants provide efﬁcient incremental learning capabilities but
generally exhibit poor autonomous drift identiﬁcation. To
overcome this, hybrid systems that couple explicit drift detec-
tion with incremental updates have emerged (Cai et al. 2025;
Jemili et al. 2024; Chu et al. 2023). Y et, these systems
predominantly rely on uniform, “one-size-ﬁts-all” response
strategies, executing identical adaptation procedures regard-
less of the actual severity or type of the detected drift
(Lara-Gutierrez et al.2025; Zhang et al.2026; Guerrero Cano
et al. 2026).
The proposed ADAWU-IDS framework is designed to
address these limitations from a calibration-oriented per-
spective. MSDI summarizes drift severity using feature-level
and class-conditional information. ADAWU updates ensem-
ble weights using recent predictive behavior, drift-severity
information, and temporal relevance. More importantly, the
response policy is not treated as an always-active hierarchi-
cal mechanism. Instead, candidate response conﬁgurations
are selected on a held-out validation stream and then ﬁxed
123

before ﬁnal chronological testing. This design distinguishes
ADAWU-IDS from methods that assume a ﬁxed response
action for all detected drift events (Thein et al.2024; Nassred-
dine et al. 2025; Chen et al. 2024).
2.5 Recent distribution-shift-aware anomaly
detection studies
Recent studies outside network intrusion detection also high-
light the importance of robustness under complex deploy-
ment environments, limited labeled data, and domain shift.
In railway arc detection, multimodal imitation learning has
been used to combine visible-light and infrared features,
transferable feature learning, and optimal-transport-based
representation alignment to improve robustness under glare,
reﬂection, weather interference, and small-target variation
(Y an et al. 2025a0. Other multimodal learning studies have
introduced distribution constraints and knowledge distilla-
tion to improve robustness and computational efﬁciency
under uncertain degradation and long-tail data distributions
(Y an et al.2024).
Limited-data anomaly detection has also attracted increas-
ing attention. A DDPM-based multimodal arc detection
framework uses self-supervised pretraining on unlabeled
data and audiovisual semantic cues to improve detection
under sparse labeled samples (Y an et al. 2025b). However,
the same study also shows that practical deployment may be
affected by high computational cost, multimodal hardware
dependency, and limited interpretability. These concerns are
relevant to adaptive IDS deployment, where adaptation cost,
data availability, and explanation of failure cases are critical.
Domain-shift-aware unsupervised anomaly detection pro-
vides another relevant perspective. Transformer and graph
convolution-based anomalous sound detection has been stud-
ied under domain shifts, where the model must distinguish
normal and anomalous distributions despite changes in oper-
ating conditions (Y an et al.2024). This line of work reinforces
the need for adaptive feature alignment, domain-aware mod-
eling, and explicit evaluation under shifted conditions.
Although these studies are not designed for NIDS, they
reﬂect a broader trend in anomaly detection: robustness
should be evaluated not only by aggregate predictive accu-
racy, but also by adaptation behavior, calibration strategy,
computational overhead, and failure modes under distribu-
tion shift. This motivates the positioning of ADAWU-IDS
as an interpretable and validation-calibrated drift-response
framework rather than a universally superior classiﬁer.
2.6 Research gap and positioning of ADAWU-IDS
The above review suggests that existing adaptive and drift-
aware methods have made important progress, but several
gaps remain for concept drift-aware intrusion detection. First,
drift signals are often treated as binary triggers rather than
severity-oriented diagnostic information. Second, ensem-
ble weight updates are frequently driven mainly by recent
predictive performance and do not explicitly combine drift
severity and temporal relevance. Third, response actions such
as retraining are often predeﬁned, although their effective-
ness may depend strongly on the dataset, attack distribution,
and validation behavior. Fourth, many studies report aggre-
gate performance without sufﬁcient statistical validation,
component-interaction analysis, false-negative discussion, or
computational trade-off analysis.
ADAWU-IDS is proposed to address these gaps in a trans-
parent and testable manner. Its novelty does not lie in claim-
ing universal predictive superiority over all adaptive ensem-
bles. Instead, the contribution lies in coupling MSDI-based
drift-severity estimation, ADAWU-based dynamic weight
control, and validation-selected response policy within a
chronological IDS evaluation pipeline. This formulation
allows the framework to expose when adaptive components
are useful, when they are harmful, and how response actions
should be calibrated before ﬁnal testing.
3 Methodology
In this section, we present the proposed ADAWU-IDS
framework. We ﬁrst formulate intrusion detection under
non-stationary trafﬁc as a temporally ordered classiﬁcation
problem. We then describe the LSTM-based detection back-
bone and introduce the main methodological components
of the framework: the Multi-Scale Drift Index (MSDI), the
Adaptive Drift-Aware Weight Update (ADAWU) mecha-
nism, and a validation-selected response policy. Finally, we
summarize how these components are integrated into a uni-
ﬁed dynamic ensemble learning process.
3.1 Problem formulation
Network intrusion detection in real-world environments is
inherently non-stationary (Seth et al. 2024; Lara-Gutierrez
et al. 2025). As network services, user behavior, and attack
strategies evolve over time, the joint distribution of trafﬁc
features and labels may change, causing predictive models
trained on historical data to degrade after deployment (Luo
et al. 2024; Cai et al. 2025; Chu et al. 2023). This prob-
lem is particularly important for intrusion detection systems,
where delayed adaptation can lead to a substantial loss of
detection capability under newly emerging trafﬁc conditions
(Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Haddane et al. 2026).
In this work, we consider a temporally ordered intru-
sion detection setting in which network trafﬁc arrives as
a sequence of consecutive data chunks, D

The objective of this study is to design an adaptive intru-

training stage and an incoming sequence of temporal chunks,
the framework should: (1) provide an interpretable signal for
characterizing distributional changes, (2) adjust the contri-

behavior and drift-severity information, and (3) evaluate
whether validation-calibrated adaptation can mitigate perfor-

assuming universal superiority over all adaptive baselines.
To achieve this objective, we propose ADAWU-IDS,
a drift-aware adaptive ensemble framework that combines
explicit drift-severity estimation, dynamic weight adjust-
ment, and validation-selected response control. The frame-
work is intended to support transparent drift-response behav-
ior under evolving trafﬁc distributions rather than to replace
all existing online ensemble methods in raw predictive per-
formance.
3.2 Base LSTM detection model
As the predictive backbone of the proposed framework, we
adopt Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks to model
temporal dependencies in network trafﬁc sequences (Hore
et al. 2024; Hochreiter and Schmidhuber 1997; Devendi-
ran and Turukmane 2024). LSTM is suitable for this task
because intrusion patterns often evolve across consecutive
packets or ﬂow observations rather than appearing as iso-
lated feature values (Bamber et al. 2025; Hochreiter and
Schmidhuber 1997). Compared with purely static classiﬁers,
recurrent architectures are better suited to capturing short-
term temporal structure in sequential trafﬁc data.
For each input sample, a sequence of length L with fea-
ture dimension d is fed into the LSTM model. The hidden
representation is updated recurrently over time through the
standard gating mechanism, including the forget gate, input
gate, output gate, cell state, and hidden state. The ﬁnal hid-
den representation is then passed to a classiﬁcation head to
produce the predicted trafﬁc label.
In the current implementation, each base learner uses a
two-layer LSTM architecture followed by normalization,
regularization, and a ﬁnal fully connected classiﬁer. Con-
cretely, the model consists of a ﬁrst LSTM layer with 64
hidden units and sequence output, a second LSTM layer with
32 hidden units and ﬁnal-state output, a dense layer with 64
neurons and ReLU activation, a dropout layer with dropout
rate 0.2, batch normalization layers for training stabilization,
and a two-neuron softmax output layer. Model training uses
the Adam optimizer with sparse categorical cross-entropy
loss.
This design serves two purposes. First, it provides a
temporally aware detection backbone suitable for sequence-
based intrusion analysis. Second, it enables adaptive ensem-
ble control, where multiple LSTM-based learners trained
under different initializations can contribute unequally as the
trafﬁc distribution evolves (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thock-
chom et al. 2023; Nassreddine et al. 2025).
3.3 Multi-scale drift index (MSDI)
A key challenge in adaptive intrusion detection is that distri-
butional change may appear in multiple forms (Mahdi et al.
2024). Drift may affect low-level feature distributions, class-
conditional structure, or the overall relationship between
benign and malicious trafﬁc. Relying on a single indica-
tor may therefore provide an incomplete view of temporal
change. To address this issue, we introduce the Multi-Scale
Drift Index (MSDI), which quantiﬁes distributional change
from complementary perspectives and provides a structured
signal for adaptation (Hu et al. 2025).
It is important to clarify that MSDI is not treated as
a complete standalone drift detector. Instead, it is used
as a drift-severity signal that supports adaptive weight-
ing and response-policy calibration. Its diagnostic value is
assessed empirically in the drift-detection analysis rather
than assumed a priori.
In practical deployment, MSDI should be interpreted as
an auxiliary severity indicator rather than as an autonomous
alarm module. A high MSDI value indicates that the current
trafﬁc chunk differs from the reference state in feature-level
or class-conditional structure, but it does not by itself prove
that the predictive decision boundary has changed. Therefore,
MSDI is used to modulate ensemble weights and to sup-
port response-policy calibration, while operational decisions
should also consider predictive performance, false-negative
behavior, computational constraints, and analyst-deﬁned risk
tolerance.

3.5 Validation-selected response policy
In non-stationary intrusion detection, not all drift events
require the same response (Lara-Gutierrez et al. 2025; Zhang
et al. 2026; Guerrero Cano et al. 2026). Some changes are
mild and may be handled through lightweight reweight-
ing, whereas stronger distributional shifts may require more
substantial intervention (Jamshidi et al. 2025). However,
applying stronger intervention unconditionally can also be
harmful. If the current chunk contains noisy labels, short-
term imbalance, or a non-representative attack pattern,
aggressive retraining may over-adapt to that chunk and
degrade attack detection in later segments.
For this reason, the ADAWU-IDS framework does not
treat hierarchical response as an always-active core module.
Instead, response behavior is formulated as a validation-
selected policy. A response policy deﬁnes how the framework
reacts to the current drift-severity score and adaptation
context. Candidate policies may include: 1. weight-only
adaptation, where ADAWU updates ensemble weights with-
out retraining; 2. mild response, where only lightweight
weight adaptation is applied; 3. moderate response, where
localized incremental retraining or targeted parameter refresh
may be enabled; 4. severe response, where stronger interven-
tion may be enabled when the validation stream indicates that
such intervention is beneﬁcial.
During calibration, candidate conﬁgurations differ in the
weighting coefﬁcients, drift thresholds, temporal decay set-
ting, minimum weight constraint, and whether stronger
response actions are enabled. Each candidate conﬁguration
is evaluated only on the held-out validation stream. The ﬁnal
conﬁguration is selected according to an IDS-oriented val-
idation score that emphasizes Attack F1 and Attack Recall
while penalizing false negatives. The exact calibration proto-
col and the selected conﬁguration are reported in Section5.4.
After validation, the selected response policy is ﬁxed
before ﬁnal testing. No information from the ﬁnal test
stream is used to select thresholds, weighting coefﬁcients,
or response actions. This design is intended to prevent test
leakage and to avoid treating stronger intervention as uni-
versally beneﬁcial. Under the CICIDS2017 protocol used
in this study, the validation-selected conﬁguration retains
MSDI-guided dynamic weighting but disables hierarchical
retraining. Therefore, hierarchical response is best under-
stood as an optional candidate response action rather than
a mandatory component of ADAWU-IDS.
3.6 Dynamic ensemble learning framework
The complete ADAWU-IDS framework integrates LSTM-
based base learners, MSDI-based drift-severity estimation,
ADAWU-based dynamic weight updating, and validation-
selected response control into a uniﬁed online learning
process (Thockchom et al. 2023).
At initialization, the framework trains K diverse LSTM-
based learners from the available historical data and assigns
them initial ensemble weights. During online operation, the
system processes temporally ordered chunks sequentially
(Jemili et al. 2024;H ue ta l .2025). For each incoming chunk

4 System design and implementation
This section describes the implementation pipeline of ADA-
WU-IDS. The system is designed as a temporally ordered
drift-response framework, in which prediction is performed
before any update on the current chunk. This protocol
avoids using current labels before prediction and better
reﬂects online deployment conditions. The framework con-
sists of ﬁve main stages: data preprocessing and sequence
construction, initial ensemble training, online prediction,
drift-severity estimation and dynamic weight updating, and
validation-selected response control.
4.1 Architecture overview
The proposed ADAWU-IDS framework is implemented as a
chronological drift-response pipeline. Unlike a static deploy-
ment setting, in which a trained model is directly applied to
all future trafﬁc, ADAWU-IDS processes incoming trafﬁc
chunks sequentially and updates its adaptive state only after
predictions for the current chunk have been produced. This
design is important for avoiding test-label leakage and for
reﬂecting a realistic online evaluation setting.
Figure 1 summarizes the chronological workﬂow of
ADAWU-IDS. Each incoming trafﬁc chunk is ﬁrst processed
and evaluated using the current ensemble state before its
labels are observed. After prediction, delayed labels become
available and are used for online evaluation, MSDI-supported
drift-severity analysis, and ADAWU-based weight updating.
Any stronger response action is applied only when selected
through validation calibration. The updated ensemble and
reference state are then carried forward to the next chunk.

4.3 Model training module
The training module initializes the predictive backbone of
ADAWU-IDS before online adaptation begins. The current
implementation follows a two-stage procedure.
First, the system constructs LSTM-based base learners
using the architecture described in Section 3.2. Each model
is initialized independently so that the ensemble contains
diversiﬁed learners rather than identical copies of the same
network instance (Swarnalatha et al. 2025; Thockchom et al.
2023; Nassreddine et al. 2025). In the training code, multi-
ple learners are created with different random seeds, which
improves the potential usefulness of later adaptive reweight-
ing.
Second, the initialized learners are trained on the same
initialization-stage data under a consistent optimization pro-
tocol. During ensemble initialization, the framework uses
bootstrap-like resampling to provide each learner with a
slightly different training view, further increasing ensemble
diversity. No validation or test data are used during ini-
tial training. The held-out validation stream is reserved for
response-policy calibration.
In the current experimental pipeline, the ensemble con-
tains three base learners with uniform initial weights. The
purpose of this design is not to maximize architectural hetero-
geneity, but to provide a controlled setting in which weight
adaptation can be examined clearly. After training is com-
pleted, the initialization-stage data are also used to establish
the initial reference state for later drift detection.
This module therefore provides the ofﬂine foundation of
ADAWU-IDS: a set of LSTM-based learners that share the
same general architecture and task objective, but retain suf-
ﬁcient diversity to support adaptive ensemble control during
online deployment.
4.4 Online prediction and update protocol
The online adaptation module is the central operational
component of ADAWU-IDS. It combines drift monitoring,
adaptive weight control, and applies the validation-selected
response policy ﬁxed before ﬁnal testing within a chunk-
based chronological evaluation setting.
The module contains three interconnected components:
Drift monitoring component: The component combines
MSDI with complementary performance-based and distribu-
tion-based signals (Hu et al. 2025; Mahdi et al. 2024). In the
current code structure, drift evidence may arise from three
sources: predictive degradation on the current chunk, distri-
butional deviation from the reference state, and the MSDI
score itself. These signals are aggregated into a conﬁdence-
aware decision that determines whether the current chunk
shows meaningful distributional deviation from the recent
reference state. This component is used for drift-severity
monitoring and response support, rather than as a standalone
binary drift detector.
Weight updater: The weight updater executes ADAWU to
rebalance the contribution of ensemble members over time
(Thockchom et al. 2023;Y ue ta l . 2024; Chen et al. 2024).
For each base learner, the update considers recent predictive
behavior, the current drift score, adaptation conﬁdence, and a
temporal decay factor. The resulting weights are normalized
and constrained by a minimum weight threshold to avoid
model starvation. This mechanism allows the ensemble to
adapt continuously without forcing abrupt model switching
in every window.
Response-control module: The response-control mod-
ule applies the response policy selected on the held-out
validation stream. In the calibrated CICIDS2017 conﬁgura-
tion, this policy relies on MSDI-guided dynamic weighting
and disables hierarchical retraining. Stronger intervention is
therefore treated as an optional candidate action rather than
a mandatory response.
Operationally, the online adaptation module processes each
incoming chunk in sequence. It first obtains the current predic-
tion result, then estimates drift, updates ensemble weights,
determines drift severity, and ﬁnally applies stronger adap-
tation only when necessary. This staged design is impor-
tant because it separates routine monitoring and reweighting
from higher-cost adaptation actions, thereby supporting the

deployment-oriented trade-off between robustness and efﬁ-
ciency.
4.5 Drift-severity estimation and dynamic weight
updating
After each chunk is evaluated, MSDI is computed to summa-
rize the degree of distributional change between the current
chunk and the reference state. As described in Section 3.3,
MSDI combines feature-level and class-conditional informa-
tion to provide a structured drift-severity signal. The resulting
value is not treated as a perfect drift detector; rather, it is
used as diagnostic information for adaptive weighting and
response control.
The ADAWU module then updates the ensemble weights.
Learners with stronger recent predictive behavior retain
greater inﬂuence, while learners that become less reliable
under changing trafﬁc conditions are downweighted. The
weight update also incorporates drift-severity information
and temporal relevance, allowing the ensemble to adapt grad-
ually instead of switching abruptly between models.
To avoid learner starvation, a minimum weight constraint
is used before normalization. This prevents any base learner
from being completely eliminated due to temporary degra-
dation and allows it to regain inﬂuence if its later predictive
behavior improves. All experiments were conducted on a
machine equipped with 16 vCPU Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold
6430 CPU and RTX 4090(24GB) GPU.
4.6 Validation-selected response control
The response-control module determines whether the system
should rely only on dynamic weighting or apply stronger
adaptation actions. In the proposed implementation, response
behavior is not ﬁxed a priori. Candidate conﬁgurations are
evaluated on a held-out validation stream, and the selected
conﬁguration is ﬁxed before ﬁnal testing.
This design is important because stronger response actions,
such as retraining or aggressive model intervention, may be
harmful when the current chunk is noisy, highly imbalanced,
or not representative of future trafﬁc. Therefore, ADAWU-
IDS treats hierarchical response as an optional candidate
action rather than a mandatory core module.
During ﬁnal testing, the system applies only the validation-
selected response policy. No information from the ﬁnal test
stream is used to choose thresholds, weighting coefﬁcients,
or response actions. Under the CICIDS2017 protocol used
in this study, the selected conﬁguration retains MSDI-guided
dynamic weighting and disables hierarchical retraining. The
ﬁnal test-time conﬁguration is denoted as ADAWU-IDS-
Calibrated in the experimental sections. This name refers to
the validation-selected instance of the ADAWU-IDS frame-
work, rather than to a separate model architecture.
4.7 Implementation and reproducibility
considerations
The implementation is organized to ensure that training, vali-
dation, and test operations remain separated. Initial training is
performed only on the designated training stream. V alidation
data are used only for calibration of response conﬁguration
and adaptive-control parameters. Final test segments are used
only after the conﬁguration has been ﬁxed.
All adaptive methods in the ﬁnal evaluation follow the
same chronological protocol: the model ﬁrst predicts the cur-
rent chunk, and only then uses the current labels for updating.
This protocol is applied consistently to ADAWU-IDS and the
online ensemble baselines to support fair comparison.
For reproducibility, the experimental section reports the
chronological data split, the evaluated baselines, the selected
validation conﬁguration, repeated-run results, statistical tests,
ablation settings, segment-wise performance, drift-detection
diagnostics, and computational cost. These components are
reported to clarify not only the predictive behavior of
ADAWU-IDS, but also its calibration behavior, component
interactions, and deployment trade-offs.
5 Experimental design
This section describes the experimental protocol used to eval-
uate ADAWU-IDS under temporally ordered concept drift
conditions. The evaluation is designed to avoid information
leakage between training, validation, and test streams. All
adaptive methods follow the same online protocol: each tem-
poral chunk is ﬁrst predicted using the current model state,
and the labels of that chunk are used only after prediction for
model updating or adaptation. CICIDS2017 is used as the
main chronological evaluation dataset, while UNSW-NB15
is retained as a secondary dataset for cross-dataset general-
ization analysis.
5.1 Dataset and chronological partitioning
The experiments are conducted on two widely used intru-
sion detection datasets: CICIDS2017 and UNSW-NB15.
CICIDS2017 contains benign and attack trafﬁc collected
over multiple days, making it suitable for evaluating con-
cept drift-aware intrusion detection under temporally ordered
conditions. UNSW-NB15 is used as an additional dataset
to examine whether the proposed framework generalizes
beyond CICIDS2017 and to assess dataset-speciﬁc limita-
tions.
For CICIDS2017, this study adopts a strictly chrono-
logical partitioning strategy instead of random train-test
splitting. Tuesday and Wednesday are used for initial train-
ing. Thursday WebAttacks is used as the validation stream for
123

hyperparameter calibration and response-policy selection.
Thursday Inﬁltration, Friday Morning, and Friday PortScan

This chronological design has two purposes. First, it pre-
serves the temporal order of trafﬁc evolution and prevents
information leakage caused by random shufﬂing. Second,
it allows the evaluation to examine whether an adaptive
framework calibrated on a held-out validation stream can
generalize to later unseen trafﬁc and attack segments.
UNSW-NB15 is retained as a secondary cross-dataset
generalization case. Unlike CICIDS2017, it does not pro-
vide the same multi-day chronological structure for con-

the UNSW-NB15 results are interpreted as robustness and
generalization evidence rather than as the primary validation-

dataset-speciﬁc calibration when transferred to trafﬁc distri-

The preprocessing procedure follows the same gen-

are removed when appropriate, categorical attributes are
encoded, missing or invalid values are handled, and numer-

from the training portion. For sequence-based LSTM input,
trafﬁc records are transformed into ﬁxed-length temporal

Because this study focuses on preventing test leakage and
evaluating validation-calibrated adaptation under chronolog-

segment-wise drift-window analysis, drift-detection diag-

discussed as a secondary generalization case, especially in
relation to dataset-speciﬁc calibration and the limitations of
ADAWU-IDS under different trafﬁc distributions.

The following baselines and compared methods are evaluated
under the same chronological protocol whenever applicable.
Static LSTM is used as the non-adaptive baseline. It is
trained on the initial training stream and then directly eval-

baseline measures the degradation of a static temporal clas-

ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated denotes the validation-selected

instance of ADAWU-IDS rather than to a separate model
architecture.
Full default ADAWU denotes the full conﬁguration in
which MSDI, dynamic weighting, and hierarchical response
are all enabled according to the initial design. This conﬁgura-
tion is included in the ablation study to examine whether the
complete uncalibrated response mechanism is always bene-
ﬁcial.
DWM, Online Bagging, and Leveraging Bagging are
included as classical online ensemble baselines for non-

the same prediction-before-update protocol as ADAWU-

included to provide a fair comparison with established adap-

Additional ablation variants are evaluated to analyze com-

without MSDI, without dynamic weighting, and without

For UNSW-NB15, the comparison follows the dataset
protocol used for the secondary generalization experiment.
Because UNSW-NB15 does not provide the same chrono-

interpreted cautiously and are not used to claim universal
cross-dataset superiority.
5.3 Online evaluation protocol and metrics
All adaptive methods in the CICIDS2017 experiment are
evaluated using a chunk-based online protocol. For each
incoming test chunk, the method ﬁrst predicts the labels of the
current chunk using the model state available before observ-
ing the current labels. After prediction, the true labels of the
chunk are revealed and used for evaluation, weight updating,
and any response action allowed by the method.
This prediction-before-update order is applied consis-
tently to ADAWU-IDS and all adaptive baselines. It prevents
the model from using labels from the current chunk before
making predictions on that same chunk. Therefore, the
reported results reﬂect online adaptation behavior rather than
retrospective optimization.
For ADAWU-IDS, MSDI is computed after prediction
using the current chunk and the reference state. ADAWU then
updates ensemble weights for subsequent chunks. If stronger
response actions are enabled by the selected policy, they are
applied only after the current chunk has already been eval-
uated. In the ﬁnal calibrated conﬁguration selected in this
study, hierarchical retraining is disabled and the framework
relies on MSDI-guided dynamic weighting.

5.5 Ablation study design, statistical validation,
and reporting structure
To examine the contribution and interaction of the proposed
components, an ablation study is conducted in addition to
the main chronological comparison. The ablation study com-
pares the validation-selected ADAWU-IDS conﬁguration
with the full default ADAWU conﬁguration and component-
removal variants. Speciﬁcally, the evaluated variants include
ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated, Full default ADAWU, w/o MSDI,
w/o Dynamic Weighting, and w/o Hierarchical Response.
The purpose of the ablation study is not only to determine
whether each component improves performance individu-
ally, but also to identify harmful component interactions. This
is important because adaptive mechanisms may not always be
beneﬁcial under non-stationary and imbalanced IDS streams.
In particular, stronger response actions such as hierarchical
retraining may over-adapt to short-term chunk labels when
the current chunk is noisy, imbalanced, or not representative
of later trafﬁc. Therefore, the ablation study is used to evalu-
ate whether the full response mechanism should be retained
as an always-active module or treated as a candidate response
action selected through validation calibration.
To assess variability, the main CICIDS2017 experiments
are repeated across three random seeds. The reported results
are based on the mean performance across these runs.
In addition, paired statistical tests are performed between
ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated and Static LSTM on the main IDS-
sensitive metrics. A p-value threshold of 0.05 is used. If the
paired comparison does not reach this threshold, the result is
interpreted as an improvement trend rather than statistically
signiﬁcant superiority.
This design follows a conservative interpretation strat-
egy. The purpose is not to overstate the performance of
ADAWU-IDS, but to determine whether the validation-
calibrated framework provides reliable improvement over
static deployment, how its components interact, and how it
compares with established online ensemble baselines.

make the chronological evaluation protocol transparent and

6.4 Ablation study and component interaction
Table 6 presents the ablation study used to examine the
contribution and interaction of the main ADAWU-IDS com-
ponents. The evaluated conﬁgurations include the validation-
calibrated conﬁguration, the full default ADAWU con-
ﬁguration, and variants without MSDI, without dynamic
weighting, and without hierarchical response.
The most important observation is that the full default
ADAWU conﬁguration performs poorly. Its Weighted F1 is
0.6967, Attack Recall is 0.0001, Attack F1 is 0.0002, and
FNR is 0.9999. This indicates that the mandatory hierarchical
response in the default conﬁguration can severely harm attack
detection under the current chronological CICIDS2017 set-
ting.
This result reveals an important component interaction
issue. Stronger intervention is not necessarily beneﬁcial
when applied unconditionally. If hierarchical retraining or
aggressive response is triggered under short-term chunk
labels that are imbalanced, noisy, or not representative of
future trafﬁc, the model may over-adapt to the current chunk
and lose its ability to detect attack trafﬁc in later segments.
Therefore, hierarchical response should not be treated as an
always-active core component.
The validation-calibrated conﬁguration avoids this failure
by disabling hierarchical retraining and retaining the weight-
only attack-sensitive conﬁguration. This result supports the
design choice of selecting response actions through valida-
tion calibration rather than assuming that stronger adaptation
is universally useful.
The ablation results also show that removing MSDI,
dynamic weighting, or hierarchical response produces results
close to the calibrated conﬁguration under the current
CICIDS2017 setting. This indicates that the marginal con-
tribution of MSDI and dynamic weighting is limited in this
particular protocol. Therefore, MSDI should be interpreted
as a drift-severity signal that supports transparent response
analysis rather than as a standalone guarantee of predictive
improvement.
Overall, the ablation study clariﬁes the role of the
ADAWU-IDS components. The main value of the frame-
work lies in exposing and controlling drift-response behavior
through validation calibration. The results do not support the
claim that every adaptive component is independently bene-
ﬁcial in all settings. Instead, they show that adaptive response
actions require careful calibration and that component inter-
actions can strongly affect IDS performance.

7 Discussion
This section discusses ADAWU-IDS from four deployment-
oriented perspectives: its practical motivation relative to
strong online ensemble baselines, the interpretation of MSDI
as a drift-severity signal, cross-dataset generalizability, and
deployment trade-offs involving false-negative risk, calibra-
tion dependence, and computational cost.
7.1 Practical motivation relative to strong online
ensemble baselines
The comparison with classical online ensemble baselines
clariﬁes the complementary role of ADAWU-IDS. Dynamic
Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, and Leveraging Bag-
ging remain stronger choices when the primary deployment
objective is raw predictive performance, especially Attack
Recall and false-negative reduction. ADAWU-IDS addresses
a different deployment need: transparent drift-response anal-
ysis, response-policy calibration, and component-level diag-
nosis under chronological trafﬁc evolution. These results
indicate that when the primary deployment goal is to max-
imize immediate predictive accuracy or minimize missed
attacks under limited computational cost, classical online
ensembles remain highly competitive choices.
The motivation of ADAWU-IDS is therefore comple-
mentary rather than purely competitive. Classical online
ensembles are effective because they update learner inﬂu-
ence or sample weighting according to recent prediction
behavior. However, their adaptation process is usually less
explicit in explaining whether a performance change is asso-
123

Journal of King Saud University Computer and Information Sciences (2026) 38:546 Page 19 of 23 546 
ciated with distributional drift, how severe the drift is, and
whether stronger intervention should be applied or avoided.
In practical IDS deployment, these questions can be impor-
tant because operators may need not only a prediction output,
but also diagnostic information about trafﬁc evolution, model
temporal relevance, and response-policy sensitivity.
ADAWU-IDS addresses this deployment need by cou-
pling drift-severity estimation, adaptive weight control, and
validation-selected response policy in a temporally ordered
IDS pipeline. MSDI provides an interpretable indication
of distributional pressure from feature-group and class-
conditional perspectives. ADAWU uses recent predictive
behavior, MSDI-based drift severity, and temporal relevance
to adjust ensemble contributions. The response policy is
selected on a held-out chronological validation stream rather
than being treated as a mandatory intervention. This design
makes it possible to examine when adaptive components are
useful, when they become harmful, and how response actions
should be calibrated before ﬁnal testing.
Thus, the practical value of ADAWU-IDS lies in trans-
parent drift-response analysis and deployment calibration. It
is most relevant when interpretability, auditability, response
control, and failure-case analysis are required alongside
predictive performance. This role complements accuracy-
oriented online ensembles rather than replacing them in
low-latency attack detection.
7.2 Practical interpretation and robustness of MSDI
MSDI is designed as an auxiliary drift-severity signal rather
than a standalone binary drift detector. Its purpose is to sum-
marize the magnitude of temporal distributional deviation
from complementary feature-group and class-conditional
perspectives, and to provide interpretable information for
downstream adaptive weighting and response-policy calibra-
tion. Therefore, a high MSDI value should not be interpreted
automatically as evidence that retraining is required, and a
low MSDI value does not necessarily imply that the classiﬁer
will remain reliable.
The behavior of MSDI depends on the type and severity of
the underlying drift. Under abrupt and distributionally sepa-
rable drift, feature-group and class-conditional deviations are
more likely to increase simultaneously, making MSDI easier
to interpret as a strong drift-severity signal. Under gradual
drift, the change may accumulate slowly across consecutive
chunks, and the MSDI trajectory may show a progressive
increase rather than an isolated peak. Under recurring drift,
MSDI may increase when the current trafﬁc distribution
departs from the recent reference window, even if a simi-
lar pattern appeared earlier in the stream. This reﬂects the
local-reference nature of the current implementation. Under
class-conditional drift, MSDI may be informative when the
feature structure of benign or malicious samples changes, but
its reliability depends on the availability and representative-
ness of class labels within each chunk.
MSDI and predictive degradation are not necessarily syn-
chronized. A high MSDI value with limited performance
loss may indicate covariate shift, benign trafﬁc-composition
changes, or distributional movement that does not substan-
tially affect the decision boundary. Conversely, a low or
moderate MSDI value with clear attack-detection degrada-
tion may occur when the drift mainly affects rare attack
patterns, minority-class behavior, or class boundaries that are
not fully captured by aggregate distributional summaries. For
this reason, MSDI should be interpreted together with IDS-
sensitive metrics such as Attack Recall, false-negative rate,
Attack F1, and segment-wise post-drift performance. Table8
summarizes the expected interpretation of MSDI under dif-
ferent drift scenarios.
In the proposed framework, MSDI is therefore used as
a response-support signal. It contributes drift-severity infor-
mation to ADAWU-based weight updating and helps analyze
when adaptive intervention may be useful. However, the ﬁnal
response policy is not triggered solely by MSDI. Candidate
response conﬁgurations are selected through chronological
validation calibration, and the selected conﬁguration is ﬁxed
before ﬁnal testing. This design reduces the risk of treating
every distributional deviation as requiring stronger interven-
tion, which is particularly important when short-term chunks
are noisy, imbalanced, or not representative of future traf-
ﬁc. Therefore, MSDI is most informative when distributional
deviation is aligned with observable feature-group or class-
conditional changes, but it should be interpreted together

7.3 Cross-dataset generalizability
and dataset-specific calibration
The UNSW-NB15 evaluation indicates that the effective-
ness of ADAWU-IDS is strongly affected by dataset-speciﬁc
distributional structure and calibration conditions. Unlike
CICIDS2017, which contains more temporally concentrated
attack periods and clearer trafﬁc-phase changes, UNSW-
NB15 presents a more mixed trafﬁc composition, different
attack-category distribution, and different feature-generation
process. As a result, a response policy calibrated under one
chronological trafﬁc stream should not be assumed to trans-
fer directly to another dataset. This observation is important
because it shows that drift-aware IDS frameworks require not
only adaptive mechanisms, but also dataset-aware calibration
before deployment.
A key reason is that distributional deviation and clas-
siﬁcation difﬁculty may not be aligned in the same way
across datasets. MSDI measures feature-group and class-
conditional distributional changes relative to a recent refer-
ence state. This signal can be informative when distributional
movement corresponds to meaningful changes in the clas-
siﬁcation problem. However, on UNSW-NB15, the trafﬁc
distribution may contain more overlapping attack and benign
patterns, more mixed attack categories, or less clearly sep-
arated temporal drift segments. In such cases, an increase
in MSDI may indicate distributional pressure without nec-
essarily identifying the changes that most affect the deci-
sion boundary. Conversely, security-critical degradation may
occur in minority attack patterns even when the aggregate
MSDI value remains moderate.
This mismatch helps explain why explicit drift-response
control may be less effective than classical online ensem-
ble adaptation on UNSW-NB15. Methods such as Dynamic
Weighted Majority, Online Bagging, and Leveraging Bag-
ging respond directly to recent prediction errors or resam-
pling-based online updates. When the main objective is
raw predictive performance, especially Attack Recall and
false-negative reduction, such error-driven mechanisms can
adapt more directly to the observed classiﬁcation behav-
ior. ADAWU-IDS, by contrast, introduces an additional
drift-severity signal and response-control mechanism. These
components provide diagnostic value, but they may also
amplify short-term imbalance, noisy chunk behavior, or non-
representative distributional changes when the calibration
stream does not adequately match the ﬁnal test stream.
The ablation behavior on UNSW-NB15 should therefore
be interpreted as evidence of dataset-dependent compo-
nent interaction. If removing MSDI or weakening adaptive
response improves performance on a speciﬁc dataset, this
does not necessarily mean that the component is always
invalid. Rather, it indicates that the beneﬁt of MSDI-based
weighting depends on whether the drift-severity signal is
aligned with attack-sensitive predictive degradation. When
this alignment is weak, simpler error-driven online ensembles
may provide stronger classiﬁcation performance. This ﬁnd-
ing supports a calibration-aware interpretation of ADAWU-
IDS and cautions against direct cross-dataset transfer without
reconﬁguration.
The UNSW-NB15 results also highlight the limitation of
the current calibration strategy. The selected response pol-
icy, drift threshold, and weighting parameters are calibrated
on a held-out chronological validation stream. This proce-
dure reduces overclaiming and prevents response actions
from being treated as automatically beneﬁcial. However, the
candidate search space remains limited, and the validation
stream may not cover all drift regimes or attack-composition
changes encountered in another dataset. Therefore, the
selected conﬁguration should be regarded as dataset-speciﬁc
rather than universally transferable.
These results suggest that ADAWU-IDS should not
be deployed on a new dataset by directly transferring
the CICIDS2017-calibrated conﬁguration; instead, dataset-
speciﬁc validation calibration is necessary. In practical
deployment, ADAWU-IDS should be recalibrated when
applied to a new network environment. Such recalibration
should consider not only aggregate metrics such as Accuracy
and Weighted F1, but also attack-sensitive objectives includ-
ing Attack Recall, false-negative rate, and Attack F1. Future
work should further investigate which dataset characteris-
tics make MSDI-based response control beneﬁcial, neutral,
or harmful. Controlled studies involving different attack dis-
tributions, temporal drift structures, class-imbalance levels,
and recurring or gradual drift patterns would help clarify
the conditions under which diagnostic drift-response frame-
works provide advantages beyond classical online ensemble
baselines.
7.4 Deployment trade-offs, limitations, and future
implications
Several limitations should be considered when interpreting
the results. First, ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated provides only
modest improvements over Static LSTM in selected F1-
based metrics, and these improvements are not statistically
signiﬁcant at the p < 0.05 level. More importantly for IDS
deployment, the calibrated framework does not consistently
improve Attack Recall or reduce the false-negative rate.
Since missed attacks are often more costly than benign mis-
classiﬁcation in security applications, in high-risk settings,
deployment decisions should prioritize Attack Recall and
false-negative reduction over aggregate F1 gains.

Journal of King Saud University Computer and Information Sciences (2026) 38:546 Page 21 of 23 546 
Second, adaptive response introduces additional com-
putational cost. The calibrated ADAWU-IDS conﬁguration
requires more processing time per chunk than the clas-
sical online ensemble baselines. This overhead is mainly
associated with multi-learner LSTM inference, MSDI com-
putation, adaptive weight updating, and response-control
logic. Therefore, in latency-sensitive environments where the
main requirement is fast and accurate classiﬁcation, simpler
online ensembles may be more suitable. Its added value is
strongest when operators require interpretable drift diagnosis
and calibrated response analysis.
Third, the validation-calibration strategy depends on the
representativeness of the validation stream. If the validation
period does not contain drift patterns similar to those encoun-
tered during ﬁnal deployment, the selected response policy
may be suboptimal. A limited candidate search space can
also restrict the achievable performance of the calibrated
conﬁguration. These limitations are especially relevant for
cross-dataset transfer, where trafﬁc composition, attack dis-
tribution, and temporal drift structure may differ substantially
from the calibration data.
Fourth, MSDI should be viewed as a complementary
severity indicator rather than a complete replacement for
performance-based monitoring. As discussed in Section 7.2,
MSDI may respond strongly to distributional changes that do
not affect classiﬁcation performance, and it may be less sen-
sitive to rare but security-critical decision-boundary changes.
Practical deployment should therefore combine MSDI with
attack-sensitive predictive metrics, uncertainty monitoring,
and failure-case inspection.
Future work should address these limitations in several
directions. First, larger repeated-run studies and broader
uncertainty analysis would improve conﬁdence in the obser-
ved trends. Second, controlled experiments involving grad-
ual, recurring, class-conditional, and rare-attack drift would
provide a more systematic assessment of MSDI robustness.
Third, stronger attack-sensitive calibration objectives could
be explored to prioritize Attack Recall and false-negative
reduction rather than aggregate F1 alone. Fourth, lightweight
backbones or incremental update mechanisms could reduce
computational cost. Finally, broader cross-dataset and real-
world streaming evaluations are needed to identify when
diagnostic drift-response frameworks such as ADAWU-IDS
provide practical beneﬁts beyond accuracy-oriented online
ensemble baselines.
8 Conclusion and future work
This study proposed ADAWU-IDS, an interpretable and
validation-calibrated drift-response framework for concept
drift-aware network intrusion detection. The framework
couples MSDI-based drift-severity estimation, ADAWU-
based dynamic ensemble weighting, and validation-selected
response control within a chronological IDS evaluation
pipeline. Unlike ﬁxed-response adaptive designs, ADAWU-
IDS does not assume that stronger intervention is always
beneﬁcial. Instead, candidate response conﬁgurations are cal-
ibrated on a held-out validation stream before ﬁnal chrono-
logical testing. The contribution of ADAWU-IDS is therefore
not a new standalone classiﬁer or a universally superior
online ensemble, but a calibrated framework that explicitly
links drift-severity estimation, adaptive weight control, and
response-policy selection under chronological IDS evalua-
tion.
The experimental results provide a cautious understand-
ing of the framework. Under the chronological CICIDS2017
protocol, ADAWU-IDS-Calibrated shows modest improve-
ments over Static LSTM in Weighted F1 and Attack F1.
However, these improvements are not statistically signiﬁcant
at the p < 0.05 level. The calibrated framework does not con-
sistently improve Attack Recall or reduce the false-negative
rate, and classical online ensemble baselines achieve stronger
raw predictive performance under the same chronological
protocol. These ﬁndings indicate that ADAWU-IDS should
not be interpreted as a universally superior IDS classiﬁer.
The ablation and diagnostic analyses further clarify the
role of each adaptive component. MSDI provides an inter-
pretable drift-severity signal that helps characterize distribu-
tional deviation, but it should not be used as a standalone
binary drift detector. ADAWU supports continuous ensem-
ble reweighting by incorporating recent predictive behavior,
drift-severity information, and temporal relevance. In con-
trast, stronger response actions such as retraining or model
refresh may over-adapt to short-term chunk behavior when
applied unconditionally. Therefore, hierarchical response is
better formulated as an optional response action selected
through validation calibration rather than as a mandatory
component.
Overall, the main value of ADAWU-IDS lies in its trans-
parent drift-response structure, validation-based conﬁgura-
bility, and interpretable adaptation behavior. The framework
provides a testable pipeline for examining when adaptive
components are useful, when they are harmful, and how
response actions should be selected before deployment. This
positioning is particularly relevant for intrusion detection
under non-stationary trafﬁc, where aggregate accuracy alone
is insufﬁcient and must be considered together with attack-
recall behavior, false-negative risk, computational cost, and
failure cases.
Future work will focus on four directions. First, stronger
attack-recall-oriented optimization is needed to reduce false
negatives under severe attack-distribution shifts. Second,
more efﬁcient online adaptation strategies should be explored
to reduce computational overhead in latency-sensitive deploy-
ment scenarios. Third, MSDI can be further improved by
123

incorporating more ﬁne-grained drift annotations and evalu-
ations under gradual, recurring, and class-conditional drift.
Fourth, broader validation across additional datasets, longer
trafﬁc streams, and real-world deployment environments is
required to assess the generalizability of validation-selected
drift-response policies.
