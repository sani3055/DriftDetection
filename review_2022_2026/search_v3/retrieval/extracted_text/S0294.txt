Abstract 
Operational deployment of machine -learning (ML) intrusion detection systems (IDS) in security operations centers 
(SOC) often generates large volumes of alerts that are difficult to justify, prioritize, and audit. Although explainable 
AI (XAI) techniques ar e increasingly used to interpret IDS predictions, most studies remain limited to post -hoc 
explanations and do not translate insights into analyst -facing operational diagnostics. This paper proposes 
Explainability as Operational Diagnostics , a SHAP -guided workflow that operationalizes explanations into two 
reusable artifacts: (1) a Failure Casebook  that captures recurring false -positive and false -negative patterns using 
explanation signatures and confidence cues, and (2) a Triage Rationale that maps attribution evidence and prediction 
confidence into transparent investigation priorities for SOC analysts. The workflow is evaluated using flow -based 
IDS data with 78 features and two supervised classifiers, XGBoost and Random Forest, applied to  an explainability 
subset of 1,000 instances per model. In the analyzed baseline run, both models achieve perfect separation (TN = 639, 
TP = 361, FP = 0, FN = 0), resulting in an empty failure casebook under this controlled condition. Rather than 
presenting this outcome as a final operational claim, we  frame it as baseline diagnostic validation and explain how 
the workflow would populate the casebook under realistic conditions, including class imbalance, noise, and 
distribution shift. Even without misclassifications, SHAP analyses reveal consistent domin ant flow -level drivers 
across models, providing a reproducible foundation for SOC justification and model auditing. Overall, this work 
advances XAI for cybersecurity from prediction-centric interpretation toward actionable operational diagnostics that 
support trustworthy ML deployment in high-risk SOC environments. 
 
Keywords : Explainable AI, Failure Analysis, Intrusion detection, Operational diagnostic, SHAP, SOC triage.

1. INTRODUCTION 
Machine learning (ML) -based Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) are increasingly commonly 
used to help Security Operations Centers (SOC) process high volumes of logs/flows and detect 
anomalous activities as well as attacks. However, improvements in model accuracy do not automatically 
translate to improvements in operational decision quality. In practice, incident analysis is influenced by 
alert load, triage time limitations, and the risk of misclassification, which directly impact incident 
response (for example, false positives trigger wasted investigations, while false negatives risk leaving 
attacks unaddressed). Therefore, the need for explainability in IDS is not merely about 'knowing 
important features,' but also about providing rationale that can be used for failure diagnostics and quick, 
targeted triage  [1], [2]. 
The XAI literature has developed rapidly, including the clustering of concepts, taxonomy, 
opportunities, and challenges of XAI to support model accountability [1], design frameworks and cross-
disciplinary evaluation of XAI systems [2], as well as comprehensive reviews of XAI methods across 
various domains [3]. In the realm of cybersecurity, surveys confirm that the need for interpretability 
arises from the dynamic nature of the problem, the presence of adversarial actors, and the demands for

implementations in cybersecurity still often stop at the level of descriptive “post-hoc explanations” (for 
example, global feature importance) and have not yet become operational mechanisms to guide the 
investigation of misclassification cases and facilitate evidence-based triage [4], [5], [10]. Recent open-
access studies also argue that explainability in intrusion detection should support forensic/operational

In IDS, the challenge of XAI is "two-sided": on one hand, it enhances transparency and user trust,

not properly managed [10]. In addition, the nature of IDS, which often faces class imbalance, changing

diagnostic tools rather than static artifacts [10], [12] . This reinforces the need for an operationally 
oriented explainability framework  namely, explainability that is directly connected to: (i) identifying 
model failure patterns, (ii) creating a replicable failure casebook, and (iii) justifying triage priorities 
based on confidence levels and risk [12], [13], [14]. 
Although explainable AI has been widely applied to intrusion detection, most implementations 
still stop at producing explanatory visualizations or feature rankings. In practice, SOC analysts need 
more than interpretability: they need explanations that can be operationalized into systematic failure 
diagnostics (to understand recurring false alarms and missed attacks) and triage decision support  (to 
justify priorities under time pressure). The lack of structured, reusable artifacts that connect explanation 
evidence to operational actions leaves a gap between XAI research outcomes and SOC workflows. 
Existing SHAP-based explainable IDS studies commonly focus on interpreting model predictions 
through feature importance rankings or local explanations for selected samples. In contrast, our work 
operationalizes explanations into reusable diagnostic artifacts namely the Failure Casebook and Triage 
Rationale that are explicitly designed for SOC workflows (failure forensics, prioritization, and audit -
friendly justification). This distinction is methodological (artifact formalization and confidence -aware 
routing) and operational (supporting repeatable analyst decision -making rather than one -off 
interpretability) [15], [16] . 
Based on this gap, this paper proposes the approach of Explainability as Operational Diagnostics, 
which utilizes SHAP to: (1) create a Failure Casebook containing archetypes of failure cases (FP/FN) 
and patterns of feature drivers of predictions, and (2) build a Triage Rationale that maps explainability 
outputs into operational reasons for prioritising investigations. The focus of this paper is to make 
explainability not just an interpretation, but a diagnostic tool and triage rationale that can be used by 
SOC analysts and IDS model management teams [17], [18]. The main contributions of this paper are as 
follows: 
1) Conceptual contribution (reframing XAI): We reframe explainability in intrusion detection as 
an operational diagnostic capability that targets analyst needs justification, failure forensics, 
and SOC triage decision support [10], [18]. 
2) Methodological contribution (reusable artifacts): We formalize two analyst-facing artifacts: a 
Failure Casebook that structures recurring misclassification archetypes (false positives/false

links attribution evidence and confidence to transparent prioritization rules [17], [18] 
3) Empirical contribution (baseline validation): We provide an initial validation on flow-based IDS 
artifacts (78 features) using XGBoost and RandomForest, reporting predictive metrics and 
showing that SHAP summaries yield consistent dominant drivers across models, while

2. METHOD 
2.1. General Design and Operational Objective 
The method is designed to address SOC operational needs: when an IDS generates predictions, 
analysts require two things: (i) why an instance is predicted as an attack/normal, and (ii) if a 
misclassification occurs, what the failure pattern is and how to pr ioritize handling it. This framework 
follows the XAI design principles that require a connection between the form of the explanation and the 
user's goal [2], [9]. 
2.2. Data, Experimental Settings, and Models 
This work is an explainability -focused extension of our prior IDS experimentation, with the 
primary objective of operationalizing explainability into reusable diagnostic artifacts (Failure Casebook 
and Triage Rationale). To ensure reproducibility, we build  upon a preserved experimental artifact that 
records the end -to-end workflow, including preprocessing routines, model training configuration, 
inference logs, and SHAP outputs. In this study, the underlying data are drawn from the publicly 
available CICIDS2018 benchmark, specifically the 02-14-2018 capture (02-14-2018.csv). After 
removing non-predictive identifiers (e.g., Timestamp) and the label column, we obtain 78 tabular 
network-flow features  commonly used in IDS research (e.g., dst_port, flow_duration, and 
packet-rate statistics such as flow_pkts/s and bwd_pkts/s). This representation is consistent with 
recent explainable NIDS work that uses SHAP not only to interpret predictions but also to support 
feature reasoning and computationally feasible deployment pipelines [20]. 
For detection baselines, we analyze explanations for two supervised classifiers XGBoost and 
RandomForest because both are operationally relevant for structured flow telemetry and are widely 
reported as competitive baselines in explainable IDS studies [15], [16]. The dataset is converted into a 
binary classification task (Benign = 0, Attack = 1). Dataset size and class distribution are reported in the 
Dataset Description and Provenance subsection, preprocessing and leakage prevention are described in 
the Preprocessing and Data Splitting subsection, and model configuration and software versions are 
detailed in the Model Configuration subsection. 
2.2.1 Dataset Description and Provenance 
This study uses a publicly available intrusion detection benchmark from the CICIDS2018 
collection. CICIDS2018 is a public benchmark released by the Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity 
(University of New Brunswick) and provides ground-truth labels for benign traffic and multiple attack 
scenarios. We focus on the 02 -14-2018 traffic capture (exported as 02 -14-2018.csv), which represents 
network-flow records generated from benign activities and multiple attack scenarios. Each record 
corresponds to a single agg regated flow described by tabular flow statistics, such as port information, 
duration, packet-rate variables, and window/segment indicators. 
The original dataset provides a categorical label per flow that distinguishes benign traffic from 
specific attack types. Following common practice in explainable IDS studies that evaluate binary 
detection pipelines, we convert the multi -class labels into a  binary target: Benign → 0 (normal) and

2.2.2 Preprocessing and Data Splitting 
We apply a consistent preprocessing pipeline prior to model training and evaluation to ensure data 
quality and reproducibility. First, we perform basic validity checks on flow features (e.g., verifying that 
duration- and rate-related attributes are well -defined). We then replace invalid numeric values with 
missing values and handle missing entries using median imputation , which is robust for skewed 
network-flow distributions and preserves the scale of continuous attributes. 
Because both XGBoost and RandomForest are tree -based models and are generally robust to 
feature scaling, normalization is not strictly required for predictive performance. However, to keep the 
preprocessing pipeline uniform across experiments and to stabilize probability outputs for downstream 
diagnostics, we apply min–max scaling to map features into a comparable numeric range. Importantly, 
to prevent data leakage, all preprocessing steps that learn statistics from data (imputation and scaling) 
are fitted on the training partition only and then applied to the held -out test partition using the learned 
parameters. 
We split the dataset into training and test partitions using a stratified hold-out split (80/20)  to 
preserve the attack/benign class ratio in both subsets, and we fix the random seed (random_state = 
42) for reproducibility. Model evaluation is conducted exclusively on the held -out test set defined by 
this split, while the explainability subset used for SHAP analysis is sampled from the held-out evaluation 
data as described in Section 2.3. 
All reported results are produced from this fixed preprocessing -and-splitting protocol, ensuring 
that performance metrics and explanation artifacts are derived from data that are not used during model 
fitting. 
2.2.3 Model Configuration (XGBoost and RandomForest) 
We train two supervised classifiers  XGBoost and RandomForest  as representative tree -based 
models that are widely adopted for tabular network -flow telemetry. XGBoost is implemented using 
XGBClassifier from the xgboost library (v3.1.3) and RandomForest is implemented using 
RandomForestClassifier from scikit -learn (v1. 6.1). To ensure reproducibility and consistent 
comparisons, we fix the random seed (random_state = 42) and enable parallel execution (n_jobs = −1) 
for both models. For XGBoost, we explicitly set eval_metric = 'logloss' and disable label encoding 
(use_label_encoder = False) to ensure stable binary classification behavior. Unless stated otherwise, all 
remaining hyperparameters follow the library default settings. This choice is intentional because the 
goal of this paper is to evaluate the diagnostic workflow (SHAP-guided explainability, triage rationale, 
and failure casebook construction) under a standard baseline configuration; hyperparameter tuning can 
be treated as an orthogonal optimization step in future work.

2.3. Subset for Explainability Analysis  
To keep the explainability workflow operationally feasible, SHAP computations are performed 
on a bounded subset of the held -out evaluation data. Specifically, we sample 1,000 instances for 
XGBoost and 1,000 instances for RandomForest. This design reflects realistic SOC constraints where 
explanation latency and analyst time are limited, making it impractical to generate local attributions for 
the entire test set in near real-time. 
Beyond computational efficiency, using a fixed-size subset improves reproducibility of diagnostic 
artifacts: the same subset size can be re -used across repeated runs, model variants, and operational 
environments, enabling consistent comparison of attributi on signatures and stability patterns. 
Importantly, this subset is used only for explanation and diagnostic artifact construction (Failure 
Casebook and Triage Rationale), not as a replacement for standard detection evaluation on the full test 
partition, which is reported separately. In the reported diagnostic run, the explainability subset contains 
639 benign and 361 attack instances (n = 1,000), reflecting the class composition of the held -out 
evaluation data from which it is sampled. 
This positioning is aligned with deployment -oriented IDS studies emphasizing that explanation 
components should remain tractable under real constraints (compute budget, time -to-decision, and 
analyst workload) while still supporting consistent diagnostics [12], [17], [21].  
2.4. Explainability engine: SHAP for local attribution 
SHAP is used as a post-hoc explanation to calculate the contribution of features to predictions at 
the instance level. This local explanation is important for diagnostic purposes because model errors are 
almost always case -specific and cannot be sufficient ly explained by global feature importance alone 
[17], [18]. In this paper: 
a) Global summaries are obtained by averaging the absolute SHAP values (mean |SHAP|) to map 
dominant features.

2.7. Triage Rationale: explainability mapping → operational decisions 
The Triage Rationale translates model outputs and SHAP evidence into a transparent investigation 
priority that can be applied consistently in SOC workflows. For each instance 𝑥, we use: (i) the predicted 
attack probability 𝑝(𝑦 = 1 ∣ 𝑥), (ii) the confidence score conf(𝑥) = max(𝑝(𝑦 = 1 ∣ 𝑥), 1 − 𝑝(𝑦 = 1 ∣
𝑥))(Section 2.5), and (iii) the local SHAP attribution signature (top contributing features and their 
directions). We define an alert as high-confidence when conf(𝑥) ≥ 𝜏, where 𝜏 is a configurable 
threshold (we use 𝜏 = 0.95 in this study as a conservative SOC -oriented setting). Based on the 
prediction outcome and confidence, we apply the following prioritization rules: 
1. High priority (critical FN): 𝑦true = 1, 𝑦pred = 0, and conf(𝑥) ≥ 𝜏. These are confidently missed 
attacks and represent the most operationally damaging cases. 
2. Medium priority (critical FP): 𝑦true = 0, 𝑦pred = 1, and conf(𝑥) ≥ 𝜏. These cases can drive alert 
fatigue and consume analyst time, especially when they occur repeatedly with similar attribution 
signatures. 
3. Low priority (borderline cases): conf(𝑥) < 𝜏. These represent uncertain predictions and are 
routed to lower-priority review or deferred investigation depending on SOC capacity. 
For each prioritized case, the top-𝑘 SHAP features provide an explanation “evidence bundle” that 
supports analyst justification (e.g., dominant port-level or flow-rate drivers) and can be re-used to guide 
mitigation actions such as threshold tuning, targeted retraining, or feature sanity checks. This scheme is 
consistent with the idea of integrating XAI for risk -oriented decision -making and cyber -risk 
management [13], [22] . In the SOC context, the ultimate goal is to reduce investigation workload 
without compromising the detection of critical incidents [12], [13]. 
The workflow begins with network -flow data represented by 78 flow -derived features and 
supervised IDS models (XGBoost and RandomForest) producing predicted labels and attack 
probabilities. A cost-aware explainability subset (1,000 instances per model) is u sed to compute post-
hoc SHAP attributions (global mean ∣ 𝑆𝐻𝐴𝑃 ∣and local per -instance explanations). Misclassifications 
are labeled as FP/FN and scored for criticality (“confidently wrong” errors). When FP/FN exist, 
attribution vectors can be clustered into stable archetypes to populate a Failure Casebook, which then 
supports a triage rationale mapping (high priority: high -confidence FN; medium: high-confidence FP; 
low: borderline cases) and operational outputs for analyst decision-making and model/data refinement.

4. DISCUSSIONS 
This study positions explainability not merely as an interpretability add-on, but as an operational 
diagnostic layer that helps SOC analysts justify and triage IDS outputs. The central contribution is the 
transformation of SHAP explanations into two reusab le artifacts: a Failure Casebook and a Triage 
Rationale. Conceptually, this bridges a recurring gap in explainable intrusion detection research, where 
explanations are often produced but rarely operationalized into structured decision-support tools. 
From an empirical perspective, the perfect separation observed on the analyzed explainability 
subset indicates that, under the evaluated condition, both XGBoost and RandomForest can distinguish 
the two classes without error. While such performance is uncom mon in realistic IDS deployments, it 
does not invalidate the diagnostic framework; rather, it establishes a baseline scenario where the 
casebook is empty by definition (no FP/FN to catalog) and where the diagnostic value shifts toward 
explanation-based aud iting and justification. In practical SOC settings, misclassifications typically 
emerge under class imbalance, evolving traffic patterns, noisy labels, or distribution shift. Under those 
conditions, the proposed workflow would populate the Failure Casebook by grouping FP/FN cases that 
share similar attribution signatures and confidence profiles, producing actionable “failure archetypes” 
that can guide mitigation (e.g., threshold adjustment, feature review, targeted retraining, or rule -based 
safeguards). 
Compared with prior SHAP-based explainable IDS studies that primarily report feature rankings 
or local explanations, this work emphasizes operational reuse: explanations are organized into artifacts 
that support triage transparency and repeated analyst use. This reframing aligns with broader Informatics 
and Computer Science directions in trustworthy AI, where explainability is expected to support human 
decision-making, auditability, and system -level reliability in high -risk domains. Future work should 
extend the evaluation to larger and more diverse datasets, include stress tests under drift/imbalance, and 
report populated failure archetypes to quantify how the casebook improves troubleshooting efficiency 
and analyst trust. 
4.1. What the current results do and do not validate 
The perfect subset performance validates that, under the specific configuration represented in the 
uploaded artifacts, both models behave deterministically and with very high confidence, leaving no 
misclassification to triage. This outcome is valuable but limited: in operational IDS, error -driven 
diagnostics are most useful precisely when the model encounters difficult boundary regions, 
distributional shifts, or novel attack variants [10], [18]. Consequently, the present results mainly validate 
the baseline explainability layer (stable drivers, consistent feature signals), rather than the full failure -
casebook mechanism. 
4.2. Why a “no-error subset” matters for an operational diagnostics framing 
A common pitfall in XAI -for-IDS deployments is to present explanations even when the 
underlying evaluation regime does not reflect operational complexity. Surveys caution that XAI can 
create illusory assurance if analysts interpret stable explanations as proof of robustness or generalization 
[10], [18]. From an operational diagnostics perspective, a no-error subset should be treated as: 
a) sanity-check phase (are dominant features plausible and consistent?), and 
b) a baseline reference for later comparisons under stress conditions (imbalance, drift/shift, noisy 
labels, adversarial perturbations), which cybersecurity XAI work emphasizes as crucial for 
trustworthy SOC integration [12], [15], [23], [24].

4.3. Interpreting cross-model SHAP agreement 
Cross-model agreement on dominant features strengthens interpretability claims: it suggests that 
explanations are not merely artifacts of a single modeling choice. This is useful for governance and 
auditability, themes repeatedly emphasized in trustworthy and ethical XAI discussions [9], [25] . 
However, agreement is not equivalent to correctness under change; IDS-XAI surveys specifically argue 
that explanation stability should be evaluated under realistic operational shifts [10], [15], [18], [23] . 
Therefore, the next methodological step is not “more plots,” but targeted scenario testing designed to 
produce meaningful failure archetypes (FP/FN), enabling the casebook and triage rationale to 
demonstrate operational value. 
4.4. Limitations and recommended next experiments 
Although the proposed workflow is designed to operationalize explainability into reusable 
diagnostics (Failure Casebook) and analyst -facing decision support (Triage Rationale), the current 
evaluation is bounded by the artifacts available in this study and the diagnostic subset used for SHAP 
computation. As a result, the findings should be interpreted as a baseline diagnostic validation  
demonstrating how explanations can be structured into operational outputs, rather than as a complete 
operational validation across diverse real -world SOC conditions. The following limitations therefore 
clarify what is and is not evidenced by the present r un, and motivate the next experiments required for 
stronger external validity. 
In particular, two limitations are explicit given the available materials and the observed prediction 
outcomes. First, failure -driven diagnostics cannot be instantiated because false positives and false 
negatives are absent in the analyzed subset, which prevents the Failure Casebook from being populated 
with misclassification archetypes. Second, robustness across operational scenarios  such as class 
imbalance, distribution drift/shift, or adversarial effects is not quantified in the provided results, despite 
being a prominent concern in IDS-XAI surveys and recent XAI-for-IDS studies [10], [14], [15], [18]. 
To move from baseline validation to operational validation, a coherent follow -on design would 
introduce controlled shift and imbalance regimes and then evaluate: (1) the emergence of “confidently 
wrong” errors, (2) the stability of attribution signatures a cross scenarios, and (3) whether the triage 
rationale meaningfully reduces investigative load while preserving detection of high-risk events, aligned 
with cyber-risk-oriented XAI frameworks [13], [22]. 
5. CONCLUSION 
This paper reframes explainability for ML-based intrusion detection as an operational diagnostic 
mechanism, rather than a purely descriptive interpretability add -on. By integrating SHAP attributions 
with confidence-aware error labeling, the proposed workflow produces two analyst-oriented artifacts: a 
Failure Casebook for organizing recurring misclassification archetypes and a Triage Rationale that links 
explanation evidence to actionable prioritization in SOC workflows. Using flow -based features (78 
attributes) and two tabular classifiers (XGBoost and RandomForest), the evaluated explainability subset 
(1,000 instances per model) yields perfect performance on the provided run (FP=0, FN=0), which 
explains why the failure casebook is not populated in this instance. Importantly, SHAP global 
summaries still provide a reproducible diagnostic baseline showing consistent dominant drivers across 
models, supporting transparency and operational monitoring.  
The current evidence is bounded by the absence of misclassifications on the analyzed subset; 
therefore, the full diagnostic value of the failure casebook is expected to emerge under more challenging 
operational scenarios (e.g., class imbalance, distribution shift/concept drift, and adversarially influenced 
traffic). Future work should prioritize scenario -driven stress testing to intentionally surface FP/FN
