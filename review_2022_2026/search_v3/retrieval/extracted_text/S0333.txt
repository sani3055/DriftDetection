A B S T R A C T  
Ransomware is a critical cybersecurity threat that disrupts healthcare, finance, and government by 
encrypting data and demanding ransom. Although narrative and systematic reviews have examined 
ransomware from technical, sector -specific, and legal perspecti ves, quantitative bibliometric mapping 
remains scarce, and existing studies cover shorter periods and fewer analytical techniques. This study 
addresses that gap through a bibliometric analysis of 1,386 ransomware-related publications indexed in 
Scopus from 2007 to 2024, using Scopus, BiblioMagika, VOSviewer, Publish or Perish, and 
Bibliometrix. It examines publication trends, leading authors, institutions, countries, thematic clusters, 
and collaboration patterns. Results show a sharp rise in output since 2017, though only seven researchers 
have published ten or more papers on the topic. Keyword and thematic -map patterns point to early 
detection techniques, deep learning, data breach anal ysis, convolutional neural networks, feature 
selection, machine learning, and artificial intelligence as areas of growing activity, consistent with a shift 
toward proactive and intelligent defense mechanisms. By mapping the intellectual structure and 
evolution of ransomware research, this study offers insights for cybersecurity professionals, academics, 
and policymakers seeking to understand current trends, anticipate future challenges, and design more 
effective mitigation strategies.
 
 
1. INTRODUCTION 
In today's digital era, we are immersed in a continuous stream of data, created, stored, and shared every second [1]. While 
these technological advancements offer tremendous opportunities for progress, they also open the door to increasingly 
complex cyber threats. These dangers extend far beyond personal devices, reaching critical infrastructures such as public 
utilities, corporate networks, and consumer-integrated healthcare devices, where a successful intrusion can disrupt operations 
and directly jeopardize patient safety [2]. Prevalent threats in this landscape include denial -of-service (DoS), distributed 
denial-of-service (DDoS), and man-in-the-middle (MitM) attacks, but ransomware stands out as the most alarming [2], [3]. 
Reported increases of over 50% in ransomware assaults on consumer -integrated healthcare devices illustrate how quickly 
this threat has escalated  [4]. Its ability to inflict severe financial losses and paralyze operations in an instant makes it a 
persistent and destructive force in the cybersecurity landscape. 
Ransomware is a malicious program that has recently become very popular due to its devastating power. This software can 
cripple various vital facilities, stop businesses, and even uncover private data. Several cases, such as the Colonial Pipelin e 
ransomware attack, show just how severe the damage and losses incurred by ransomware can be [5], [6]. Given that we are 
currently highly dependent on data, it's crucial for us to understand how ransomware works and its impact, so we can be 
more careful and prepare better protection [7]. 
Ransomware, a malicious software designed to lock files or systems for ransom, has surged dramatically, not just in its 
sophistication but also in the frequency of its attacks [8], [9], [10]. The facts show that the impact of ransomware incidents 
like the WannaCry attack in 2017 crippled over 200,000 computers across more than 150 countries [11]. More recently, the 
Colonial Pipeline incident in 2021 caused widespread fuel shortages in the United States [12], exposing the vulnerability of 
critical infrastructure to cyberattacks.

The 2021 ransomware attack on the Irish Health Service Executive (HSE) caused chaos, severely disrupting healthcare 
services and sparking a nationwide public sector crisis  [13]. The attack on the HSE server permeated every aspect of 
healthcare service from outpatient appointments, radiological and laboratory investigations, and result reporting to electronic 
patient records and personal data including contact information and onl ine communication methods, even compromising 
secure and confidential HSE email servers. More strikingly, in late 2023, the Xfinity data breach, linked to a widespread 
vulnerability (CVE-2023–4966) in Citrix technology, exposed the data of nearly 36 million  people [2]. These incidents 
vividly illustrate ransomware's disruptive potential and the dire consequences it imposes on both individuals and 
organizations. 
Fortunately, the battle against ransomware is also evolving right alongside the threat. Technological innovation has paved 
the way for far more robust and sophisticated ransomware detection and prevention mechanisms. A significant amount of 
research has focused on artificial intelligence-based solutions, particularly machine and deep learning algorithms, to predict 
and identify ransomware activities in real-time [2], [14], [15], [16]. Additionally, extensive research has explored the use of 
blockchain technology to secure data transactions and prevent unauthorized access. As we move forward, ongoing research 
and innovation are essential to staying ahead of this persistent and ever-evolving threat. 
While technical efforts have advanced significantly including detection systems, mitigation strategies, and encryption 
mechanisms, non-technical studies tend to rely heavily on Systematic Literature Reviews (SLRs) to explore conceptual 
developments. However, relatively few have utilized bibliometric methods to systematically map the intellectual structure, 
influential contributions, and thematic evolution within the ransomware research domain—a gap this study seeks to address. 
The lack of bibliometric exploration leaves critical questions unanswered: How has the field evolved? Who are the central 
contributors shaping it? What are the dominant clusters of knowledge, and where are the underexplored areas? Unlike 
conventional reviews, bibliometric analysis provides quantitative insights into citation patterns, author collaboration, and 
conceptual trends at scale. 
Leveraging these strengths, this study presents a comprehensive bibliometric and network analysis of ransomware research 
indexed in Scopus, from its inception to 2024. It identifies key research trends and emerging themes, leading contributors, 
high-impact publication venues, and foundational works that have shaped the field —while simultaneously uncovering 
literature gaps that point to new directions in ransomware research. 
Specifically, this study seeks to answer the following research questions: 
1. What is the current landscape of research on ransomware?  
2. What new trends are appearing in ransomware research? 
3. Who are the key contributors driving progress in ransomware research?  
4. What are the main publications that serve as leading platform for disseminating high-impact ransomware research? 
5. What seminal works have shaped the trajectory and key discussions within ransomware research? 
6. What are the main research themes that characterize the development and expansion of ransomware studies, and 
how do these themes suggest future research directions in this field?  
The importance of this analysis extends beyond documenting the historical progression of ransomware studies; it also reveals 
key influences and future research paths, thereby supporting future scholarly work and industry advancements in our data-
driven world. 
The key contributions of this study are fourfold. First, it provides the most temporally extensive bibliometric mapping of 
ransomware research to date, spanning the entire period available in Scopus at the time of retrieval (2007–2024), compared 
with the narrower 2013–2023 window covered by the only prior ransomware -specific bibliometric study [17]. Second, it 
extends the analytical scope beyond conventional performance metrics (publication trends, key authors, institutions, 
countries, and publishers) to include an overall publication-landscape overview, an author-keyword co-occurrence network, 
and a thematic map, none of which were reported in [17]. Third, it cross -validates the reported indicators across five 
complementary tools (Scopus, BiblioMagika, Publish or Perish, VOSviewer, and Bibliometrix) to improve reliability. 
Fourth, by explicitly linking the identified thematic clusters (e.g., early d etection, deep learning, and feature selection) to 
concrete future research directions, this study offers actionable guidance for researchers, practitioners, and policymakers 
seeking to anticipate the next phase of ransomware research. 
 
2. LITERATURE REVIEW 
As discussed previously, this form of malicious software, known as ransomware, prevents users from accessing their 
computer system or data unless a payment is made. [8], [9]. The victim must adhere to the instructions provided in the ransom 
note to make a Bitcoin payment in order to decrypt and regain access to the original files. Attackers use Bitcoin because 
transactions are pseudonymous, making it difficult to trace the recipient [2].

Ransomware attacks typically begin with the distribution of malicious files through various methods [18]. These files are 
often spread via drive-by downloads, where users inadvertently download malware by visiting compromised websites, as 
well as through phishing emails that trick recipients into clicking malicious links or attachments [15], [16]. Additionally, 
attackers exploit vulnerabilities in internet -accessible systems and strategically compromise web servers to distribute 
ransomware [6], [18]. 
Ransomware can be categorized into three main types, as shown in Figure 1 as adapted from [11]: Scareware, Locker, and 
Crypto. Scareware is designed to intimidate users into paying a ransom by displaying fake warnings or alerts. Locker is a 
more aggressive form that locks victims out of their entire device or screen, denying access until a ransom is paid, but unlike 
crypto ransomware, it does not encrypt files. Crypto ransomware focuses on encrypting valuable data, making it inaccessible 
until a ransom is paid [19]. Additionally, Crypto ransomware can be further categorized into Symmetrical, Asymmetrical, 
and Hybrid encryption methods. Symmetrical encryption uses the same key for both encryption and decryption, while 
Asymmetrical encryption employs a pair of public and private keys. Hybrid encryption combines both methods to enhance 
security. 
 
Fig. 1. Types of ransomware [11] 
Regarding the ransomware life cycle, researchers in [3] explained that it consists of six phases as illustrated in Figure 2. It 
begins with distribution, where ransomware files are spread through vectors like phishing emails and drive -by downloads. 
This is followed by the infection phase, where the malicious p ayload is executed. The ransomware then enters the 
communication phase, establishing a connection with a command -and-control server. Next is the file search phase, where 
the ransomware scans the system for valuable files to encrypt. In the encryption phase , it uses algorithms to encrypt these 
files, rendering them inaccessible. Finally, the ransom demand phase involves presenting the victim with instructions to pay 
a ransom, typically in cryptocurrency, to regain access to their files. This organized process shows the clear steps ransomware 
takes to compromise target systems. 
 
Fig. 2. Ransomware life cycle [3] 
A deeper understanding of ransomware attack complexities emerges with the help of a detailed taxonomy provided in [7]. 
This work breaks down different elements into four main categories: Target, Infection, Communication, and Harmful Action, 
illustrated in Figure 3. This structure genuinely helps us make sense of how ransomware functions and the different tactics 
it uses to infect and harm computer systems. 
The Target category is the target of ransomware attacks, which is divided into 2 subcategories. The first, Platforms, covers 
devices and systems like PCs, workstations, mobile devices, and IoT/CPS. The second, Victims, refers to the specific entities 
targeted, such as end -users or particular organizations. The Infection category, which comes next, describes the various 
tactics used to get ransomware onto systems, including common ones like phishing emails, malicious applications, and even 
drive-by downloads. Whereas vulnerabilities are security gaps in the system that can be exploited by hackers to infect devices 
with ransomware.

The third category, Communication, explains how ransomware interacts with command -and-control servers, either using 
hard-coded domains or Domain Generation Algorithms (DGA) to evade detection. Finally, the  Malicious Action category 
outlines the harmful activities carried out by ransomware after infection, such as data encryption, system locking, and data 
exfiltration—tactics designed to pressure victims into paying a ransom. This taxonomy provides a structured approach to 
understanding ransomware operations, aiding cybersecurity professionals in developing effective prevention and response 
strategies [7]. 
Research on ransomware identified in the Scopus database began in 2007 with the study titled ‘Awareness Education as the 
Key to Ransomware Prevention’ [20]. In this study, Luo and Liao proposed a framework comprising four steps to proactively 
prevent ransomware threats. The steps include policy/ procedure/regulation, access control & management, exposure 
analysis & reporting, and finally, awareness education  & training. This awareness needs to be comprehended by 
management, IT staff, and end-users alike. 
Subsequent publications on ransomware detection began to emerge in 2014 and 2015, where expert system-based detection, 
dynamic analysis, and static analysis started to be developed [21], [22]. Authors in [21] discussed an expert system that 
authorizes processes based on administrator approval using hash codes generated by the MD5 algorithm. This system 
enhances data and information security by detecting and preventing ransomware attacks and other malware, whil e also 
improving operating system performance by limiting CPU usage and resource utilization.  Whereas the researchers in [22] 
discussed the detection of ransomware on mobile devices using a combined approach of static and dynamic analysis. This 
method enhances mobile security by detecting and analyzing ransomware behavior to understand its attack principles 
comprehensively. Since then, the number of studies on ransomware has significantly increased, peaking in 2024 with 284 
publications on Scopus. 
Over the past five years, numerous studies examining ransomware trends and developments have been released in various 
publications. These studies extensively cover a wide array of critical areas and platforms, as detailed in Table I. These reviews 
also strive to equip researchers and practitioners with meaningful insights that can inform the development of more resilient 
and adaptive defense strategies. Ideally, the recommendations offered will help guide future efforts in tackling the 
increasingly complex landscape of ransomware threats.

Several studies have explored how ransomware can be detected more effectively. For example, researchers in [2] and [33] 
reviewed methods that use deep learning to spot ransomware activity. These techniques are especially useful because they 
can recognize complex patterns in large amounts of data. Other researchers, such as those in [11], [16], [28], have focused 
on detecting ransomware early before it starts encrypting files. Catching it at this stage is important because it gives systems 
a chance to stop the attack before serious damage is done. This kind of early warning can help prevent data loss , reduce 
downtime, and limit financial impact. It also gives security teams time to respond and block further threats. Overall, these 
studies highlight the need for smarter, real-time monitoring tools that can keep up with how ransomware is changing. 
Moreover, there is a review paper that discusses the interdisciplinary aspects between technology and law [6]. This study 
emphasizes the need for an integrated approach to develop effective defense, mitigation, and sanction strategies, proposing 
guidelines such as defining ransomware as an autonomous offense to address this global cybersecurity challenge. 
Most recently, several 2026 studies have continued to advance the technical frontier that this bibliometric analysis 
independently identifies as an emerging thematic cluster. Barnes and Ghafarian [37] evaluated static, PE -header-based 
machine learning classifiers for ransomware detection, finding that tree-based ensembles such as XGBoost perform strongly 
when ransomware and other malware families are structurally distinct, while recommending hybrid static–dynamic pipelines 
for harder cases. Maghanaki et al. [38] benchmarked a broad set of machine learning and deep learning models for IoT 
malware detection across eight malware categories including ransomware, reinforcing the centrality of ML/DL techniques 
already highlighted in our keyword co -occurrence network. These 2026 contributions confirm that the field continues to 
move quickly, further motivating the need for an up-to-date bibliometric account such as the one presented here.

Despite numerous review papers available on ransomware, bibliometric analysis specifically focusing on this threat has been 
scarce until very recently. Bibliometric analysis and traditional surveys or reviews differ significantly: bibliometric analysis 
involves a quantitative evaluation of academic literature to identify key research trends, influential authors, and knowledge 
gaps, while surveys or reviews are typically qualitative, summarizing findings and offering critical assessments. 
A recent and notable exception is the work by [17]. Their study delves into 1,200 publications from the WoS Core Collection, 
covering articles from 2013 to 2023. Mohanty et al. examined crucial aspects like publication trends, key authors, institutions, 
countries, publishers, highly cited documents, and research themes. 
Beyond this, broader bibliometric studies have been conducted on malware in general  [39], [40]. For instance, one study 
[40] analyzed over 4,000 articles from the ISI Web of Science between 2005 and 2015. It focused on the geographical spread 
of research activities, highlighting significant contributions from Asia. This research broke down articles, citations, research 
areas, k eywords, institutions, and authors, even classifying terms for malware detection systems. Another project [39] 
specifically tackled Android malware, reviewing 5,622 Web of Science articles from 2010 to 2019. After filtering, 1,278 
articles were analyzed. This study mapped the academic scene, assessing productivity, research areas, Web of Science 
categories, authors, most-cited articles, institutions, and impactful journals. Both of these broader studies consistently showed 
the dominant role of Asian researchers and institutions in malware research.

This chapter outlines the methodology used to conduct a bibliometric analysis of ransomware-related literature. Drawing on 
approaches from [42] and [43], the method was adapted to suit the specific context of ransomware research. It includes a 
structured search strategy designed to capture a broad range of academic and industry publications. The discussion is divided 
into two parts: the first exp lains how the data were collected (Search Strategy), and the second describes the bibliometric 
techniques and tools used to analyze the dataset (Techniques and Tools). 
3.1 Search Strategy 
The data for analysis were obtained from the Scopus database, which is recognized for its reliable and comprehensive citation 
information. The search string employed was “ransomware*”, where the asterisk * was utilized as a wildcard to ensure broad 
coverage of variations, including “ransomware” and “ransomwares.” This string was applied to document titles and 
keywords. The time span for the data was before February 2025, and the source types selected included conference 
proceedings, journal articles, review s, and books. This search focused on documents written in English only. This query 
returned a total of 1,424 records retrieved on 1 February 2025. Before analysis, all 1,424 records were screened against 
explicit eligibility criteria: a record was excluded if it (i) was an exact duplicate across export batches, (ii) had an inaccessible 
or unretrievable abstract/full metadata entry in Scopus, (iii) was missing bibliographic fields essential for bibliometric 
processing (e.g., author, year, or source title), or (iv) was not written in English despite matching the search string. Applying 
these criteria removed 38 records, leaving 1,386 documents that constitute the bibliometric corpus analyzed in this study and 
exported in RIS and CSV formats. The four criteria were applied together in a single screening pass rather than logged 
separately, so a per-criterion breakdown of the 38 exclusions is not available; Figure 4 reports them as a single aggregate 
count. The 1,386 -document corpus (processed via BiblioMagika, VO Sviewer, Publish or Perish, and Bibliometrix to 
generate the statistics, networks, and maps in Section 4) is distinct from this manuscript’s own numbered references [1] –
[70], which were consulted to write the Introduction and Literature Review and are not a sample of the corpus, though some 
overlap is expected. This study is classified as a bibliometric analysis rather than a systematic literature review, so Figur e 
4’s screening constructs the quantitative corpus rather than following a full PRISMA protocol. The corpus is bounded at 
2024 because, at the February 2025 retrieval date, that year’s Scopus indexing was still incomplete; early-2025 records were 
therefore excluded so that every included year (2007–2024) reflects a complete annual count.  
These articles served as the foundation to evaluate the existing state of ransomware research. Analyzing these foundational 
articles allowed us to pinpoint key advancements, existing hurdles, and promising directions in ransomware research. This 
meticulous process guaranteed our insights were derived directly from cutting -edge primary literature, thus reflecting the 
most current and crucial findings. The overall process is depicted in Figure 4, with the methodology adapted from [42]. 
3.2 Techniques and Tools 
According to [43], bibliometric analysis consists of two techniques: main techniques and enrichment techniques. The main 
techniques include performance analysis and science mapping. This research employed both by performing various metrics 
for performance analysis, including publication-related metrics, citation-related metrics, and a combination of citation-and-
publication-related metrics. For science mapping, the research utilized citation analysis, co -citation analysis and co -
authorship analysis. 
Publication-related metrics include total publications, number of contributing authors, sole -authored publications, co -
authored publications, and more. Citation -related metrics encompass total citations and average citations. Citation -and-
publication-related metrics cover the number of cited publications, citations per cited publication, h-index, and more.

In science mapping, citation analysis explores the relationships among publications and identifies the most influential 
publications. Co -citation analysis examines the relationships among cited publications and foundational themes. Co -
authorship analysis investigates social interactions and relationships among authors, including their affiliations. Additionally, 
the research involved enrichment techniques focused on visualizations to facilitate the interpretation of the analyzed data. 
These visualizations help in presenting complex data in a more accessible and understandable manner.

The Scopus website [44] was utilized to download the dataset for this bibliometric analysis. By using the “Analyze Results” 
feature on Scopus Website, we could obtain various visual and tabular information related to the searched publications, such 
as documents by year, document s per year by source, documents by author, documents by affiliation, and documents by 
country. Some statistical results, like documents by subject area, documents by type, and documents per year by source, 
could be directly used. However, for other results, we needed to perform refinement and harmonization processes to ensure 
more accurate data, particularly regarding author names and affiliations. Therefore, the Scopus website served as a crucial 
tool for addressing RQ1, RQ2, and RQ3 in this research. 
Another tool used in this study is BiblioMagika, developed by Aidi Ahmi from Universiti Utara Malaysia [45]. Built on 
Microsoft Excel, BiblioMagika is designed to support bibliometric analysis, particularly in tracking publication output and 
measuring research impact. It also helps streamline the process of cleaning and organizing bibliographic data, which is 
essential for accurate analysis. In this study, we used BiblioMagika to process raw data retrieved from Scopus, including 
data cleaning and refinement. This step was crucial to ensure the validity of the results. Without it, inconsistencies such a s 
author name variations due to typos, or institutional names being treated as different entities because of abbreviations (e.g., 
“University” vs. “Univ.”), could lead to misleading conclusions. Given its features and flexibility, BiblioMagika was well-
suited for addressing all six research questions (RQ1–RQ6) in this study.

Harzing’s Publish or Perish  is a widely used software tool for analyzing citation data in academic publications [46]. It 
supports a variety of data sources and can extract raw bibliographic data, which it then processes into several citation metrics,

ransomware research. 
VOSviewer is a software tool commonly used to create and visualize bibliometric networks [47]. These networks help 
illustrate how journals, authors, or publications are connected through citations, co-authorships, or co-citations. The tool also 
includes text mining features, which allow it to generate visual maps of key terms found in scientific literature. In this study, 
we used VOSviewer to produce visualizations that support our analysis for RQ3, RQ4, and RQ6, particularly in identifying 
research clusters and keyword patterns. 
We also used iipmaps.com [48] to support our analysis of global research contributions. This online tool provides country-
level visualizations that show how publications and authors are distributed around the world. For our study, it was particularly 
helpful in answering RQ3, as it allowed us to quickly identify which countries are most active in ransomware research. By 
using this tool, we gained a clearer picture of regional trends and academic engagement across different parts of the world. 
The final tool used in this study is Bibliometrix, an R-based package designed for conducting detailed bibliometric analysis 
[49]. It offers a wide range of features for exploring research trends, identifying influential publications, and mapping 
collaboration networks. In our research, we used Bibliometrix to help answer RQ1, RQ2, RQ4, RQ5, and RQ6. However, 
we chose not to use it for RQ3 due to inconsistencies in how some author names were recognized in our dataset. These issues 
led to discrepancies when compared with results from other tools, particularly in identifying contributors by country.

4.2 Publication Trends 
This section addresses RQ2, focusing on emerging trends in ransomware publications by examining publication and citation 
patterns (Figure 6). From 2007 to 2015, ransomware publications indexed in Scopus remained relatively low, with

The analysis includes not only Total Publications (TP) but also additional citation-based metrics such as the Number of Cited 
Publications (NCP), Total Citations (TC), Average Citations per Publication (C/P), Average Citations per Cited Publication 
(C/CP), as well as the h -index, g-index, and m -index. These indicators collectively provide a comprehensive view of a 
researcher's academic influence and productivity. All scores were initially retrieved from the Scopus database and 
subsequently refined using BiblioMagika. The results are presented in Table X and Table XI. 
For clarity, TP refers to the total number of publications authored by a researcher. NCP indicates how many of those works 
have been cited at least once. TC represents the total number of citations received. C/P is calculated by dividing total citations 
by total publications, while C/CP reflects the average number of citations for only the cited works, offering insight into the 
impact of a researcher’s most recognized contributions. 
The h -index measures both the productivity and citation impact of a researcher's publications, where an h -index of 'n' 
indicates the researcher has 'n' papers each cited at least 'n' times. In contrast, the g -index focuses on the distribution of 
citations received by a researcher's publications, emphasizing the impact of highly cited papers; a g-index of 'n' signifies the 
top 'n' articles together have at least 'n²' citations. It considers the cumulative impact of a researcher's publications. La stly, 
the m -index is calculated by dividing the h -index by the number of years the researcher has been active, offering an 
annualized measure of their impact.

Bradford's Law, a bibliometric principle, describes the distribution of articles among journals in a field [42]. Bradford’s Law 
describes the distribution of scientific productivity (not citation impact) among the sources that publish on a given topic. It 
divides sources into three zones of roughly equal total publication output: Zone 1 (the core) contains the smallest number of 
sources that together produce roughly one-third of all publications on the topic, meaning these are the most prolific rather 
than necessarily the most-cited sources; Zone 2 contains a larger number of sources producing a similar share of publications 
at a lower average output per source; and Zone 3 (the tail) contains the largest number of sources, each contributing only a 
few publications, together producing the remaining third. Bradford’s Law predicts that the number of sources in Zones 2 and 
3 will be approximately n and n² times greater than in Zone 1, respectively, following a 1 : n : n2 progression [70]. 
Applying Bradford’s Law with Bibliometrix and BiblioMagika helps identify the most productive publication sources within 
the ransomware dataset and visually represents how publication output is distributed across sources. In this dataset, Zone 1 
(the core) comprises 64 sources, Zone 2 comprises 322 sources, and Zone 3 (the tail) comprises 461 sources. Figure 14, 
generated using Bibliometrix, shows the core sources identified under Bradford’s Law. 
 
Fig. 14. Core sources by Bradford’s Law. 
The observed zone distribution (64 : 322 : 461) departs noticeably from the theoretical 1 : n : n² progression implied by 
Bradford’s Law. Taking Zone 1 as the reference (n₁ = 64), the observed multipliers are only about 5.0 (322/64) for Zone 2 
and 7.2 (461/64) for Zone 3, far below the multiplicative (n vs. n²) growth the law predicts, while the ratio between Zone 3 
and Zone 2 (461/322 ≈ 1.4) is much closer to linear than to the expected n-fold increase over Zone 2. This pattern suggests 
that the ransomware literature is comparatively concentrated in a small set of core venues relative to its long tail, which is 
common in emerging, still -consolidating interdisciplinary fields where authors have not yet converged on a stable set of 
specialized outlets. Restricting the corpus to Scopus -indexed sources may also compress the tail relative to what a multi -
database corpus (e.g., including non -Scopus conference proceedings or regional journals) would show, since additional 
peripheral sources would be expected to inf late Zone 3 disproportionately. Despite this quantitative departure from the 
classical model, Bradford’s Law remains a useful heuristic for identifying the core sources that concentrate ransomware 
research output, as reflected in Figure 14.

5. CONCLUSIONS 
This study presents a bibliometric analysis of ransomware-related research published in the Scopus database before the year 
2025. Since the first Scopus article on ransomware was published in 2007, this analysis focuses on papers published from 
2007 to 2024. A total of 1,424 documents were initially collected. After removing records with issues such as inaccessibility, 
incomplete data, or duplicates, 1,386 documents remained. 
Various tools were used in the analysis process, including the Scopus website, Microsoft Excel, Biblio Magika, Harzing's 
Publish or Perish, VOSviewer, iipmaps, and Bibliometrix. Using these tools, the analysis aimed to answer six research 
questions regarding: the current research landscape, publication trends, key contributors (including the most productive and 
influential authors, institutions, and countries), leading platforms for publications, landmark papers, main research themes, 
and future research directions. 
The key findings of this study are listed as follows: 
1. Since 2018, more than 100 papers related to ransomware have been published annually in Scopus. Specifically, in 2023 
and 2024, ransomware-related publications reached 282 and 284, respectively. 
2. The majority of these publications are research papers, either in the form of conference papers (48.92%) or journal 
articles (43.29%). 
3. The journal IEEE Access has published the most ransomware-related research, with a total of 43 publications. 
4. Amrita Vishwa Vidyapeetham (Amrita University) in India is the most productive institution, with 51 publications 
related to ransomware. 
5. Universiti Teknologi Malaysia, Malaysia, is the most influential institution, with a total of 2,249 citations. 
6. Only seven authors have published 10 or more papers on ransomware by 2024. 
7. Bander Ali Saleh Al-Rimy is the most productive and influential author, with 13 publications, 809 total citations, an h-
index of 11, and a g-index of 13. 
8. The United States is the most productive and influential country in this field, with the highest number of publications 
(363), the highest number of contributing authors (772), and the highest total citations (6,150). 
9. Computer Science is the most popular research area for ransomware publications, with 1,155 publications, accounting 
for 83.33% of the total publications. 


 
 
 
 
Rafrastara et al, Babylonian Journal of Artificial Intelligence Vol. (202 6), 2026, 46–76 72 
10. The publication by Scaife et al. in 2016, titled "CryptoLock (and Drop It): Stopping Ransomware Attacks on User 
Data," has the highest number of citations, with a total of 354. 
11. Keyword and thematic-map patterns (Section 4.8) point to early detection, deep learning, data breach, CNN, feature 
selection, machine learning, and artificial intelligence as areas of growing research activity, though this reading is 
qualitative rather than a statistically confirmed classification (Section 4.8.2). 
This bibliometric study thus fills a critical void in the existing literature by uncovering structural patterns, scholarly 
influences, and evolving themes within ransomware research—effectively answering the gap articulated in the introduction. 
The finding s provide valuable insights for researchers, policymakers, and industry practitioners, helping to identify 
prominent areas, key players, and future research directions essential for effectively combating the ever -evolving threat of 
ransomware. 
6. LIMITATIONS AND DIRECTIONS FOR FUTURE WORK 
This study provides a comprehensive overview of ransomware research through bibliometric and network analysis; however, 
several limitations should be noted. The data were limited to English-language publications indexed in Scopus up to 2024, 
which may exclude relevant research from other databases or more recent contributions. Differences among bibliometric 
tools may also influence the interpretation of collaboration patterns and thematic clusters. Additionally, citation -based 
indicators used in this study may undervalue recently published but potentially impactful works. 
Despite these limitations, the findings offer a clear foundation for future work. The keyword and thematic -map patterns 
associated with early ransomware detection using artificial intelligence, particularly deep learning and Convolutional Neural 
Networks (CNN), represent promising areas for further exploration, and confirming their emerging status with directly 
computed temporal indicators (Section 4.8.2) is itself a priority for that future work. Future studies may also benefit from 
refining feature selection methods to enhance the precision and speed of ransomware detection models. Expanding the scope 
to include multi-database sources, more languages, or hybrid approaches that combine bibliometric and qualitative analyses 
could further enrich the understanding of this dynamic research domain.
