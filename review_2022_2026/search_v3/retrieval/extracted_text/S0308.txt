Anomaly Detection in Software System Logs Using Machine
Learning
Jirong Han
College of Arts and Sciences, University of Washington, Seattle, USA
hjr23679@uw.edu
Abstract. Modern software systems generate large volumes of logs that record events, states,
errors, and user or component activities. Manual inspection is increasingly unable to support
timely fault diagnosis, performance monitoring, and security analysis. This review examines
how machine learning is used to detect anomalies in software system logs, with emphasis on
the full pipeline from raw log messages to operational alerts. It first explains software logs,
event templates, anomaly types, labels, and commonly used evaluation measures. It then
compares log parsing, window construction, count-based features, semantic embeddings,
classical machine learning, deep sequence models, self-supervised learning, and parser-free
approaches. Recent journal studies show that model accuracy depends not only on
architecture but also on parsing distinguishability, feature representation, window design,
label quality, dataset imbalance, and cross-system differences. Traditional models can
remain competitive when features and tuning are appropriate, while transformer,
contrastive, and end-to-end methods improve semantic modeling and adaptation to unseen
events. However, data drift, limited labels, inconsistent benchmarks, false alarms,
interpretability, privacy, and deployment cost remain important barriers. Future work should
prioritize adaptive, transferable, explainable, and privacy-aware systems that combine
accurate detection with practical maintenance needs.
Keywords: log anomaly detection, software logs, machine learning, log parsing, system
monitoring
1. Introduction
Web services, cloud platforms, databases, distributed applications, and Internet of Things systems
continuously produce logs while they run. A log message may record a timestamp, a component
name, an operation, a severity level, an error code, or a short description written by a developer.
These records are among the most direct observations of software behavior after deployment. They
support troubleshooting, reliability analysis, performance monitoring, security auditing, and incident
response. The practical difficulty is scale. A busy service may generate millions of messages in a
short period, and meaningful abnormal events may be hidden among repetitive normal activity.
Manual keyword searches and fixed rules are still useful for known failures, but they cannot easily
keep pace with changing software, unfamiliar faults, or long event sequences. Machine learning-
based log anomaly detection addresses this problem by learning patterns associated with normal or

abnormal behavior and then identifying observations that do not follow the learned patterns. A
recent survey shows that the field now covers preprocessing, representation learning, sequence
modeling, threshold selection, and operational evaluation rather than a single classification step [1].
The central challenge is that log data are not naturally ready for machine learning. Headers are
often structured, but the message body is semi-structured text that mixes stable words with changing
values such as addresses, file paths, identifiers, durations, and counts. Most pipelines therefore parse
raw messages into templates, group events into sessions or time windows, and convert the resulting
units into numerical features. These preprocessing choices determine what information is preserved
for the detector and can therefore affect downstream anomaly-detection performance. An empirical
study comparing six representation techniques, seven learning models, and four public datasets
found that the representation and aggregation method can substantially affect downstream
performance [2]. Parsing quality also requires careful interpretation. Khan et al. found no strong
general correlation between commonly used parsing-accuracy measures and anomaly-detection
accuracy; instead, the ability of parsed templates to keep normal and abnormal behaviors
distinguishable was more important [3]. These findings show that an accurate-looking intermediate
component does not necessarily produce an accurate end-to-end detector.
Research has moved from keyword rules, statistical thresholds, principal component analysis
(PCA), and shallow classifiers toward models that learn event semantics and order. LayerLog
represents words, log events, and log sequences in a hierarchy so that local meaning and sequence
context can be modeled together [4]. ContexLog avoids a separate parsing stage and preserves raw-
message information while strengthening contextual representation [5]. LogEncoder applies
contrastive representation learning so that normal and anomalous sequences become more separable
in the learned space [6]. A semi-supervised bidirectional temporal convolution network models
contextual dependencies in both directions while reducing reliance on fully labeled data [7]. At the
same time, the growth of deep learning does not make classical methods irrelevant. An evaluation of
an improved PCA pipeline reported that a lightweight, semantically informed traditional method
could approach the effectiveness of more complex methods while using fewer resources and
remaining stable with limited training data [8]. The appropriate method therefore depends on the
data, supervision, latency, and maintenance setting rather than on model complexity alone.
This paper reviews anomaly detection in software system logs from a data-science perspective.
Its goals are to explain the basic concepts of log data and anomaly types, describe how raw
messages are parsed and represented, compare classical and deep learning methods, summarize
representative journal studies from the past three years, and identify limitations that affect practical
use. The discussion connects software operations with text processing, event sequences, feature
representations, classification, and anomaly-detection metrics. Because this is a literature review, it
does not reproduce a model or claim a new experimental result. Instead, it focuses on how
methodological choices influence reported findings and on what is still needed for reliable, efficient,
and trustworthy deployment.
2. Basic concepts of software logs and anomaly detection
A software log is a sequence of records produced by programs, operating systems, middleware,
servers, or network devices during execution. A typical record contains a structured header and a
less structured message. The header may include a timestamp, host, process or thread identifier,
component, and severity level such as INFO, WARN, or ERROR. The message describes an event,
but often combines fixed text with variable parameters. For example, two messages that report a
failed connection may contain the same words but different Internet Protocol addresses and port

numbers. A log parser replaces or groups variable fields and assigns messages to event templates. A
template is therefore an abstraction of a family of similar messages, while the original parameters
may still contain important evidence. Parsing reduces the size of the event vocabulary and makes
counting or sequence analysis possible, but excessive abstraction can remove values that reveal an
anomaly. The distinction between stable tokens and parameters is thus a modeling decision, not a
purely mechanical cleaning step [2, 3].
An anomaly is an observation or pattern that differs from the expected behavior of the system. A
point or single-event anomaly occurs when one message is unusual by itself, such as an unknown
error template or an impossible parameter value. A frequency anomaly occurs when a familiar event
appears too often or too rarely within a given period. A sequential anomaly occurs when normal
events appear in an unexpected order, an expected event is missing, or a transition is unlikely. A
contextual anomaly may be normal in one context but abnormal for a specific component, host,
time, or workload. Finally, a collective anomaly is visible only when several records are considered
together. These categories overlap. A repeated login failure, for example, can be normal as a single
event but abnormal as a high-frequency sequence. This is why window construction and contextual
representation strongly affect what a detector can recognize [1, 4].
Web access logs, application logs, server logs, and distributed-system logs differ in both content
and purpose. Web logs emphasize requests, status codes, resources, response sizes, and client
information. Application logs contain developer-defined events that may change when source code
changes. Server and operating-system logs record resource, process, hardware, and service states.
Distributed systems add cross-node timing and interleaving, which makes it difficult to reconstruct
one request or failure path. Public research often uses the Hadoop Distributed File System, Blue
Gene/L, Thunderbird, Spirit, or Hadoop log datasets because they provide established benchmarks,
but their event distributions, labels, and units of analysis differ. Hadoop Distributed File System logs
are commonly grouped by block identifiers, whereas supercomputer logs are often divided by fixed
windows. A performance number from one dataset is therefore not automatically comparable with a
number from another.
Learning settings are usually described as supervised, semi-supervised, or unsupervised.
Supervised methods use labeled normal and anomalous examples. Semi-supervised methods
combine labeled and unlabeled data, while one-class methods are often trained primarily on normal
examples and detect deviations from learned normal behavior. Terminology varies across log-
anomaly studies, so the supervision setting should be stated explicitly, especially when a method is
trained only on normal data. Real datasets are highly imbalanced because failures are rare, and
labels may describe an entire session rather than the exact responsible event. Training, validation,
and test sets should also respect time or system boundaries; otherwise, similar messages can leak
across splits and make results too optimistic. These basic definitions are necessary for interpreting
model comparisons.
3. Data processing and machine learning methods for log anomaly detection
A typical pipeline begins with collection and normalization. Duplicated records may be removed
when they are produced by the collection process, and timestamps, severity fields, and component
names are converted to consistent formats. Raw messages may then be parsed into templates using
tree-based, clustering, or similarity-based parsers. The records are grouped into fixed time windows,
sliding windows, sessions, or identifier-based sequences. A fixed window is simple and supports
online processing, but it may split one failure across two windows or combine unrelated requests. A
sliding window preserves more local context but creates overlapping samples and increases

computation. Session windows can better represent a complete transaction when a reliable identifier
exists. The output can be an event-count vector, a transition or sequence, a term-frequency vector, or
an embedding learned from the text. Empirical evidence indicates that representation, parsing, and
aggregation should be evaluated together rather than selected independently [2, 3].
Classical machine learning methods remain useful because they are relatively fast, interpretable,
and available in mature software libraries. Clustering methods group similar event-count or text
vectors and may flag samples that lie far from dense clusters or cluster centers as anomalous. PCA
models the dominant variation in normal count vectors; a large reconstruction residual can indicate
behavior outside the normal subspace. Isolation Forest uses random partitions and tends to isolate
rare observations with fewer splits. One-Class Support Vector Machines learn a boundary around
normal examples, while logistic regression, decision trees, and random forests can be trained when
labeled anomalies are available. Their main limitations are dependence on hand-designed
aggregation, difficulty capturing long order relationships, and sensitivity to scale, class weights,
thresholds, and hyperparameters. Nevertheless, improved PCA results show that semantic features
and careful tuning can make a simple model competitive [8]. This supports selecting classical and
deep models according to dataset characteristics and operational constraints, rather than assuming
one family is always superior.
Deep learning methods reduce some manual feature engineering by learning representations from
sequences or text. Recurrent neural networks and long short-term memory networks model
relationships among ordered events and can flag events that are unlikely given the recent event
history. Convolutional networks detect local patterns in event or character sequences and can be
efficient in parallel computation. Attention and transformer models capture relationships across a
wider context and support pre-training on large unlabeled log collections. Hierarchical models
encode information at several levels, such as tokens, messages, and windows [4], while contextual
raw-log models can retain information removed by parsing [5]. Contrastive learning constructs an
embedding space in which positive pairs, often different views of related samples, are pulled closer
while negative pairs are pushed apart [6]. Semi-supervised bidirectional temporal convolution can
learn context from both directions while reducing the need for complete labels [7]. These models
can capture semantics and order more directly than event counts, but they require more training
resources and may be difficult to interpret or update.
Recent pipelines also question whether a separate parser is always necessary. OneLog uses a
character-based end-to-end neural model that reduces dependence on separate parsing and
vectorization stages while preserving digits and punctuation that template pipelines may discard [9].
LogFiT fine-tunes a pre-trained language model on normal logs with self-supervised objectives and
does not require a fixed vocabulary of event templates [10]. Parser-free methods can reduce
dependence on template parsing and may avoid cascading errors caused by parser mistakes,
although their performance on unseen messages still requires empirical validation. However, they
process longer raw inputs, may require more computation, and can learn accidental correlations
from identifiers or formatting. A practical compromise is to retain raw and parsed views, using the
template for stable event structure and selected parameters for values that carry operational meaning.
Evaluation usually reports precision, recall, and F1-score. Precision asks what proportion of alerts
are truly anomalous, while recall asks what proportion of known anomalies are detected. F1-score
balances the two, but it does not show all operational trade-offs. False positive rate is important
because even a small percentage can create thousands of alerts in a high-volume system. Detection
delay matters for online monitoring, and training time, prediction time, memory use, and retraining
cost affect deployment. Results must also state whether metrics are computed per event, window,

session, or incident. Thresholds should be selected on validation data instead of the test set, and
time-aware splits are preferable when the intended use is future detection. Without these controls, an
apparently high F1-score may reflect data preparation rather than robust anomaly recognition.
4. Recent studies of machine learning-based log anomaly detection
Recent journal studies illustrate a shift from isolated model proposals toward representation,
transfer, and end-to-end evaluation. LayerLog uses a word-log-sequence hierarchy and emphasizes
that a log is neither only plain text nor only an event identifier [4]. ContexLog instead processes logs
without a separate parser, preserving character and non-character information while strengthening
contextual representation [5]. LogEncoder uses contrastive learning to improve separability in the
representation space [6]. These studies share the goal of extracting more meaning than event-count
vectors, but their mechanisms differ. Hierarchy explicitly organizes information levels, parser-free
modeling retains raw evidence, and contrastive learning shapes distances among samples. Their
effectiveness therefore depends on tokenization, the available corpus, the definition of positive and
negative pairs, and how windows preserve the fault context.
Learning under limited supervision is another major research direction. Yin et al. propose a semi-
supervised detector based on bidirectional temporal convolution, allowing contextual information to
be captured from both directions in an event sequence [7]. This direction is important because
abnormal logs are rare and expert annotation is expensive. Yet semi-supervised learning introduces a
risk: incorrect pseudo-labels or incorrect assumptions about normal samples can be reinforced,
especially when anomalies are diverse or the normal distribution changes. Evaluation should
therefore report label selection, class balance, and sensitivity to the labeled fraction, not only the
final score.
The continued value of traditional machine learning is demonstrated by the improved PCA study.
By combining PCA with a lightweight semantic representation and carefully designed settings, Yang
et al. found that a simpler unsupervised approach could be competitive with advanced supervised or
semi-supervised deep models while remaining efficient and stable when training data were limited
[8]. This result does not imply that PCA is universally superior. It means that conclusions about
model families are sensitive to feature engineering, tuning, data volume, and evaluation design. It
also supports a practical baseline rule: complex models should be compared against well-tuned
classical methods rather than weak default configurations.
Parser-free and end-to-end studies respond to unstable templates and unseen messages. OneLog
applies a character-based hierarchical convolutional network directly to raw messages or sequences
and merges the parser, vectorizer, and classifier. Its experiments across message- and sequence-
based datasets suggest that an end-to-end model can learn useful representations directly from raw
logs and can benefit from multi-project training [9]. LogFiT takes a language-model approach, fine-
tuning a pre-trained transformer through self-supervised learning on normal log data [10]. The two
methods preserve raw text but make different trade-offs. Character models have small vocabularies
and retain punctuation and numerical form, whereas transformer language models offer rich
contextual semantics but typically require more memory and careful tokenization. Both reduce
dependence on manually selected templates, although neither removes the need for normalization,
windowing, and threshold selection.
Cross-system generalization has also become more central. LogETA adds time-interval
information and optimizes inter-class boundaries for transfer to another system, addressing the fact
that identical event orders may have different timing meanings [11]. LogGT represents log behavior
with heterogeneous graph features and uses transfer learning to improve cross-system anomaly

detection [12]. These methods recognize that a model trained on one service should not be expected
to work unchanged on another. They also raise difficult questions about what is transferable: words,
event relationships, timing, labels, or decision boundaries. A method may show strong transfer
between related public datasets but still fail when production components, logging practices, or fault
distributions are different.
Broader empirical work provides a useful counterweight to individual model results. Yu et al.
compared classical and deep machine learning techniques across five public log anomaly detection
datasets. Their study found that simple classical methods can outperform deep learning methods in
both accuracy and time efficiency under the evaluated settings [13]. This conclusion helps explain
why papers can report conflicting results even when they use familiar benchmarks. It also suggests
that future evaluations should publish complete preprocessing choices and resource costs, not only
the highest F1-score.
Two 2025 studies extend the discussion toward realistic adaptation. Ladle targets a setting with
many log types produced by different components. It embeds overlapping log segments with a
sentence transformer, compares them with type-specific reference data, and recenters anomaly
scores so that variation levels are comparable across log types. New reference records can be added
to adapt to data drift without retraining the language model [14]. LogSentry combines Bidirectional
Encoder Representations from Transformers with contrastive learning and a retrieval-augmented k-
nearest-neighbor component, joining a learned classifier with similarity to stored examples [15].
Both approaches separate general language representation from a local reference or retrieval
mechanism. This design may be valuable in changing systems because local evidence can be
updated more easily than a large neural model. However, Ladle's evaluation used injected anomalies
when sufficient real failures were unavailable, and retrieval-based methods depend on the quality
and privacy of stored examples.
Taken together, recent studies show progress in semantic representation, reduced dependence on
labeled data, parser-free processing, cross-system learning, and adaptation. They do not establish a
single universally superior architecture. Public datasets differ in anomaly ratio, message stability,
label granularity, and sequence construction, while industrial logs add confidential fields, component
heterogeneity, software updates, and limited fault labels. The strongest conclusion is therefore
methodological: an anomaly detector is a pipeline, and its result reflects the interaction among data
source, parsing or tokenization, windows, representation, model, threshold, and metric.
5. Limitations and prospects
The first limitation is instability in log format and meaning. Developers modify logging statements,
components are upgraded, identifiers change, and new services appear. A parser trained on an earlier
version may split one event into several templates or merge events that should remain distinct. Even
when a parser scores highly against a template benchmark, it may remove precisely the distinction
required by the anomaly detector [3]. Raw-text models avoid template identifiers but are not
immune to drift because their token distributions and normal contexts also change. Future systems
need monitoring for representation drift, controlled updates, and rollback when an update causes a
true fault to be treated as normal. Incremental reference stores, as used by Ladle, offer one practical
direction because they can incorporate a new normal state without full neural retraining [14].
A second limitation is the scarcity and quality of labels. Many logs are labeled only at the session
or machine level, so the exact event responsible for a failure is unknown. Labels may be produced
from tickets, alerts, or injected faults and can contain delays or mistakes. Severe class imbalance
encourages a detector to favor normal behavior. Semi-supervised and self-supervised learning

reduce annotation needs [7, 10], but they also rely on assumptions about normal data and
augmentations. If the normal training set contains hidden failures, the model may learn to accept
them. If pseudo-labels are wrong, the model may amplify the error. Future research should report
uncertainty, use active learning to prioritize informative cases for expert labeling, and distinguish
unknown anomalies from simple low-confidence predictions.
Window design and evaluation create a third set of problems. A window that is too short loses
context; one that is too long delays alerts and mixes separate processes. Fixed, sliding, session, and
identifier-based windows can produce different sample counts and different levels of class
imbalance from the same raw file. Random train-test splits may place nearly identical neighboring
windows in both sets. Metrics can also hide operational costs. F1-score gives equal weight to
precision and recall, but an operations team may care more about avoiding missed critical failures or
limiting daily false alerts. The empirical comparison by Yu et al. demonstrates how dataset
characteristics, preprocessing choices, and computational cost can affect conclusions [13]. Future
studies should use time-based and cross-version validation, report event- and incident-level metrics,
measure delay, and publish the threshold-selection process.
Generalization across systems remains limited. Event identifiers have no shared meaning across
projects, and natural-language similarity does not guarantee operational similarity. Transfer methods
such as LogETA and LogGT model timing, relationships, or boundaries, but they still require a
credible connection between source and target data. A model trained on a supercomputer may not
recognize anomalies in a web application whose failures are expressed through response codes and
traces. Cross-system research should separate transfer without target labels, transfer with a small
target sample, and full target retraining. Multi-source monitoring could also combine logs with
metrics, traces, topology, and change records, reducing the pressure on text alone to explain the
system state.
Computational cost and interpretability affect whether a model can move from a research
prototype to an operational production environment. Large transformer models can improve
representation quality but may increase training cost, inference latency, energy use, and dependence
on specialized hardware.
A high anomaly score without a reason does not tell an engineer which component failed or
which event transition was important. Lightweight baselines and the improved PCA result show that
efficiency should be treated as a performance dimension [8]. Character-based OneLog suggests that
end-to-end representations can preserve information useful for parsing and anomaly detection, but
these internal representations still require separate explanation and validation [9]. Future detectors
should return supporting events, influential tokens or parameters, similar historical incidents, and
calibrated confidence. Retrieval-augmented methods can support this goal by presenting comparable
stored cases, provided that retrieval does not simply repeat incorrect or outdated labels [15].
Privacy and security are often underemphasized. Logs may contain user names, addresses,
request paths, query content, tokens, device identifiers, or internal network details. Centralizing logs
and retaining examples for retrieval can increase the damage of a data leak. Aggressive masking
protects privacy but may remove the parameter pattern that signals an attack. Future work should
examine privacy-preserving tokenization, access control, retention limits, encrypted processing, and
federated or distributed learning. Models must also be protected from log injection and poisoning,
because an attacker may create repeated malicious messages that are gradually learned as normal.
These issues connect anomaly detection with governance and secure software operations, not only
predictive accuracy.

A promising future direction is an adaptive hybrid pipeline. Self-supervised representation
learning can use large collections of unlabeled normal data, while a lightweight statistical or
retrieval layer can be updated quickly for a particular component. Parsed templates can describe
stable structure, and selected raw parameters can preserve numerical and textual evidence. Online
drift monitoring can decide when the reference set, threshold, or model should be updated. Cross-
system pre-training can provide a starting representation, but validation on target-system data should
precede deployment. Finally, explanations and human feedback should be built into the detection
loop. Progress should be measured by timely and actionable incident support, not only by a
benchmark F1-score.
6. Conclusion
This paper reviewed machine learning-based anomaly detection in software system logs. Raw logs
contain structured headers, semi-structured messages, changing parameters, and event relationships
that must be converted into useful model inputs. The review compared parsing, window
construction, count features, semantic embeddings, classical machine learning, sequence models,
transformers, contrastive learning, semi-supervised learning, and parser-free methods. Recent
studies demonstrate advances in semantic representation, reduced dependence on labels, cross-
system transfer, and adaptation to multiple log types. They also show that complex deep models are
not automatically superior to well-designed classical baselines. Parsing distinguishability, feature
choice, window length, class balance, labels, thresholds, and evaluation splits can be as important as
model architecture. Taken together, the reviewed evidence indicates that reliable log anomaly
detection depends on the entire processing pipeline rather than on the detector alone. Parsing and
tokenization determine which details remain available, window construction defines the context
presented to the model, and threshold selection affects the balance between missed anomalies and
false alarms. Classical methods remain useful when computational efficiency, limited labels, and
straightforward interpretation are priorities, whereas deep, contrastive, self-supervised, and parser-
free methods offer greater capacity to learn semantic and sequential patterns. Cross-system transfer
and retrieval-based adaptation provide additional options for changing environments, but their
effectiveness depends on differences in logging practices, data drift, and the quality of target or
reference data. Practical evaluation should therefore consider time-aware or cross-version splits,
label quality, detection delay, resource cost, and operational interpretability in addition to
conventional accuracy metrics. Privacy and security also require attention because logs may contain
sensitive identifiers and may themselves be affected by poisoning or malicious injection. Future
systems should combine self-supervised learning, online updates, cross-system adaptation, retrieval,
and interpretable evidence while protecting sensitive log data. Understanding these methodological
choices helps connect software operational data with data-science methods and supports more
reliable alerts and maintenance decisions.
