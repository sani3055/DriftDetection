Cybersecurity
Cross: a cloud-native approach to automated 
remediation and self-healing in cyber-physical 
systems
Obinna Johnphill1, Ali Safaa Sadiq1*  , Omprakash Kaiwartya1 and Mohammed Adam Taheir2 
Abstract 
Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) operate in increasingly complex and security-critical environments where system 
faults, misconfigurations, and cyberattacks can compromise safety, availability, and operational integrity. This paper 
presents CROSS (Cross-platform Remediation and Observability Self-Healing System), a cloud-native, cross-platform 
approach that extends the self-healing paradigm beyond anomaly detection to encompass autonomous, security-
aware remediation. Building upon the Log Intelligence and Self-Healing System (LISH) (Johnphill et al. 2023a), which 
utilised CountVectorizer and Multinomial Naive Bayes (MNB) for log-based anomaly classification, CROSS introduces 
a policy-driven remediation layer that executes context-specific recovery actions such as service restarts, system 
updates, device reboots, and configuration enforcement across Android, Linux, macOS, and Windows. Prometheus-
based observability (Pai and Srinivas 2024) provides fine-grained telemetry on anomalies and remedial actions, ena-
bling continuous monitoring, auditability, and adaptive security governance. Experimental evaluation demonstrates 
measurable reductions in mean time to recovery (MTTR) and improvements in anomaly containment and resilience 
across heterogeneous CPS environments.
Although CROSS includes mechanisms that are applicable to cybersecurity scenarios, the present evaluation 
focuses on operational anomalies rather than explicit attack-induced behaviours. Accordingly, its cybersecurity 
relevance is framed as an architectural capability, with empirical security benchmarking identified as future 
work. The proposed approach bridges the gap between anomaly detection and active cyber defence, embedding 
explainable, automated remediation within the operational lifecycle of CPS.

Introduction
Cyber-Physical Systems (CPS) underpin critical national 
infrastructure across sectors such as healthcare, trans -
portation, energy, and manufacturing. As these systems 
become more interconnected and software-dependent, 
they inherit a growing range of vulnerabilities that expose 
them to both accidental faults and deliberate cyberat -
tacks (Johnphill et  al. 2023a ; Loh and Thing 2023). The 
convergence of operational technology (OT) and infor -
mation technology (IT) has expanded the attack surface, 
allowing misconfigurations or malware to cascade across

interdependent subsystems, compromising safety and

Traditional fault-tolerance mechanisms restore func -
tionality after benign faults but are often inadequate 
against security-induced anomalies. Hence, the ability 
to autonomously detect, diagnose, and respond to inci -
dents is essential for maintaining resilient CPS. Advances 
in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) 
now enable adaptive fault management capable of oper -
ating with minimal human intervention (Cheng et  al. 
2023). These techniques allow CPS to identify deviations 
from normal behaviour, classify anomalies, and initiate 
corrective actions that preserve operational continuity 
even under hostile conditions.
Self-healing systems have emerged as a promising para-
digm for autonomously mitigating both technical faults 
and security breaches. A self-healing CPS continuously 
monitors operational telemetry, detects anomalies, and 
performs automated recovery to maintain service qual -
ity. However, many existing approaches employ static, 
pre-defined responses that are insufficient for dynamic, 
multi-platform environments (Vervaet 2021; Naqvi et al. 
2022). This rigidity limits responsiveness during unex -
pected faults or cyber incidents, highlighting the need 
for flexible, intelligent remediation strategies that operate 
seamlessly across diverse operating contexts.
Recent developments in cloud computing, edge intelli -
gence, and container orchestration now make it feasible 
to execute remediation processes at scale. Cloud plat -
forms enable rapid deployment and telemetry analysis, 
while edge computing reduces latency by enabling local -
ised decision-making for time-sensitive applications such 
as autonomous vehicles and medical devices. By combin -
ing these paradigms, self-healing CPS can detect anom -
alies in near real time and respond promptly through 
distributed remediation workflows. This hybrid approach 
not only improves recovery speed but also enhances 
resilience by ensuring isolated compromises do not esca -
late into systemic failures.
Building on this foundation, we introduce CROSS, 
the Cross-platform Remediation and Observability 
Self-Healing System. CROSS extends our previous Log 
Intelligence and Self-Healing (LISH) framework, which 
employed CountVectorizer and Multinomial Naïve Bayes 
(MNB) to classify anomalies from multi-platform sys -
tem logs. While LISH focused on fault detection, CROSS 
operationalises the post-detection phase by autono -
mously executing platform-specific remedial actions 
across Android, Linux, macOS, and Windows environ -
ments. In addition, CROSS integrates Prometheus-
based observability to expose real-time telemetry of 
detected anomalies, remediation events, and system 
recovery outcomes. This facilitates auditability and aligns 
the framework with modern DevSecOps monitoring 
standards.
Although this study uses general-purpose operat -
ing systems as representative CPS platforms, the archi -
tecture and methodology are transferable to industrial 
CPS contexts. The operating-system layer in controllers, 
embedded devices, and production networks exhibits 
comparable behavioural patterns and failure signatures, 
making OS-level anomaly classification a scalable proxy 
for CPS-level diagnostics. CROSS therefore bridges con -
ventional IT observability with OT resilience, offering 
a unified pathway toward autonomous, cross-domain 
self-healing.
This research addresses two key questions: (1) Can 
a cross-platform self-healing system autonomously 
detect and remediate both operational and security-
related faults in real time using only preprocessed log 
data? (2) How effectively can remediation be executed 
across heterogeneous environments under constraints 
such as missing tooling, sandboxed processes, or limited 
connectivity?
From these, two hypotheses are proposed: (H1) A log-
driven self-healing system can autonomously execute 
OS-specific corrective actions with minimal human over-
sight while maintaining secure operational continuity. 
(H2) Platform-specific limitations (for example, unavail -
able systemd, PowerShell, or ADB) can be mitigated 
through simulation and fallback mechanisms to sustain 
uninterrupted remediation.
This paper makes the following contributions:
• CROSS Framework: A cloud-native, cross-platform 
self-healing system integrating log-based anomaly 
classification with automated, policy-driven remedia-
tion.
• Cyber-Resilience Orientation: Embeds security 
awareness and autonomous defence within the reme-
diation workflow.
• Integrated Observability: Provides fine-grained moni-
toring and transparency through Prometheus metrics 
and Grafana (Siddiqui et al. 2023) dashboards.
• Adaptive Fallback Logic: Introduces simulated reme -
diation routines for constrained or tool-limited envi -
ronments.
• Scientific Evaluation: Demonstrates efficiency across 
four operating systems using metrics such as preci -
sion, recall, MTTR, and resource impact.

Related work
Self-healing and resilience strategies have long been 
explored across critical domains such as power distribu -
tion, cloud computing, and CPS. Early approaches pre -
dominantly addressed safety and fault tolerance rather 
than cybersecurity, focusing on restoring service avail -
ability after physical or software faults. However, with the 
increasing convergence of operational technology (OT) 
and information technology (IT), the boundary between 
safety faults and cyber-induced anomalies has become 
blurred, necessitating self-healing mechanisms capable of 
addressing both.
Fault tolerance and self‑healing in power and distributed 
systems
In the context of power systems, fault detection and 
recovery mechanisms have been well studied. Arefifar 
et al. (2023) proposed a set of control actions, including 
grid reconfiguration, distributed generation (DG) man -
agement, load adjustment, and reactive power control, 
to restore service after grid faults. Subsequent work by 
Wang et  al. (2019) optimised load restoration priori -
ties to maximise service continuity under constrained 
resources. These self-healing control strategies empha -
sise fault isolation and load balancing to maintain reli -
ability in safety-critical systems.
Although these studies significantly contributed to 
resilience in physical infrastructures, they primarily tar -
get deterministic faults rather than adversarial or data-
driven anomalies. The absence of adaptive, data-centric 
recovery approaches limits their effectiveness against 
cyber disruptions, where malicious interference can 
mimic or exacerbate normal fault patterns.
Resilience in cloud‑native and containerised environments
Self-healing in cloud computing environments has 
evolved through automated failover, load balancing, and 
container orchestration. Pakrijauskas and Mazeika (2022) 
examined the availability of stateful microservices during 
failover, highlighting Kubernetes as a key enabler of auto-
mated recovery. Load balancing and database resilience 
mechanisms, such as LABAREDA (Marinho et al. 2018) 
and Hihooi (Georgiou et al. 2019), further improved elas-
ticity and availability through predictive performance 
modelling and replica routing.
Kubernetes has emerged as the de facto standard 
for container orchestration, with built-in self-healing 
capabilities including pod restart, health probing, 
and automated service recovery (Cloud Native 2025). 
Shabariram et al. (2024) demonstrated how cloud deploy-
ment strategies such as Blue-Green and Canary models 
significantly reduce downtime and enhance reliability in 
distributed applications. Similarly, Jhuang and Wu (2022) 
applied Kubernetes load balancing to achieve seamless 
IP failover for VoIP services, ensuring high availability in 
latency-sensitive contexts.
While these architectures automate recovery at the 
infrastructure layer, they do not integrate intelligent 
fault reasoning or log-driven remediation. CROSS builds 
upon these foundations by linking anomaly classifica -
tion directly to OS-level corrective actions, extending 
traditional DevOps automation toward intelligent cyber 
resilience.
High availability, load balancing, and observability
Several studies have addressed resilience through 
improved resource distribution and observability. 
Nugroho et  al. (2023) developed a distributed Round-
Robin load balancer for automatic proctoring systems, 
achieving a 22% reduction in response time and con -
sistent performance during failover. Malhotra et  al. 
(2023) further evaluated near-zero-downtime solutions 
for enterprise systems, underscoring the importance of 
redundancy and clustering for service continuity.
More recent work has focused on observability and 
continuous monitoring as complementary to self-healing. 
Siddiqui et al. (2023) and Akinbolaji et al. (2024) explored 
the integration of Prometheus and Grafana within 
DevOps pipelines to improve proactive detection and 
response. Pai and Srinivas (2024) demonstrated that inte-
grating multi-source observability tools enhances fault 
traceability and reduces mean time to recovery (MTTR). 
These findings support CROSS’s design choice to embed 
Prometheus metrics directly into the remediation work -
flow, enabling real-time feedback, auditing, and adaptive 
control.
Cybersecurity‑oriented self‑healing and anomaly detection
Traditional self-healing frameworks often assume faults 
are benign. However, the increasing frequency of cyber -
attacks has prompted research into systems that can 
autonomously detect and respond to malicious activ -
ity. Cheng et  al. (2023) reviewed AI for IT Operations 
(Cheng et al. 2023) (AIOps) and emphasised the role of 
machine learning in identifying cyber anomalies in cloud 
platforms. Ali et  al. (2025) presented an AI-powered 
anomaly detection approach for proactive fault diagno -
sis and self-healing, integrating ML-based detection with 
security-oriented response logic. Loh and Thing (2023) 
proposed mechanisms for enhancing CPS resilience

through implicit guarantees against adversarial disrup -
tions, reinforcing the importance of combining security 
and reliability.
Despite these advancements, few existing solutions 
bridge detection and automated remediation in a unified 
cross-platform system. Most frameworks either focus 
on predictive monitoring or rely on manual incident 
response. CROSS addresses this gap by coupling light -
weight log-based anomaly detection with immediate, 
context-aware remediation actions. This makes it capable 
of handling both operational faults and potential attack-
induced anomalies within a single adaptive architecture.
From LISH to CROSS: motivations for evolution
The Log Intelligence and Self-Healing System (LISH) by 
Johnphill et  al. (2024) served as the foundation for this 
work. LISH applied the CountVectorizer and MNB to 
classify system logs into normal, warning, and error cat -
egories across multiple platforms. While it achieved high 
recall for anomaly detection, it lacked a mechanism to 
execute remediation actions or provide runtime observa -
bility. CROSS extends LISH by introducing a remediation 
layer that maps detected anomalies to platform-specific 
corrective actions, executed automatically via unified 
handlers. It integrates telemetry using Prometheus and 
Grafana, offering transparency and traceability; key 
requirements for cyber-resilient architectures.
By bridging detection and remediation, CROSS trans -
forms passive fault monitoring into active cyber defence. 
Its design reflects a broader shift in CPS research from 
reliability-focused safety assurance toward intelligent, 
autonomous resilience capable of addressing both faults 
and attacks in real time.
Design and operational workflow of the CROSS 
framework
To enable intelligent fault tolerance across diverse com -
puting platforms, we present CROSS, a unified self-
healing framework that builds upon our previous LISH 
system. It leverages preprocessed and labelled system 
logs for machine learning-driven anomaly detection and 
integrates rule-based remediation logic and real-time 
observability. CROSS supports Android, Linux, macOS, 
and Windows environments and uses predefined thresh -
olds for errors and warnings to trigger platform-specific 
actions, offering continuous insight into system health 
and automated recovery from faults.
Figure 1 illustrates the CROSS architecture. Logs from 
heterogeneous platforms are preprocessed and vector -
ised using CountVectorizer, then classified via an MNB 
model into normal, warning, or error states. Events 
exceeding threshold levels trigger the Remediation 
Action Dispatcher, which maps to OS-specific recovery 
handlers: Android uses ADB to clear caches and reboot 
devices; Linux systems use package updates and service 
restarts; macOS performs simulated system updates and 
service recoveries; Windows executes PowerShell-based 
maintenance scripts.
Metrics such as anomaly counts and remedial events 
are exported to Prometheus and visualised in Grafana. 
The system comprises five layers: data acquisition, feature 
engineering, anomaly inference, threshold evaluation, 
and remediation. Core principles include lightweight 
anomaly detection, cross-platform adaptability, automa -
tion through threshold logic, and full observability to 
support transparency and auditability.
Figure 2 outlines the CROSS workflow. After anomaly 
classification, if error or warning thresholds are breached, 
the system identifies the OS and selects an appropriate 
remediation strategy. If the OS is unsupported, execution 
is gracefully skipped to avoid unnecessary system modifi -
cation. Supported platforms execute a defined remedia -
tion set, including:
• System updates: Apply OS-specific patches and 
updates to fix vulnerabilities.
• Service restarts: Restart unresponsive services to 
restore normal operations.
• Disk clean-up: Remove temporary or obsolete files to 
recover storage.
• Resource reallocation: Free memory or CPU to opti -
mise responsiveness.
Platform-specific tools such as ADB (Android), sys -
temctl (Linux), PowerShell (Windows), or simulated 
shell commands (macOS) are abstracted into an OS 
registry, ensuring fault handling remains consistent 
and explainable across environments. This architecture 
decouples detection, decision-making, and execution, 
promoting adaptive, reproducible, and scalable recovery 
across cyber-physical systems.

how CROSS responds to both benign system faults and

Feature engineering and model justification
CROSS leverages the CountVectorizer to transform 
unstructured system logs into sparse bag-of-words repre-
sentations. This choice enables computational efficiency 
and high interpretability, essential for explainable self-
healing systems. The MNB classifier was selected for its 
proven robustness, low computational cost, and supe -
rior recall in log-based anomaly detection (Vervaet 2021; 
Ali et  al. 2025). In cybersecurity contexts, high recall 
ensures that rare or emerging threats are more likely to 
be detected, even at the cost of slightly lower precision.
Alternative classifiers such as Support Vector Machines 
(SVM) and Random Forests were evaluated during pre -
liminary testing; however, these models exhibited higher 
latency and resource overhead, which made them less 
suitable for real-time self-healing pipelines. MNB there -
fore offers an optimal balance between accuracy, speed, 
and explainability.

Evaluation setup and experimental design
Preprocessed log datasets were obtained from Android, 
Linux, macOS, and Windows systems using the LISH 
preprocessing pipeline. Each log was labelled as nor -
mal, warning, or error. Additionally, security-
related anomalies, such as unauthorised access attempts, 
repeated authentication failures, and abnormal network 
activity, were injected to simulate cyberattack conditions.
For each platform, the following steps were executed: 
1. Train the MNB classifier using preprocessed logs.
2. Stream incoming logs through CROSS’s anomaly 
detector.
3. Accumulate error and warning counts over a sliding 
window.
4. Trigger remediation actions when thresholds are 
breached.
5. Record performance metrics and Prometheus telem -
etry.
Each experiment was executed ten times per platform to 
ensure statistical robustness. Mean values and standard 
deviations were computed for recovery time, detection 
accuracy, and system overhead. Remedial actions were 
executed either natively (for example, via systemctl, 
adb, or PowerShell) or in simulated mode where 
native tooling was unavailable.
Observability metrics, including anomaly rates, action 
frequencies, and system health states, were exposed 
through Prometheus and visualised with Grafana for 
temporal and comparative analysis.
Comparative baseline analysis
To contextualise CROSS’s performance, two baselines 
were implemented:
• Rule-Based System: A static remediation engine map-
ping specific log patterns to predefined shell com -
mands.
• Manual Script Execution: Conventional administra -
tor-led corrective actions triggered upon manual log 
inspection.
Metrics evaluated include precision, recall, Mean 
Time to Recovery (MTTR), CPU/memory overhead, 
and fallback activation rate. CROSS achieved a 
mean reduction in mean-time-to-recovery (MTTR) 
of 37% when compared against the manual script 
baseline, demonstrating significant improvements in 
recovery efficiency. Importantly, containerised isola -
tion ensured minimal system interference.

Architecture overview
CROSS is implemented in Python with modular archi -
tecture. A central registry maps OS types to their 
remediation handlers using Python decorators. This 
enables dynamic dispatch of actions tailored to each 
environment:
• Android: Uses ADB for cache clearing and reboot. 
Simulated if ADB is unavailable.
• Linux: Executes updates, service restarts, and disk 
clean-up. Falls back to simulated execution if sys -
temctl is missing.
• macOS: Performs simulated updates, service 
restarts, and clean-up.
• Windows: Uses PowerShell scripts for maintenance. 
Falls back to simulated logic if needed.
Logs are structured into tabular form using Python and

Baselines and benchmark protocol
Baselines. We evaluate CROSS against two non-learn -
ing baselines:
• B1: Manual Scripts. System administrators inspect 
logs and execute corrective scripts (service restarts, 
cache/disk cleanup, configuration reloads) by hand. 
This represents the conventional, non-automated 
workflow.
• B2: Static Rule-Based Remediation. A determinis -
tic engine that maps log patterns to actions using a 
hand-crafted ruleset. No statistical classification is 
used.
Rule-base derivation (for B2). Rules were derived 
from the same preprocessed log corpus used by CROSS 
(see Data Availability; derived from Loghub  (LOGPAI 
2023; He et al. 2023) and our Zenodo release (Johnphill 
and Sadiq 2024)). We extracted frequent error/warn -
ing templates and service names, then associated each 
template with the most common successful corrective 
action observed during pilot runs. 
1. Template mining: tokenise logs and keep lines 
labelled warning/error; normalise dynamic fields 
(PIDs, IPs, timestamps) to placeholders; count tem -
plate frequencies.
2. Trigger terms: retain templates containing service/
process tokens (for example, systemd, sshd, 
docker, nginx, winlogon) and failure terms 
(for example, failed, timeout, unreachable, 
permission denied).
3. Action mapping: map each template to one corrective 
action chosen from A ={ restart service, reload con -
fig, clear cache, restart device, enforce policy, no-op}.
4. Conflict resolution: if multiple actions match a tem -
plate, pick the one with highest empirical success 
rate during pilot runs; break ties with least-disruptive 
action.

Evaluating observability of CROSS framework
CROSS integrates observability using Prometheus for 
metrics collection and Grafana for dashboard visualisa -
tion. Observability, particularly within Kubernetes and 
distributed systems, refers to the ability to infer the inter-
nal state of a system solely from its external outputs. It 
goes beyond traditional monitoring by offering a richer, 
more structured method of understanding system behav-
iour, leveraging primary telemetry signals, namely logs, 
metrics, and traces, alongside auxiliary indicators such as 
health checks. These signals operate in unison to deliver 
comprehensive insight into application performance and 
the operational state of the system (Siddiqui et al. 2023; 
Hallur 2022; Akinbolaji et  al. 2024; Pai and Srinivas 
2024). Modern microservices architectures, cloud-native 
deployments, and complex distributed environments 
present challenges that conventional monitoring tools 
often struggle to overcome. Observability bridges this 
gap by providing a holistic perspective on system behav -
iour under real-world conditions. It enables engineering 
teams to proactively detect anomalies, trace problems to 
their root cause, and significantly reduce both the mean 
time to detect (MTTD) and the mean time to resolve 
(MTTR) faults within the system (Siddiqui et  al. 2023; 
Hallur 2022; Akinbolaji et  al. 2024; Pai and Srinivas 
2024).
In addition to the Prometheus-Grafana stack currently 
integrated within CROSS, further observability can be 
achieved by incorporating emerging tools such as Loki 
for centralised log aggregation and HashiCorp Vault for 
managing secure telemetry pipelines, as recommended 
in recent observability studies (Akinbolaji et al. 2024; Pai 
and Srinivas 2024). These tools contribute enriched con -
text, support trace correlation, and provide secure access 
to system-level metrics, which are particularly impor -
tant in multi-tenant CPS environments or scenarios 
involving sensitive infrastructure. Their inclusion would 
strengthen traceability, enhance compliance, and support 
production-grade deployment of CROSS. The founda -
tional pillars of observability, logs, metrics, and traces, 
play distinct yet complementary roles. Logs provide 
timestamped, immutable records of discrete events that 
offer valuable context around system actions and failures. 
Metrics represent quantitative measurements, such as 
CPU usage, memory consumption, and request through -
put, which together reflect the real-time performance 
characteristics of a system. Traces capture the end-to-
end journey of a request across multiple services within 
a distributed architecture, revealing critical insight into 
inter-service communication paths and potential latency 
bottlenecks (Hallur 2022).
The implementation of observability in this self-healing 
system leverages a monitoring and visualisation stack 
built around Prometheus and Grafana. Prometheus is an 
open-source monitoring system designed for time-series 
data collection and analysis. Within the Kubernetes envi -
ronment, it is configured to scrape metrics from pods, 
nodes, and internal components of the remediation 
framework. These metrics encompass system resource 
usage, application health indicators, and frequency of 
observed errors (Siddiqui et al. 2023; Hallur 2022; Akin -
bolaji et al. 2024; Pai and Srinivas 2024).To visualise this 
data, Grafana is deployed alongside Prometheus, ena -
bling real-time and historical views through customisable 
dashboards. This integration allows stakeholders to mon-
itor platform-specific error rates, CPU and memory utili -
sation, remediation success ratios, and response latencies 
between error detection and recovery. Grafana’s intuitive 
interface complements Prometheus by offering flexible

Discussion
The CROSS framework addresses critical challenges in 
the design and deployment of self-healing systems, par -
ticularly within heterogeneous CPS environments. Unlike 
existing solutions that tend to focus on either detection 
or remediation within narrow operating boundaries, 
CROSS presents a holistic, platform-agnostic approach 
that fuses detection, remediation, and observability into 
a cohesive, modular architecture. It not only introduces 
a practical and extensible design for autonomous reme -
diation but also raises the benchmark for cross-platform 
self-healing systems by combining fault detection, execu -
tion logic, and system introspection within a lightweight, 
scalable solution. While CROSS currently leverages 
lightweight probabilistic models for anomaly detec -
tion and employs threshold-based remediation, future 
enhancements may draw on advances in AIOps (Artifi -
cial Intelligence for IT Operations), as explored by Cheng 
et al. (2023). Their review highlights the growing role of 
machine learning in cloud-native diagnostics, root cause 
analysis, and intelligent automation; directions that align 
closely with CROSS’s development roadmap, including 
reinforcement learning or predictive models to support 
more context-aware remediation decisions. This sec -
tion critically evaluates the broader implications of the 
framework, distinguishing it from existing solutions and 
exploring its potential real-world applications.
CROSS demonstrates considerable adaptability 
through its successful deployment of remediation logic 
across Android, Linux, macOS, and Windows environ -
ments. This flexibility is achieved through the use of a 
dynamic registry for OS-specific handlers, which sup -
ports extensibility by allowing additional platforms to be 
incorporated without restructuring the core logic. In this 
regard, CROSS outperforms other solutions that are typi-
cally bound to a single operating system or dependent on 
rigid, static rule sets. Implementation testing revealed 
meaningful insights and challenges. For example, the 
system effectively managed variations in available tooling 
and operating constraints across platforms, particularly 
those arising from containerisation, such as process ID 
(PID) isolation and limited access to system toolchains. 
These operational conditions underscored the impor -
tance of robust fallback routines and precise error han -
dling. CROSS addresses this by simulating executions 
where necessary and maintaining comprehensive logging, 
thereby ensuring that remediation attempts are traceable 
and continue even under less-than-ideal conditions.
In terms of strategic positioning, CROSS aligns with 
emerging industry trends favouring autonomous, AI-
driven infrastructure. Its integration with standard 
observability stacks, such as Prometheus and Grafana, 
supports low-latency recovery loops and quantifi -
able remediation metrics. Its lightweight classification 
approach and threshold-triggered execution logic make 
it particularly well-suited to resource-constrained envi -
ronments, such as embedded or edge CPS deployments. 
Nonetheless, the framework has its limitations. Chief 
among these is the inability to invoke certain native 
remediation tools within containerised environments, an 
issue stemming from restricted process control and privi-
lege boundaries. There may also be minor delays intro -
duced by log processing or orchestration overhead. These 
limitations are offset to a degree by the system’s modular 
design, transparent logging architecture, and OS-aware 
remediation logic. Future work may include the devel -
opment of native agents or elevated runtime modules to 
bridge these gaps more effectively.
Adaptive fallback logic and CPS‑relevant scenarios
Adaptive fallback logic provides resilience in  situa -
tions where standard remediation routines cannot be 
executed due to unavailable tooling, permission restric -
tions, or degraded system states. Each remediation 
action is associated with one or more fallback alterna -
tives, organised in a prioritised sequence. If an action 
fails, for example, due to missing binaries or command 
rejection, the fallback is automatically attempted, ena -
bling CROSS to maintain operational continuity. In 
CPS contexts, fallback logic is particularly valuable.
For example, if a soft reset of a network service on 
an industrial gateway fails, CROSS may revert to a 
bounded interface reset, followed by a policy-defined 
escalation such as transferring the affected node into 
a maintenance-safe state. Similarly, should an edge 
controller fail to terminate an unresponsive process, 
CROSS may execute a container-level restart or, in 
highly constrained real-time environments, trigger a 
supervisory watchdog rather than performing system-
wide restarts. These examples demonstrate that the 
fallback logic extends beyond conventional IT-centric

remediation, enabling CPS-specific recovery behav -
iours such as industrial fail-safe mechanisms, Remote 
Terminal Unit (RTU) state reinitialisation, and Pro -
grammable Logic Controller (PLC)-supervised inhibi -
tion states.
Policy‑driven remediation
CROSS employs a policy-driven remediation layer 
that systematically maps detected anomalies to plat -
form-specific corrective actions. Policies are specified 
declaratively using a lightweight rules format, allow -
ing operators to define conditions, triggers, and asso -
ciated remediation procedures without modifying 
source code. A generic policy entry consists of: (i) an 
anomaly signature or label; (ii) contextual constraints 
such as platform, severity, or resource thresholds; and 
(iii) a sequence of remediation actions with optional 
escalation logic. At runtime, CROSS matches predicted 
anomalies with the policy catalogue to determine an 
appropriate remediation plan.
Policies are evaluated in a context-aware manner: for 
example, a high-CPU anomaly on Linux may trigger 
process throttling, whereas an equivalent anomaly on 
Android may invoke app-level restarts due to platform 
constraints. Policy inheritance allows common remedia -
tion strategies to be specialised for individual OS fami -
lies, ensuring consistency while enabling fine-grained 
platform tailoring. To illustrate, a network-degradation 
anomaly may trigger different sequences depending on 
execution context: (a) on a supervisory industrial gate -
way, CROSS may restart affected services or reset net -
work interfaces; (b) on a safety-critical device, policies 
may restrict CROSS to non-intrusive diagnostics only, 
deferring intrusive actions to a supervisory controller. 
This separation ensures that remediation remains aligned 
with operational and safety requirements across hetero -
geneous CPS environments.
Interpreting resource impact in representative CPS 
hardware classes
The reported CPU and memory overheads must be 
interpreted in relation to the heterogeneous hardware 
found in CPS deployments. While CROSS was evaluated 
on general-purpose computing platforms, many CPS 
nodes, including PLCs, RTUs, and embedded protection 
devices-operate with significantly constrained resources 
and rely on real-time firmware rather than a full OS 
stack.
The observed overheads (typically under X% CPU and 
Y  MB memory) are therefore most appropriate for CPS 
components with an IT-oriented architecture, such as 
industrial edge gateways, substation automation servers, 
medical IoT hubs, or container-capable embedded Linux 
devices. Legacy PLCs and microcontroller-based systems 
are not target deployment points; instead, CROSS can 
function as a supervisory node augmenting such devices. 
In these configurations, resource impact is confined to 
the supervisory host, while CPS devices benefit indi -
rectly through reduced mean-time-to-recovery (MTTR), 
improved anomaly visibility, and consistent remediation 
orchestration. This contextualisation clarifies that CROSS 
complements a layered CPS architecture: lightweight 
schemes such as LISH serve highly constrained environ -
ments, whereas CROSS delivers comprehensive remedia-
tion on more capable nodes within the CPS hierarchy.
CROSS exhibits significant potential for deployment 
in mission-critical domains such as healthcare moni -
toring, intelligent transport systems, smart grids, and 
industrial automation. Its observability model provides 
real-time insights into fault patterns, recovery latency, 
and overall system health. This, in turn, strengthens post-
incident analysis, regulatory compliance, and operational 
accountability-features that are vital for environments 
where system reliability is non-negotiable. To deepen this 
perspective, the following subsection critically reflects on 
the limitations of using OS-level telemetry as an abstrac -
tion for CPS state, and how this impacts diagnosis and 
remediation in control-dominated environments.
A limitation of the present study is that CROSS was 
not evaluated directly on physical CPS or OT devices 
such as PLCs, RTUs, or protection relays. The experi -
ments relied on OS-level telemetry as a unifying abstrac -
tion for demonstrating cross-platform anomaly detection 
and remediation. While this approach provides broad 
generalisability across heterogeneous operating systems, 
OS logs do not capture CPS-specific process deviations 
or control-layer faults observable only through field-bus 
traffic, PLC logic traces, or real-time controller telem -
etry. OS-level signals should therefore be viewed as a 
foundational but incomplete proxy for CPS behaviour. 
Future work will integrate cyber-physical system-native 
telemetry sources, including Distributed Network Pro -
tocol (DNP3) and Modbus communication events, Open 
Platform Communications Unified Architecture (OPC 
UA) process variables, and PLC execution traces, to 
enable deeper and more realistic evaluation in industrial 
environments.
Limitations of OS‑level telemetry as a proxy for CPS insight
While OS-level telemetry provides a valuable and uni -
form abstraction for cross-platform anomaly detec -
tion, its suitability as a proxy for CPS-specific process 
anomalies has inherent limitations. In many operational 
technology (OT) domains, faults manifest not in the 
general-purpose operating system but within specialised 

Page 22 of 24Johnphill et al. Cybersecurity           (2026) 9:124 
embedded subsystems. Examples include ladder-logic 
deviations in programmable logic controllers (PLCs), 
timing faults detectable only on field-bus networks (for 
example, Modbus, CAN, Profibus), or domain-specific 
measurements observed within real-time controllers and 
remote terminal units (RTUs).
Such anomalies may not propagate to the host operat -
ing system and therefore remain invisible to generic OS 
logs. CROSS addresses this by treating OS-level signals 
as a baseline source of behavioural deviation rather than 
a complete view of CPS health. This design enables broad 
deployability but does not replace the need for specialised 
telemetry in safety- or mission-critical environments. 
Future extensions could incorporate heterogeneous log 
sources such as PLC execution traces, DNP3 traffic sum -
maries, or OPC-UA telemetry, enabling a multi-layered 
view that unifies OS and control-layer anomalies. This 
hybrid approach would enhance diagnostic richness, par-
ticularly for faults arising at the physical–digital bound -
ary where OS-level signals alone are insufficient.
Deployment considerations for real‑time, deterministic, 
and air‑gapped CPS
Cyber-physical system deployments frequently oper -
ate under stringent constraints related to timing, deter -
minism, and connectivity. Systems such as industrial 
protection relays, aerospace control units, and medical 
devices often enforce fixed execution cycles, bounded 
timing jitter, and, in some domains, strictly air-gapped 
operational modes. The cloud-native design of CROSS 
does not inherently require wide-area network connec -
tivity; instead, its components can be deployed entirely 
on premises or within isolated edge clusters using light -
weight, locally managed Kubernetes container orches -
tration distributions, such as K3s, a compact Kubernetes 
implementation designed for resource-constrained and 
edge environments, and MicroK8s, a self-contained 
Kubernetes platform optimised for local, offline, and 
air-gapped deployments. These configurations preserve 
equivalent functionality to cloud-connected deployments 
while remaining compatible with security-sensitive CPS 
settings.
Real-time scheduling constraints are accommodated 
by executing anomaly detection and remediation tasks as 
background processes with bounded resource footprints, 
thereby ensuring minimal interference with time-critical 
control workloads. Where strict real-time determinism 
is required, CROSS can be configured to execute reme -
diation actions only within predefined safe execution 
windows or to defer intrusive actions to higher-level 
supervisory nodes. In fully air-gapped environments, 
all telemetry storage, policy management, and inference 
routines operate locally, eliminating any dependency 
on external cloud services. Collectively, these deploy -
ment modes extend the applicability of CROSS to legacy, 
safety-critical, and infrastructure-constrained cyber-
physical system environments.
Key Contributions. To clearly summarise its nov -
elty and impact, CROSS delivers the following core 
contributions:
• First cloud-native, cross-platform remediation frame -
work: Enables automated recovery across Android, 
Linux, macOS, and Windows, triggered via machine 
learning-based log classification.
• Integrated observability with remediation: Pro -
metheus and Grafana integration allows real-time 
inspection of errors, recovery events, and healing 
metrics.
• Simulated fallback execution: Ensures continuity 
by simulating remediation steps when native tools 
(for example, ADB or PowerShell) are unavailable in 
restricted environments.
• Modular and extensible architecture: A registry-
driven design allows easy addition of new platforms 
and remediation handlers without altering the core 
system.
• Validated against baselines: Demonstrates superior 
MTTR, precision, and recall compared to manual 
scripts and rule-based systems.
Conclusion and future work
Although CROSS integrates anomaly detection 
mechanisms that are applicable to cybersecurity 
contexts, the present evaluation focuses primarily on 
operational anomalies rather than explicit attack-
induced behaviours. As such, the cybersecurity 
contribution is framed as an architectural capabil -
ity rather than an empirically benchmarked claim. 
Evaluating CROSS using intrusion-specific datasets 
is identified as an important direction for future 
work.
This study introduced CROSS, the Cross-platform 
Remediation and Observability Self-Healing System, 
as a novel framework for autonomous fault recovery in 
cyber-physical systems. Through lightweight log-based 
anomaly detection, containerised deployment using 
Docker, scalable orchestration via Kubernetes, and an 
observability layer powered by Prometheus and Grafana, 
CROSS achieves a balanced combination of adaptability, 
transparency, and performance. Unlike many existing 
self-healing solutions confined to specific platforms or 

Page 23 of 24
Johnphill et al. Cybersecurity           (2026) 9:124 
 
toolchains, CROSS dynamically adjusts its remediation 
logic to suit Android, Linux, macOS, and Windows envi -
ronments, maintaining operational continuity even in 
constrained or hybrid execution contexts.
Beyond its practical feasibility, CROSS makes a broader 
research contribution by bridging theoretical models of 
fault tolerance with deployable, real-world self-healing 
mechanisms. It executes corrective actions autonomously 
while emitting verifiable telemetry, thereby strengthening 
both system resilience and auditability. The framework 
demonstrates that explainable log-driven learning can 
underpin secure, cross-platform self-healing architec -
tures suitable for industrial CPS domains such as smart 
grids, healthcare monitoring, and intelligent transport.
Integrated roadmap for future work
Future research will extend CROSS along several comple-
mentary directions:
• Adversarial Robustness and Security Context: Incor -
porate adversarial training and ensemble detection 
to resist evasion and poisoning attacks targeting log-
based classifiers, enabling the framework to distin -
guish operational faults from malicious anomalies.
• Reinforcement-Learning-Driven Remediation: Model 
remediation as a Markov Decision Process (MDP) 
where adaptive policies learn optimal corrective 
actions from feedback, balancing availability with 
security risk.
• Predictive and Time-Series Analytics: Employ super -
vised and temporal learning models for proactive 
fault prediction and pre-emptive remediation before 
service degradation occurs.
• Secure and Auditable Execution: Introduce sand -
boxed or privilege-restricted healing agents and 
explore blockchain-based audit trails to enhance 
the trustworthiness and verifiability of remediation 
actions.
• Edge-Cloud Hybrid Architecture: Distribute detection 
and remediation across computing tiers, latency-sen-
sitive operations at the edge and orchestration in the 
cloud, to support large-scale CPS deployments.
• Human-in-the-Loop Interfaces: Develop operator 
dashboards that allow manual override, validation, 
and contextual decision support, blending autonomy 
with expert insight.
These directions aim to evolve CROSS from an opera -
tionally self-healing engine into a fully cyber-resilient, 
explainable, and trustworthy ecosystem for heterogene -
ous CPS. The framework thus establishes a foundation 
for scalable, intelligent, and transparent self-healing 
infrastructures across both IT and OT domains.
