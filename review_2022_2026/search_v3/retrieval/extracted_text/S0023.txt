Abstract
The rapid expansion of Internet of Things (IoT) technologies in smart cities, healthcare, and industrial automation has 
intensified	the	need	for	cybersecurity	frameworks	capable	of	operating	at	scale	and	in	real	time	under	increasingly	sophis-
ticated	 threat	 conditions.	Traditional	security	 mechanisms	 and	 opaque	AI-based	 models	 are	 no	 longer	 adequate	 for	 pro-
tecting interconnected urban infrastructures, especially as regulatory and societal expectations move toward transparency 
and accountability. Although prior surveys have examined IoT security and general AI techniques, they rarely address 
the	 emerging	 role	 of	 Explainable	Artificial	Intelligence	 (XAI)	 in	 data	 mining	 for	 IoT	 cybersecurity	 or	 integrate	 recent	
advances	 in	 cognitively	 inspired	 and	 human-aligned	 explainability	 methods.	 This	 survey	 provides	 an	 up-to-date	 review	
of	 XAI-driven	 data	 mining	 approaches	 applied	 to	 IoT	 ecosystems,	 highlighting	 their	 ability	 to	 detect	 anomalies,	 inter-
pret	 complex	 sensor-driven	 behaviours,	 and	 support	 automated	 security	 decisions	 through	 transparent	 and	 interpretable	
reasoning.	The	review	identifies	critical	challenges,	including	data	privacy,	scalability,	computational	constraints,	and	the	
interpretability limitations of modern AI models. It examines how biologically inspired learning paradigms and cognitively 
grounded explanation techniques can enhance trust and situational awareness in IoT environments. Emerging technologies 
such	as	edge	intelligence,	federated	learning	(FL),	blockchain	integration,	and	quantum-assisted	analytics	are	discussed	as	
promising enablers of scalable and transparent IoT security. The survey underscores the importance of trustworthy, ethi -
cally	aligned	AI,	advocating	for	XAI	frameworks	that	enable	fair,	auditable,	and	reliable	decision-making	in	safety-critical	
infrastructure. By addressing gaps in the literature and synthesizing recent developments, this study presents a timely 
perspective	 on	 XAI,	 data	 mining,	 and	 IoT	 cybersecurity,	outlining	 future	 directions	 for	 building	 resilient,	 interpretable,	
and	human-centric	smart	city	systems.
Keywords	 Explainable	AI	(XAI)	·	IoT	·	Machine	Learning	(ML)	·	Cybersecurity	·	Smart	cities	·	Data	mining

Introduction
The IoT has rapidly reshaped modern urban environments 
by	enabling	continuous	data	collection,	large-scale	automa-
tion,	 and	 intelligent	decision-making	across	 domains	 such	
as transportation, energy distribution, environmental moni -
toring, public safety, and healthcare. The smart city systems 
are based on large numbers of interconnected sensors and 
devices	that	adjust	traffic	flows	dynamically,	optimize	energy	
use, and keep track of community health signals [1, 2]. Both 
the sheer connectivity of IoT devices and the fact that most 
of them have weak authentication protocols, low computing 
power, and little intrinsic security provide a large attack sur-
face that attackers can abuse [ 3, 4].	 Cyber	attacks	on	 IoT-
based smart cities impact the delivery of necessary services, 
undermine the security of citizens, and destabilize critical 
urban systems of infrastructure [ 5–7]. Due to the ongoing 
growth of IoT deployments, cybersecurity requirements also 
increase.	Conventional	security	systems,	such	as	rule-based	
intrusion	detection	systems	and	static	firewalls,	are	no	lon-
ger adequate for protecting against complex, dynamically 
changing cybersecurity threats [ 8]. Enhancing the security 
of	 IoT	 thus	 involves	 data-based,	 adaptive	 defense	 strate-
gies that process heterogeneous streams of data, identify 
anomalies as they occur, and respond accordingly. The use 
of	AI-	and	data	mining-driven	methodologies	has	taken	the	
focus	of	this	mission,	where	real-time	threat	detection	and	
predictive analytics over extensive and dynamically growing 
IoT ecosystems are achievable with these methods [ 9, 10]. 
Nevertheless, traditional AI models are often black boxes, 
providing	 minimal	 information	about	 the	 decision-making	
process.	 Such	 a	 transparency	deficiency	undermines	 trust,	
makes	it	difficult	to	debug	systems,	and	impedes	adherence	
to regulatory requirements for critical infrastructure [11].
XAI	specifically	is	focused	on	this	concern	and	provides	
tools	 like	 Local	Interpretable	Model-agnostic	Explanations	
(LIME) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) that ren-
der	AI	decision-making	explainable	and	responsible.	In	the	
context	of	IoT	cybersecurity,	XAI	enables	analysts	to	under-
stand	 why	 an	 anomaly	 was	 identified,	which	 key	 features	
most	affected	it,	and	whether	a	model’s	decision	is	consistent	
with domain knowledge [12]. For example, when an intrusion 
detection model raises an alarm due to odd network behavior, 
XAI	 visualizes	how	 the	 alarm	 was	 triggered	by	 abnormal	
packet timing, odd patterns of device communication, or a 
behavior that does not follow past patterns. Such interpret -
ability plays an essential role in building trust, improving 
models,	and	enabling	human	intervention	in	a	timely,	well-
informed manner. This tendency to assign greater attention 
to credible, cognitively inspired AI also underscores the 
need for transparency in automated cybersecurity mecha -
nisms. The IoT ecosystems are based on distributed sensing, 
adaptive	learning,	and	human-machine	collaboration,	charac-
teristics that parallel biological and cognitive systems in per-
ception,	reasoning,	and	decision-making.	XAI	suggests	the	
framework	required	to	ensure	that	AI-based	security	systems	
are interpretable, ethical, and consistent with human expecta-
tions and regulatory standards. Clear argumentation is most 
crucial in intelligent cities, where the outcomes of incorrect 
or	unguided	AI	decisions	may	affect	individual	security	and	
the social welfare of society.
In	this	review,	we	overview	the	cyberspace	of	IoT-based	
smart	cities,	and	study	the	potential	of	XAI-driven	data	min-
ing methods to improve the transparency, robustness, and 
interpretability	of	AI-based	defences.	We	examine	the	chal-
lenges	IoT-specific	techniques	address,	existing	weaknesses,	
and	the	extent	to	which	XAI	helps	to	enhance	accountability	
and operational trust. We also explain why modern explain-
able	 methods	 offer	greater	 resilience	 for	 smart	 city	 infra-
structure and how IoT systems in cities can be safe, reliable, 
and aligned with social values. Trustworthy AI is an essen -
tial	concept	in	 the	development	of	 IoT	 and	XAI	to	 ensure	
that	decision-making	processes	are	transparent,	ethical,	and	
accountable. With the growing reliance of innovative city 
ecosystems	 on	 autonomous	 analytical	 models,	 XAI	 offers	
the interpretability necessary to certify AI behavior, promote 
fairness, and ensure human checks and balances. As shown in 
Fig. 1,	XAI	enhances	IoT	cybersecurity	by	augmenting	deep	
learning (DL) models with transparent reasoning, allowing 
data mining techniques to contribute to a more resilient pro-
tection	framework	for	large-scale	IoT	infrastructures.

transportation, energy control, environmental monitoring, and 
citizen	security.	These	devices	are	distributed	cyber-physical	
agents that sense, infer, and collaborate in urban environ -
ments. But due to their widespread use, limited computational 
power, and heterogeneous designs, IoT systems are highly 
vulnerable to cyberattacks. Security breaches may propagate 
across interrelated subsystems, resulting in failures of critical 
infrastructure, impeding fundamental public service delivery, 
and exposing sensitive information about citizens. Maintain-
ing the dependability, security, and sustainability of smart city 
systems	thus	requires	both	strong	and	flexible	cybersecurity	
solutions that can adapt to new security challenges. Although 
there is growing concern about the security of IoT, the existing 
surveys	have	significant	gaps.	The	majority	of	reviews	rely

decentralized	architecture,	and	real-time	functionality	require-
ments. Moreover, many previous studies do not address the 
new	role	of	XAI	in	resolving	interpretability	issues	in	AI-based	
security	systems.	This	 is	 a	 severe	 constraint,	as	 black-box	
ML models, despite their power, have minimal transparency, 
which complicates alert validation, system failure diagnosis, 
and compliance with regulatory and ethical laws by operators. 
Due to rapid changes in the threat landscape, most existing 
reviews have failed to keep pace with the current state, leav-
ing	partial	and	fragmented	knowledge	of	current	XAI-enabled	
cybersecurity capabilities.
This survey addresses these gaps by providing a com -
prehensive	 and	 up-to-date	 analysis	 of	 XAI-driven	 data	
mining	 techniques	 specifically	tailored	 to	 IoT-based	smart	
city cybersecurity. The motivation for this work is the need 
for security mechanisms that not only detect and mitigate 
sophisticated cyber threats but also provide clear, interpre -
table,	 and	 human-understandable	 reasoning	 for	 their	 deci-
sions.	 Unlike	 static,	 rule-based	 defenses,	 XAI-driven	 data	
mining	techniques	process	high-volume,	real-time	IoT	data	
streams, identify subtle anomalies, and adapt dynamically 
as new threats emerge. Their interpretability enhances trust, 
facilitates human–AI collaboration, and supports transpar -
ent	 auditability.	These	 qualities	 are	 essential	 for	 mission-
critical	urban	infrastructure.	Moreover,	XAI	aligns	closely	
with principles of cognitive and biologically inspired intel -
ligence. Smart city systems increasingly require AI models 
that reason, explain, and adapt in ways that parallel human 
cognitive processes, enabling intuitive understanding, situ -
ational awareness, and informed oversight. By integrating 
interpretability	 into	 AI-driven	 cybersecurity,	 XAI	 helps	
bridge	 the	 gap	 between	 automated	 decision-making	 and	
human cognitive expectations, ultimately advancing the 
development of trustworthy and resilient smart city eco -
systems.	 Overall,	 this	 survey	 aims	 to	 demonstrate	 how	
XAI-driven	 techniques	 overcome	 the	 limitations	 of	 tra-
ditional cybersecurity methods and contribute to more 
transparent, adaptive, and robust security frameworks for 
IoT-enabled	smart	 cities.	The	 insights	 presented	 here	 pro-
vide	 a	 timely	 foundation	 for	 developing	 next-generation	
cybersecurity architectures that can safeguard critical infra-
structures against rapidly evolving threats.
Methodology
The section presents the methodology for gathering, assess-
ing, and synthesizing the state of the art to address the chal-
lenges	 of	 integrating	 XAI-driven	 data	 mining	 techniques	
into	 the	 cybersecurity	 of	 Internet-of-Things-enabled	 smart	
cities. This review aims to systematically identify, classify, 
and analyze strategies that enable explainability, interpret -
ability,	and	intelligent,	data-driven	reasoning	to	increase	the	
security, trustworthiness, and resilience of smart city IoT 
infrastructures. In addition to conventional analytical per -
spectives, this review considers cognitive and biologically 
inspired	traits	in	XAI	techniques	that	support	human-com-
patible and adaptive cybersecurity processes.
The review is structured around the following research 
questions:
 ● RQ1: What are the primary cybersecurity threats facing 
IoT systems in smart cities?
 ● RQ2:	How	can	XAI-driven	data	mining	techniques	be	
effectively	applied	to	mitigate	these	threats?
 ● RQ3: What challenges arise when deploying these tech-
niques, particularly concerning transparency, interpret -
ability, and resource constraints?
 ● RQ4: What future developments and emerging research 
directions could improve the robustness, adaptabil -
ity,	and	trustworthiness	of	XAI-driven	cybersecurity	in	
smart city environments?
To answer these questions, a systematic search and selection 
process	was	carried	out.	Peer-reviewed	studies	in	major	sci-
entific	repositories,	such	as	Web	of	Science,	Scopus,	Google	
Scholar,	IEEE	Xplore,	and	ScienceDirect,	have	been	system-
atically	analyzed.	Keywords	and	Boolean	operators	related	
to	IoT	security,	XAI,	explainability	methods,	data	mining	as	a	
means of cybersecurity, smart cities, cyber threats, intrusion 
detection,	and	ML-based	defensive	systems	were	included	
in the search strategy. Studies were included if they: (i) pro-
vided	empirical	or	theoretical	contributions	relevant	to	XAI	
or explainable data mining within cybersecurity contexts; 
(ii)	focused	on	IoT	systems	or	cyber-physical	environments	
analogous to smart cities; or (iii) addressed interpretabil -
ity, trust, or transparency as core components of intelligent 
security	models.	Older	works	lacking	methodological	rigor,

Related Work
To date, no published research work has provided an inte -
grated	review	of	how	XAI-	driven	data	mining	techniques,	
combined	 with	 cybersecurity	 strategies,	 are	 used	 in	 IoT-
enabled smart cities. Although several studies have exam -
ined	XAI	or	cybersecurity	independently,	the	intersection	of	
XAI,	data	mining,	IoT	security,	and	smart	city	ecosystems	
has	 not	 been	 addressed	 in	 a	 unified	manner.	The	 existing	
studies of AI in cybersecurity, including those in [ 13] and 
subsequently extended in [ 14], show that ML can improve 
threat detection precision and enhance understanding of 
security models. Nevertheless, these works do not account 
for the unique features of IoT infrastructure, such as device 
heterogeneity, limited computational power, and distrib -
uted sensing, which distinguish smart city landscapes from 
conventional	 IT	 systems.	 Other	 surveys	 on	 data	 mining	
in relation to cybersecurity, such as [ 15–18], mostly look 
at traditional network architectures. Consequently, they 
ignore	 the	 building,	 operational,	 and	 real-time	 constraints	
encountered	in	IoT	deployments,	where	real-time	decision-
making and continuous data streams are indispensable. 
Recent	 works	 have	 focused	 on	 the	 application	 of	 XAI	 to	
enhance the interpretability of the security model of an IoT. 
For example, Alabdulatif [19] shows that explanation tech -
niques improve anomaly detection, and [20, 21]	use	XAI	for	
DL-based	 intrusion	 detection.	 Despite	 the	 merits	 of	 inter-
pretability raised by these contributions, all tend to focus on 
specific	algorithms	rather	than	proposing	a	general	frame-
work	that	bridges	XAI	to	data	mining	across	the	various	IoT	
security layers.

in understanding the issues of urban data processing and

Network Eavesdropping
One	 of	 the	 threats	 posed	 by	 IoT	 ecosystems	 is	 network	
eavesdropping, which mostly happens because of insecure 
communication channels and lightweight transmission pro -
tocols. The majority of IoT devices make use of unencrypted 
or poorly encrypted networks to send data, hence the data 
is prone to interception by a malicious entity. A hacker 
within the same local area network as the IoT devices or 
on any pathway over which the IoT devices communicate 
with backend servers may listen to data packets transferred 
between the two parties within the network, which may then 
be utilized to monitor or modify sensitive data or informa -
tion. The type of data intercepted depends on the nature of 
the device and the manner in which the data is to be used. 
In a smart home, hacked signals may reveal personal con -
versations or breach surveillance cameras. Individual health 
statistics might be stolen in healthcare settings because of 
cues captured by wearables. Such credentials can be used to 
provide attackers with access to authentication keys, control 
scripts,	or	financial	information	in	an	industrial	or	smart	city	
application [41].	The	effects	of	these	breaches	are	devastat-
ing,	such	as	identity	theft,	financial	fraud,	manipulation	of	
the system, and unauthorized access to personal networks. 
A factor that contributed to this vulnerability is that most 
IoT devices possess very few computational capabilities 
and thus they cannot implement powerful encryption and 
authentication. Furthermore, the interception is also facili -
tated by default settings such as plaintext communication 
and weak credentials. All these design constraints make IoT 
deployments highly susceptible to eavesdropping attacks. 
To address this vulnerability, the application of safe secure 
communication	 protocols,	 end-to-end	 encryption	 of	 infor-
mation	transmitted	over	the	network,	frequent	firmware	and	
security	system	software	updates,	and	24-hour	monitoring	
systems should be employed, with atypical network man -
agement attempted [42].

Botnet Attacks
In botnet attacks, large numbers of hacked IoT devices 
are secretly exploited by a malicious actor and organized 
to carry out coordinated cyberattacks. These botnets can 
be	 used	 in	 many	 different	malicious	 tasks,	 such	 as	 DDoS	
attacks, spamming on large scale, stealing data, spread -
ing malware, and ransomware. IoT devices are especially 
susceptible to forming a botnet because they often have 
security	vulnerabilities	such	as	default	and	hard-coded	pass-
words,	 older	 firmware,	and	 lower	 computational	 capacity,	
which	 cannot	 be	 addressed	 with	 effective	 security	 strate-
gies. IoT devices once infected by malware can be silently 
incorporated into a botnet, and the owner may not know or 
be able to do anything to stop it. Attackers remotely con -
trol millions of compromised devices, either thousands or 
millions, which they can use to initiate highly disruptive 
attacks on a massive scale. During a DDoS attack, a bot -
net composed of an internationally dispersed network of 
IoT	devices	floods	a	system	with	excessive	traffic,	depleting	
bandwidth and computing resources until crucial services 
are denied [48–50]. To mitigate botnet threats, IoT devices 
must be enhanced with security measures, such as replacing 
default	credentials,	periodically	applying	firmware	updates,	
and	 configuring	devices	 securely.	Intrusion	 detection	 sys-
tems,	 anomaly	 detection	 mechanisms,	 and	 network-level	
traffic	filtering	can	help	detect	botnet	activity	before	mass	
attacks	 infiltrate	 the	 network.	 The	 adoption	 of	 encrypted	
communication channels and secure software development 
practices would further mitigate the chances of IoT devices 
being compromised by botnets and make subsequent attacks 
weaker and less impactful.

lack or have weak authentication. When devices compro -
mised, it can be remotely controlled to carry out planned

Malware and Exploits
The most susceptible threat to the IoT systems is malware 
and software exploits. The reason is that these threats can 
frequently	result	in	the	most	adverse	effects	on	device	func-
tionality, loss of security, and malfunction of system reli -
ability. The viruses, worms, Trojans, ransomwares, and 
spywares are the most prevalent malware used on the IoT 
devices. Attackers may access the IoT device unauthorized, 
steal sensitive data, disrupt system operation, or fully con -
trol the stolen device when it is infected [ 57]. Exploits, on 
the	other	hand,	prioritize	the	exploitation	of	specific	vulner-
abilities	in	the	software,	firmware,	or	hardware	of	IoT	device	
. Hackers exploit these weaknesses to execute rogue com -
mands, gain privileges, install malware, or burn network 
traffic	 with	 the	 idea	 of	 abusing	 more	 components.	 Most	
IoT	devices	are	based	on	old	firmware	that	is	not	updated	
frequently and not majorly updated with security updates, 
making them very appealing and easy targets to attackers 
who	would	want	to	either	infiltrate	them	or	disable	them	and	
make bigger systems unavailable to other systems. In the 
case of a smart home, hijacked cameras, locks, or alarm sys-
tems leave the homeowner vulnerable to invasion of privacy 
or physical insecurity [ 58]. The approach to control these 
threats	 will	 need	 multilayered	 security.	Frequent	 firmware	
and software upgrades, strong authentication controls, and 
network segmentation are necessary to minimize exposure 
of the attack surface and prevent unauthorized access [59].
Malware and Ransomware
Malware is among the most widespread and harmful threats 
to IoT environments. It can be used to gain unauthorized 
access to IoT devices with weak security settings, steal 
devices and the controlling applications or servers. The 
attacker monitors sensitive information across the network 
by posing as one of the two communicating parties, interfer-
ing	 with	 traffic,	listening	 to	 conversations,	 and	 modifying	
messages, and then passes them on to a preferred destina -
tion. This implies that the opponent is able to listen to or even 
control the behaviour of the devices as they are happening. 
MitM attacks have a catastrophic impact on data integrity 
and	confidentiality	of	information	within	IoT	systems	[51]. 
Accordingly, when an attacker overhears a centralized man-
agement system and devices of smart thermostats or security 
cameras, it is possible to modify the received commands to 
execute illegal commands, e.g., adjust environmental set -
tings	or	turn	off	surveillance	features.	Moreover,	the	shar-
ing of sensitive information between the IoT devices and 
cloud-based	services	puts	the	users	at	risk	of	identity	theft,	
financial	fraud,	and	bad	behavior	practices	[52]. The major-
ity	of	IoT	implementations	do	not	provide	any	end-to-end	
network	visibility	and	monitoring;	therefore,	it	is	difficult	to	
detect and respond to the MitM attacks [53]. The good secu-
rity practices can be used to overcome MIM attacks, includ-
ing using strong encryption schemes (e.g., TLS) to ensure 
that data is not lost during transmission and ensuring mutual 
authentication.	 The	 patching	 and	 updating	 of	 firmware	
should also be updated regularly to plan for known vulner -
abilities. Moreover, network security systems, such as intru-
sion detection systems (IDS), anomaly detection systems, 
and constant monitoring, can detect suspicious behaviours, 
which are indicators of MitM attacks and provide prompt 
reaction to the occurrence of such attacks [54].

Unauthorized Device Access
The most typical vulnerabilities that allow opponents 
to breach the IoT devices include weak or inconsistent 
authentication mechanisms. Most IoT devices use default 
or weak passwords or lack access control policies, which 
serve as a free gateway for hackers. After gaining unauthor-
ized access, attackers may alter device behavior, change 
settings, steal sensitive information, or use the colonized 
device as a pivot point to access other systems on the local 
network or other operational networks beyond the scope of 
the original attacker [ 68]. The outcomes of such breaches 
may include violations of privacy, disruptions to the work -
flow,	or	 severe	 security	 threats	 when	 IoT	 devices	 control	
vital processes.
Physical Attacks
IoT devices are commonly exposed to or prone to physical 
attacks because they are widely deployed. By directly con -
trolling device hardware, a malicious person can steal stored 
data,	 plant	 harmful	 firmware,	defeat	 security	 measures,	 or	
cause incorrect processing actions to occur as desired by 
the device developer, elsewhere a malicious person can per-
form appropriate actions to gain access to the device life 
cycle state of the stored data and modify it to either harm 
or	produce	harmful	effects	[69]. In smart city and industrial 
settings,	physical	attacks	pose	some	of	the	significant	risks,	
as compromised devices can operate critical infrastructure 
components, such as power systems, transportation sys -
tems, or integrated manufacturing machinery. A fault in one 
physically impaired sensor or actuator may result in cascad-
ing faults, operational downtime, safety issues, or massive 
service loss.
Supply Chain Vulnerabilities
Supply chain vulnerabilities are another problem that is 
gaining more prominence in cyberspace IoT issues. Hackers 
implant their devices at any point of the manufacturing, dis-
tribution, or integration process, either when malicious parts 
are introduced, malicious backdoors embedded, or malware 
installed on them that may remain inactive until it is trig -
gered, which in turn may lead to the perpetrators reaching 
a constant compromise of the device with malware [ 70]. 
These	exploitative	hacks	are	difficult	to	find	and	can	damage	
equipment in healthcare, industrial automation, consumer 
IoT, and smart cities. It has also led to data leakage, failure 
of medical devices, derailed industries, and unsupervised 
and	unlimited	long-term	access	to	sensitive	environments.	
The mitigation of the supply chain risks can be achieved 
by	conducting	effective	vetting	of	the	vendor,	authenticity	
compels the use of strong security controls on a basic layer 
of the IoT device design and maintenance. Traditionally 
proposed	remedies	are	regular	firmware	upgrades,	a	secure	
boot,	which	ensures	the	integrity	of	the	firmware	is	checked	
before	it	is	booted,	and	the	cryptographic	signature	of	firm-
ware images, which attests to authenticity prior to installing 
the image. The organization should also employ monitor -
ing systems that are capable of detecting abnormal behavior 
with	the	firmware	or	any	unauthorized	modification	so	as	to	
respond to an incident on time and reduce the chances of an 
enduring compromise [64].
Zero-Day Exploits
Zero-day	exploits	take	advantage	of	security	vulnerabilities	
that are not known to the users or the manufacturers, so 
they	 are	 attacked.	 They	 are	 also	 referred	 to	 as	 zero-days	
because	 the	 developers	 have	 a	 zero-day	 to	 find	out,	 com-
prehend, or rectify the vulnerability before the malicious 
sources exploit it. Such attacks are especially threaten -
ing	in	IoT	settings,	 where	 devices	 can	be	configured	with	
inadequate security measures, outdated software, and 
little or no monitoring. The proliferation of IoT devices 
only	 magnifies	the	 impact	 of	 a	zero-day	 exploit,	 enabling	
attackers to breach numerous systems in a short time and 
go undetected. Through unknown vulnerabilities, attack -
ers circumvent authentication, steal sensitive information, 
obtain unauthorized access to devices, and also take over 
complete control of the IoT devices. To illustrate owner -
ship, an attacker breaches the security systems of a smart 
camera	 using	 a	 zero-day	 exploit,	 enabling	 them	 to	 access	
real-time	 video	streams,	 turn	off	live	video	feeds,	 or	even	
shut down surveillance [ 65, 66]. A mitigation strategy for 
zero-day	 threats	 must	be	proactive	 on	the	layered	 system.	
It is suggested that the companies should employ more 
sophisticated threat detection software capable of detecting 
abnormal activity that can be a clue to unknown vulner -
abilities.	 Network	 segmentation,	 multi-factor	 authentica-
tion,	and	access-control	policy	can	help	overcome	potential

Layered Architecture
A	layered	architecture	is	a	basic	defense-in-depth	approach	
for protecting IoT ecosystems. This model uses many layers 
to allocate security duties, whereby each layer is meant to 
deal with particular types of threats. The architecture uses 
security functionality allocation to ensure that a failure in 
one layer does not immediately compromise the entire sys -
tem. Figure 5	depicts	the	four-layer	IoT	architecture	com-
monly applied in smart city environments, highlighting how 
layered separation contributes to improved resilience and 
comprehensive protection against cyber risks. 
1. Device Layer: The device layer is the base layer of the 
IoT	architecture	 and	comprises	 specific	sensors,	 cam-
eras, and smart meters, which are the endpoints of the 
IoT. Security on this level aims to prevent integrity of 
devices and the defence of sensitive data. Protective 
measures such as Secure Boot, encryption, and access 
control are critical. Secure boot is used to verify each 
firmware	that	is	invoked	during	setup	has	been	authen-
ticated as trusted and that malicious code has not been 
injected	 into	 firmware	 [74]. Data stored on or trans -
ferred to the device is encrypted to protect privacy, 
even in the event of intercepted conversations. Access 
control limits the ability of unauthorized actors to 
communicate with the device, providing an additional 
layer of protection against unauthorized entry.
2. Network Layer: The network layer is responsible for 
transmitting data between IoT devices and backend 
infrastructure, such as cloud services or central servers, 
securely. Transport Layer Security (TLS) and Secure 
Sockets Layer (SSL) are protocols that provide encrypted 
communication channels that resist eavesdropping, tam-
pering, or replay attacks in data communication protocols 
[75]. Secure VPNs establish additional encrypted tun -
nels through the public network infrastructure to further 
secure communication. Firewalls and IDS can also be 
deployed	at	this	layer	to	monitor	traffic	patterns,	detect	
anomalies, and identify potential cyber threats.
3. Application Layer: The application layer in the IoT 
ecosystem allows data processing services, user inter -
action, and service execution, and access control in 
software	 components	 and	IoT-communicating	 devices	
is through Application Programming Interfaces (APIs) 
of these components and devices [ 76]. The API secu -
rity prevents hacking and manipulation of the data by 
unauthorized users. Additionally, this level of software 
updating and patching should be conducted on a regu -
lar basis so as to counter vulnerabilities and counter 
new cyber threats. The architecture layers allows orga -
nizations to overcome architectural security issues 
handles threats at numerous layers, including embedded 
hardware and software, communications protocols, pro -
cessing	architectures,	 and	cloud-based	 services,	 and	cloud	
integration is required [ 72]. We have discussed the most 
significant	 security	 issues	 of	 IoT	 structures	 in	 this	 sec-

Microservices Architecture
Microservices architecture is an application architecture in 
which a system is decomposed into a network of small, auton-
omous services. Each service operates independently and 
interacts with the others via lightweight protocols, typically 
RESTful	APIs	[81]. This form of architecture strengthens the 
security of IoT systems by designing them with modularity, 
reducing interdependencies, and limiting the scope of harm in 
the event of a compromise of devices or services. 
1. Isolation of services: Microservices are designed to 
work independently. This isolation reduces the total 
attack surface and substantially diminishes the impact 
of a successful breach [82].
2. Targeted security updates: The microservices are 
decoupled meaning security patches and updates can be 
implemented	 individually	 on	 the	 affected	bits	 without	
affecting	the	whole	system	[83]. This also allows reduc-
ing	vulnerabilities	more	finely	and	more	timely,	which	
improves system resilience.
3.	 Resilience	 and	redundancy:	Microservice	architectures	
are also built on the principle of replication and redun -
dancy, i.e., multiple replicas of a particular service run 
in parallel [84]. The failure or compromise of one such 
instance does not impact other cases, thus keeping the 
system running constantly and avoiding gaps which 
may harm the availability of IoT services.

A	major	concern	with	implementing	AI-based	data	mining

gets often acquire incredibly sensitive information, like 
medical	 records,	 monetary	 records,	 and	 spatial-temporal

heterogeneous and decentralized. Information often passes 
through numerous devices, gateways, and cloud services,

protected communication, and proactive monitoring to 
track	the	threats	in	real-time	[164]. Besides, AI models are 
also resistant to adversarial manipulation, as it can result 
in the generation of misleading or harmful results by con -
trolling	 the	models.	 It	is	equally	 significant	to	ensure	 that	
the underlying data, along with communication channels, 
is as impervious as possible in order to make the model 
robust.
