A uniﬁed drift-adaptive and robust residual autoencoder with
attention and few-shot learning for zero-day intrusion detection

Abstract
Autoencoder-based unsupervised intrusion detection systems (IDS) offer a promising approach for identifying zero-day
attacks without requiring labeled malicious data. However, residual autoencoder (RAE) frameworks remain limited by static
decision thresholds, susceptibility to concept drift (CD), limited adversarial robustness, and restricted adaptability to emerging
attack patterns. To address these limitations, we propose the Attention-Boosted Streaming Residual Autoencoder (ASRAE), a
uniﬁed framework that integrates attention-enhanced representation learning, online CD detection with adaptive thresholding,
benign-only adversarial training, post-identiﬁcation few-shot meta-learning, and explainability through integrated gradients
(IG). Experiments on UNSW-NB15, CIC-IDS2017, and CSE-CIC-IDS2018 show that ASRAE outperforms the evaluated
unsupervised baselines, achieving ROC-AUC values of 0.952, 0.940, and 0.931, respectively. Under the controlled simulated
concept-drift setting, ASRAE maintains a ROC-AUC above 0.91, while adversarial evaluation using Fast Gradient Sign
Method (FGSM) and Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) demonstrates improved robustness to feature-space perturbations.
The few-shot adaptation stage further enables adaptation to held-out attack families using only a small number of labeled
samples while largely preserving benign reconstruction performance. ASRAE requires an average inference time of 9.2 ms
per sample in the evaluated experimental environment. Overall, the results demonstrate the effectiveness of ASRAE across
the benchmark, simulated-drift, adversarial, and few-shot adaptation settings.

1 Introduction
The increasing digitalization of modern society has led to
the widespread deployment of interconnected systems across
critical domains, including healthcare [ 1], ﬁnance [2], trans-
portation [3], industrial control systems [ 4], and smart cities
[5]. While this rapid connectivity has improved efﬁciency
and automation, it has also signiﬁcantly expanded the attack
surface of modern networks, exposing them to increasingly
sophisticated cyber threats. Among these, zero day attacks,
which exploit previously unseen vulnerabilities, pose a crit-
ical challenge to conventional intrusion detection systems
(IDS) [6]. Traditional signature based approaches are inher-
ently limited in this context, as they rely on predeﬁned attack
patterns and fail to generalize to emerging threats. As a result,
anomaly based IDS, particularly those leveraging deep learn-
ing, have gained increasing attention due to their ability to
model normal network behavior and identify unusual activi-
ties without requiring labeled malicious data [ 7, 8].

Among anomaly based approaches, deep autoencoder
models have become widely used for unsupervised intru-
sion detection. These models learn compact representations
of benign network trafﬁc and detect anomalies by measuring
reconstruction errors between the original and reconstructed
inputs [6, 9]. Residual autoencoders (RAE) further improve
this process by incorporating skip connections and hybrid
loss functions, which enhance training stability and recon-
struction quality [10]. However, despite these advancements,
existing autoencoder based intrusion detection frameworks
still face important limitations, particularly in dynamic and
adversarial environments where network behavior continu-
ously evolves [11].
First, these frameworks remain inherently static because
they are trained only once on historical benign trafﬁc and
cannot adapt to concept drift caused by evolving network
behavior, newly emerging applications, or temporal traf-
ﬁc variations. Second, anomaly detection thresholds are
typically ﬁxed using statistical measures derived from the
training set, which may become unreliable over time and
lead to increased false positive rates. Third, existing models
generally lack robustness against adversarial perturbations,
making them vulnerable to evasion attacks due to the absence
of dedicated defense mechanisms. In addition, their gener-
alization capability is often limited because evaluation is
commonly restricted to a single dataset, leaving their effec-
tiveness across diverse and modern network environments
uncertain. Finally, these models operate largely as black
boxes and provide limited insight into why speciﬁc trafﬁc
samples are classiﬁed as anomalous, thereby reducing inter-
pretability and complicating forensic analysis.
To address these limitations, we propose the Attention
Boosted Streaming Residual Autoencoder (ASRAE), a uni-
ﬁed and adaptive framework for robust anomaly detection in
dynamic network environments. Unlike existing approaches
that treat robustness, adaptability, and interpretability as sep-
arate problems, the proposed framework integrates these
capabilities within a single architecture. This uniﬁed design
enables the model to effectively handle evolving data dis-
tributions, adversarial perturbations, and emerging attack
patterns in a coherent manner.
The contribution of ASRAE lies in the coordinated
use of multiple adaptation mechanisms within a common
reconstruction-based framework rather than in claiming
novelty for each component individually. The attention-
enhanced latent representation supports anomaly reconstruc-
tion and selective few-shot adaptation, while reconstruction-
error statistics are used for both anomaly scoring and online
drift monitoring. Benign-only adversarial training further
improves robustness without requiring attack labels during
initial training. In this way, ASRAE extends the original static
residual autoencoder toward a uniﬁed framework for robust
reconstruction, online adaptation, and post-identiﬁcation
few-shot updating.
The main contributions are summarized as follows:
• Drift-aware adaptive anomaly detection: A streaming
adaptive threshold based on reconstruction-error statis-
tics is used to respond to changing trafﬁc distributions
and support selective online retraining.
• Attention-enhanced latent representation: Multi-head
self-attention is incorporated into the latent representa-
tion to model dependencies among compressed features
and improve anomaly discrimination.
• Benign-only adversarial robustness: Adversarial pertur-
bations are applied to benign training samples to improve
reconstruction stability and reduce sensitivity to evasion-
oriented perturbations.
• Few-shot adaptation for emerging attacks: MAML-
based selective adaptation enables the model to update
a restricted set of parameters when only a small number
of labeled samples from a newly identiﬁed attack family
are available.
• Explainable anomaly detection: Integrated gradients are
used to provide feature-level attribution for detected
anomalies and improve interpretation of model decisions.
The framework is evaluated on UNSW-NB15, CIC-
IDS2017, and CSE-CIC-IDS2018, achieving ROC-AUC
values of 0.952, 0.940, and 0.931, respectively, with a mean
ROC-AUC of approximately 0.941. Under the controlled
simulated concept-drift setting, ASRAE maintains more sta-
ble performance than the original RAE, while the adversarial
experiments show improved resistance to feature-space per-
turbations. The reported inference time remains below 10 ms
per sample in the evaluated experimental environment.
The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Sec-
tion 2 reviews related work. Section 3 presents the proposed
framework. Section 4 describes the experimental setup and
results. Section 5 discusses the ﬁndings and limitations, and
Sect. 6 concludes the paper.
2 Related work
2.1 Unsupervised IDS using autoencoders
Autoencoders have been widely adopted for unsupervised
network anomaly detection due to their ability to learn
representations of benign trafﬁc without requiring labeled
malicious data. Early work by Javaid et al. [ 12] utilized a
sparse autoencoder combined with softmax regression for
attack detection, while Farahnakian and Heikkonen [13]p r o -
posed a stacked autoencoder to capture hierarchical network

features. Shone et al. [14] further enhanced anomaly discrim-
ination by integrating a non-symmetric deep autoencoder
with a random forest classiﬁer.
More recent approaches have focused on improving rep-
resentation learning through advanced architectures. These
include attention-based autoencoders [ 15], hybrid
autoencoder–transformer models [16], and generative adver-
sarial network-based frameworks [ 17]. Attention-based fea-
ture interaction has also been investigated in related
representation-learning tasks. C2T-Net employs channel-
aware self-attention and cross-fusion to combine comple-
mentary feature representations, while CLEAR uses cross-
transformer interactions for person attribute recognition and
retrieval [18, 19]. Although these methods address computer
vision rather than intrusion detection, they provide relevant
examples of attention mechanisms for modeling interac-
tions among learned feature representations. The residual
autoencoder (RAE) [ 10] represents a notable advancement
by incorporating skip connections and hybrid loss func-
tions to improve training stability and reconstruction ﬁdelity.
However, despite these improvements, existing autoencoder-
based intrusion detection systems remain largely static and
lack adaptability to evolving network conditions, limiting
their effectiveness in dynamic environments [ 20].
2.2 Concept drift and adaptive IDS
Concept drift (CD) signiﬁcantly affects the performance of
intrusion detection systems in dynamic network environ-
ments, where data distributions evolve over time. While
various drift detection techniques, such as ADaptive WIN-
dowing (ADWIN) and Drift Detection Method (DDM), have
been proposed, they are primarily designed for supervised
learning settings [21]. Existing unsupervised approaches for
drift adaptation remain limited. For example, ExpIDS [ 22]
employs a denoising autoencoder for drift handling, but it
does not incorporate residual learning or dynamic thresh-
olding mechanisms. Similarly, [ 23] propose an adaptive
deep autoencoder for security anomaly detection in evolv-
ing network environments, but do not address adversarial
robustness or few-shot generalization to unseen attacks.
Robustness to general distribution shift has also been studied
structurally in [ 24], though not in the context of unsu-
pervised intrusion detection. Deep active learning provides
methodologies to select informative and trustworthy unla-
belled instances for human labelling [25]. This body of work
bears relevance for our online retraining buffer deﬁned in
Eq. ( 10), which currently selects candidate retraining sam-
ples purely by reconstruction-error threshold without active
sample selection criteria. Separately, emerging dynamic
graph continuous state-space models are designed for evolv-
ing networked systems [26]. Our ASRAE treats each network
ﬂow as an independent tabular vector and does not explicitly
model host-level graph interactions across ﬂows; integrating
dynamic graph representations constitutes a promising future
research direction.
Overall, there is a clear lack of methods that integrate
online drift detection, adaptive thresholding, and efﬁcient
model updating within a residual autoencoder framework,
which remains an open research challenge.
2.3 Adversarial robustness in autoencoders
Adversarial attacks, such as those generated using gradi-
ent based perturbation methods, can signiﬁcantly degrade
the performance of autoencoder based intrusion detection
systems. Existing defense strategies, including adversarial
training, are predominantly designed for supervised classi-
ﬁers rather than unsupervised models [27]. Some approaches,
such as ensemble based defense frameworks [ 28], incorpo-
rate adversarial training in autoencoder based systems, but
they rely on both benign and malicious samples during train-
ing. Similarly, AIDTF [ 29] focuses on supervised detection
models and does not address unsupervised reconstruction
based frameworks.
2.4 Few-shot learning for intrusion detection
Few shot learning has emerged as an effective strategy for
addressing data scarcity in intrusion detection systems. How-
ever, most existing approaches are limited to supervised or
semi supervised settings [ 30, 31]. For instance, adversarial
autoencoder based methods [ 32] reduce labeling require-
ments but do not fully operate in an unsupervised manner.
Modelagnostic meta learning (MAML) based approaches
have also been explored for intrusion detection [ 33]b u tt h e y
are primarily applied to classiﬁcation models and not to unsu-
pervised autoencoder based frameworks. Fine-tuning-based
adaptation under limited labeled data has similarly been used
for defect detection in other domains, such as industrial fabric
inspection [ 34], underscoring that data-efﬁcient adaptation
is a cross-domain need rather than one unique to network
security. Consequently, enabling rapid adaptation of unsu-
pervised models to new attack types using limited labeled
data remains a signiﬁcant research gap.
2.5 Explainable anomaly detection
Explainable artiﬁcial intelligence methods, including SHAP
and LIME, have been widely used to improve the trans-
parency of intrusion detection systems [ 35]. However, their
application to autoencoder based models, particularly resid-
ual architectures, remains limited. Existing work, such as
convGRU based autoencoders, has explored interpretability
using layer wise relevance propagation [ 36], but alternative
approaches such as integrated gradients have not been suf-

anomaly detection within residual autoencoder frameworks
is therefore an important yet underexplored direction.
Overall, existing studies address adaptability, robust-
ness, generalization, and interpretability in isolation. There
remains a lack of a uniﬁed framework that simultaneously
integrates concept drift adaptation, adversarial robustness,
few shot generalization, and explainable anomaly detection
within a single unsupervised architecture. This gap motivates
the proposed ASRAE framework.
3 Proposed method
3.1 Overall architecture
The proposed ASRAE framework builds upon the core resid-
ual autoencoder architecture, which consists of an encoder,
decoder, residual skip connection, and a hybrid loss func-
tion. To overcome the limitations of existing models, the
framework integrates multiple functional components within
a uniﬁed design to enhance adaptability, robustness, and
interpretability. Figure1 illustrates the overall architecture of
ASRAE and its role in the intrusion detection pipeline. The
framework follows an end to end processing ﬂow, where each
component contributes to effective anomaly detection. The
encoder transforms the input network trafﬁc features, rep-
resented as a 49 dimensional vector, into a compact latent
representation of dimension 32. This transformation reduces
computational complexity while preserving essential infor-
mation.
The latent representation is further processed using a
multi head attention mechanism, which captures depen-
dencies among compressed features and highlights subtle
patterns that may indicate anomalous behavior. The atten-
tion enhanced latent vector is then passed to the decoder,
which reconstructs the original input. A residual skip con-
nection is incorporated to preserve important input features
and stabilize the training process. The model is optimized
using a hybrid loss function that combines Smooth L1 loss
and cosine similarity. This formulation ensures accurate
reconstruction while maintaining consistency in feature rep-
resentation, allowing the model to learn meaningful patterns
rather than trivial mappings.
In addition to the core architecture, the framework incor-
porates auxiliary components, including adversarial training,
concept drift detection, few shot adaptation, and explainabil-
ity mechanisms. These components are seamlessly integrated
into the overall system to improve performance in dynamic
and adversarial environments. As a result, ASRAE retains the
efﬁciency of the residual autoencoder while addressing its
key limitations, including limited discriminative capability,
vulnerability to adversarial perturbations, lack of adaptabil-
ity, and absence of interpretability.

3.3 Hybrid loss function
The proposed framework adopts the hybrid loss function
from the original RAE to jointly optimize reconstruction
accuracy and feature consistency. This loss formulation plays
a critical role in guiding the model to learn meaningful rep-
resentations of network trafﬁc. The loss function is deﬁned
as:
L = α∥ˆx − x∥
S1 + (1 − α)(1 − cos(ˆx, x)), (7)
where ∥·∥ S1 denotes Smooth L1 loss, cos (ˆx, x) represents
the cosine similarity between the input and its reconstruc-

terms. The ﬁrst term focuses on minimizing the numerical
reconstruction error, ensuring that the reconstructed output
closely matches the original input. The second term enforces
consistency in the feature space by preserving the directional
similarity between input and reconstruction. This combina-
tion encourages the model to capture both ﬁne grained details
and global structural patterns, leading to more robust and dis-
criminative representations for anomaly detection.

3.5 Adversarial training on benign samples
To improve robustness against evasion attacks, the training
process incorporates adversarial examples generated from
benign samples using the FGSM. The adversarial sample is
deﬁned as:

with perturbation strength ϵ = 0.03. All adversarial pertur-
bations, in both training and evaluation, are constrained to the
min–max normalized [0, 1] feature space used throughout
preprocessing (Section 4.1); perturbed vectors are clipped to
this range after each perturbation step. Perturbations are fur-
ther restricted to continuous ﬂow-statistic features (e.g., byte
counts, duration, packet-length and inter-arrival statistics);
categorical and protocol-indicator features are left unper-
turbed, since arbitrary perturbation of these ﬁelds could
produce ﬂows that do not correspond to physically realiz-
able network behavior. For datasets requiring dimensionality
reduction, adversarial perturbations are generated in the orig-
inal continuous feature space before applying the ﬁtted PCA
transformation. The perturbed samples are then processed
using the same transformation applied to clean samples. The
model is trained to reconstruct both clean and perturbed
benign samples, encouraging the learning of a locally smooth
representation in the latent space. This approach enhances
robustness without requiring labeled attack data, which is
particularly important in unsupervised settings. By focus-
ing on benign samples, the model becomes less sensitive to
small input perturbations, thereby reducing vulnerability to
adversarial evasion while maintaining stable reconstruction
performance.
Equation (12) applies perturbations exclusively to benign
training samples and maximises reconstruction loss dur-
ing model training to enforce latent-space smoothness.
This training-time objective differs fundamentally from the
behaviour of a real adversarial evasion attacker, whose goal is
to reduce the anomaly score of malicious trafﬁc so that attack
instances are misclassiﬁed as benign. Section 4.8 evaluates
practical evasion attacks using this opposite optimisation
objective applied to held-out attack test samples.

4 Experimental results and analysis
4.1 Datasets
The proposed ASRAE framework is evaluated on three
widely used benchmark datasets for network intrusion detec-
tion to ensure comprehensive validation across diverse attack
scenarios and trafﬁc distributions.
• UNSW-NB15 [38]: This dataset contains approximately
2.54 million network ﬂow records described by 49 fea-
tures. It includes nine attack categories, namely Fuzzers,
Analysis, Backdoor, DoS, Exploits, Generic, Reconnais-
sance, Shellcode, and Worms, providing a diverse set of
intrusion patterns for evaluation.
Fig. 2 Workﬂow of the ASRAE framework, covering data prepro-
cessing, model inference, concept drift handling, post-identiﬁcation
few-shot adaptation, and explainability
• CIC-IDS2017 [39]: This dataset comprises approxi-
mately 2.83 million network ﬂows with 80 original
features. To ensure consistency and fair comparison with
other datasets, the feature dimension is reduced to 49
using principal component analysis. The dataset includes
a wide range of attack types, such as DDoS, Botnet, and
Port scanning, representing realistic network intrusion
scenarios.
• CSE-CIC-IDS2018 [39]: This large scale dataset con-
tains over 5 million network ﬂow records and reﬂects
modern network trafﬁc characteristics. Similar to CIC-
IDS2017, the feature dimension is reduced to 49 to
maintain uniformity across datasets and ensure consis-
tent model evaluation.
All datasets exhibit signiﬁcant class imbalance, which
reﬂects realistic network trafﬁc conditions. To ensure con-
sistency and fair comparison, we adopt the preprocessing
protocol used in the original residual autoencoder frame-
work. This includes min–max normalization, removal of
redundant features, and standardized dataset splitting.
The model is trained exclusively on benign samples, where
70% of the benign data is used for training and 10% for
validation. The remaining 20% of benign data, together with
all attack samples, is reserved for testing. This setup ensures

4.2 Baseline methods
To evaluate the effectiveness of the proposed framework,
we compare ASRAE with seven representative unsupervised
and deep-learning-based anomaly detection methods. These
baselines are selected to cover both classical approaches and
recent deep learning models, ensuring a comprehensive and
fair comparison.
• One-class SVM A classical anomaly detection method
using a radial basis function kernel with ν= 0.1, widely
adopted for modeling normal data distributions.
• Isolation forest A tree based anomaly detection technique
with a contamination factor set to 0.1, designed to isolate
anomalous samples through random partitioning.
• Plain autoencoder A baseline model using the same
encoder and decoder architecture as the RAE but with-
out residual connections, trained solely with Smooth L1
loss. This comparison highlights the impact of residual
learning.
• Original residual autoencoder (RAE) [10]The original
model incorporating residual skip connections and a
hybrid loss function. This baseline serves to evaluate
the contribution of additional components introduced in
ASRAE, including attention, drift adaptation, adversarial
robustness, and few shot learning.
• DAGMM [40]: A deep autoencoding Gaussian mixture
model that combines representation learning with density
estimation for unsupervised anomaly detection.
• USAD [41]: An adversarial training based unsupervised
anomaly detection framework that leverages reconstruc-
tion based objectives to improve detection performance.
• ExpIDS [22]: A drift-adaptive denoising-autoencoder-
based intrusion detection method included to compare
ASRAE with an existing concept-drift-aware approach.
4.3 Implementation details
The proposed ASRAE framework is implemented using
PyTorch 2.0 and trained on an NVIDIA T4 GPU with 16 GB
memory. The model is optimized using the Adam optimizer
with an initial learning rate of 10
− 3, combined with cosine
annealing and early stopping with a patience of 10 epochs.
The batch size is set to 256, and the latent dimension is ﬁxed
at 32. The multi-head attention module consists of four par-
allel attention heads.
For drift adaptation, the sliding-window size is set to
W = 1000, and the threshold coefﬁcient is β = 3. A
drift event is triggered when ﬁve consecutive samples sat-
isfy e
i > 2T(t). Adversarial training is performed using
FGSM with a perturbation magnitude of ϵ = 0.03, applied
exclusively to benign samples.
For few-shot adaptation, MAML is implemented using 5
support samples and 5 query samples per task. The inner loop
uses a learning rate of 0 .01 over ﬁve gradient-update steps.
The outer-loop learning rate is set to 10− 3, with a meta-batch
size of 32 and a total of 1200 meta-training episodes.

4.9 Few-shot adaptation for emerging attack types
To evaluate ASRAE’s ability to adapt to previously unseen
zero-day attacks, we conduct few-shot experiments on
three representative attack classes: Worms, Shellcode, and
Reconnaissance. The model is ﬁrst initialized without expo-
sure to these attack types. A small number of labeled
samples is then provided for Model-Agnostic Meta-Learning
(MAML)-based adaptation.
Table 7 reports the detection rate, measured as the true
positive rate, before and after adaptation using 5, 10, and 15
labeled samples. The original residual autoencoder (RAE),
which does not include an adaptation mechanism, shows only
negligible improvement across all settings. For example, its
detection rate for Worms increases only from 0.72 to 0.73
after 15 labeled samples. Similar limited gains are observed
for Shellcode and Reconnaissance.
In contrast, ASRAE achieves substantial improvements
even with very few labeled examples. For Worms attacks, the
detection rate increases from 0.72 to 0.87 with only 5 labeled
samples, improves to 0.91 with 10 samples, and reaches 0.93
with 15 samples. Similar trends are observed for Shellcode,
where the detection rate improves from 0.71 to 0.94, and
Reconnaissance, where it increases from 0.70 to 0.92 after
15-shot adaptation.
These results demonstrate that ASRAE can rapidly adapt
to emerging attack families using minimal labeled data. This
capability is particularly important for real-world intrusion
detection, where zero-day attacks frequently appear and col-
lecting large labeled datasets for new attack types is often
impractical.
Table 8 further shows that this adaptation does not com-
promise detection of benign trafﬁc: FPR rises from 1.12%
to at most 1.15% (Worms), a 0.03-percentage-point change,
conﬁrming that freezing the encoder and decoder body
during adaptation preserves core reconstruction behavior
as intended. The plain ﬁne-tuning baseline, which updates
the identical restricted parameter set without meta-learning
outer-loop optimisation, yields moderate detection improve-
ments but underperforms MAML-based adaptation across
all attack families. Additionally, plain ﬁne-tuning produces
a slightly larger benign false–positive rate drift compared to
MAML, demonstrating the beneﬁt of the meta-learning reg-
ularisation effect.

5 Discussion
The results show that ASRAE improves reconstruction-
based intrusion detection across the three evaluated datasets.
The model achieves ROC-AUC values of 0.952, 0.940,
and 0.931 on UNSW-NB15, CIC-IDS2017, and CSE-CIC-
IDS2018, respectively, corresponding to a mean ROC-AUC
of approximately 0.941. Compared with the original resid-
ual autoencoder and other evaluated baselines, the proposed
framework provides more reliable anomaly detection while
retaining reconstruction error as the main decision criterion.
The ablation results indicate that the improvement does
not arise from a single component. Attention alone increases
ROC-AUC from 0.903 to 0.924, while the complete ASRAE
conﬁguration reaches 0.952. The stronger performance
obtained when attention is combined with drift adaptation
and adversarial training suggests that these components
provide complementary beneﬁts. Accordingly, the contribu-
tion of ASRAE lies in coordinating representation learning,
robustness, online adaptation, and few-shot updating within a
common reconstruction-based framework rather than claim-
ing novelty for each component individually.
The adversarial experiments further demonstrate improved
robustness. Under PGD at ϵ = 0.10, ASRAE with adversar-
ial training maintains a ROC-AUC of 0.905, compared with
0.642 for the original RAE. Benign-only adversarial training
nevertheless introduces a small sensitivity trade-off: the clean
false-negative rate increases from 8.72 to 9.05%, while the
increase under FGSM perturbation is substantially reduced.
These results indicate that the selected adversarial-training
setting improves robustness with only a limited loss in clean
detection sensitivity.
The few-shot experiments should be interpreted as post-
identiﬁcation adaptation rather than part of the initial unsu-
pervised detection stage. Worms, Shellcode, and Reconnais-
sance are excluded from both autoencoder pretraining and
MAML meta-training. Once a small number of labeled exam-
ples becomes available, only the attention projections and
ﬁnal decoder layer are adapted. The benign false-positive
rate changes from 1.12% to no more than 1.15% after 15-
shot adaptation, indicating that selective updating largely
preserves the original benign reconstruction behavior.
Some limitations remain. The current drift experiment
is based on controlled feature perturbations and therefore
does not fully represent naturally evolving temporal trafﬁc.
In addition, drift-triggered retraining uses model-predicted
benign samples satisfying e
i ≤ T(t) rather than ground-

Future work should therefore evaluate ASRAE on chrono-

an adaptive extension of residual-autoencoder-based intru-

directions for further validation and development.
6 Conclusion
This study presented ASRAE, an adaptive residual-
autoencoder framework for network intrusion detection.
The framework combines attention-enhanced latent rep-
resentation, adaptive thresholding, concept-drift handling,
benign-only adversarial training, few-shot adaptation, and
integrated-gradient-based explainability within a common
reconstruction-based architecture.
Experiments on UNSW-NB15, CIC-IDS2017, and CSE-
CIC-IDS2018 achieved ROC-AUC values of 0.952, 0.940,
and 0.931, respectively. ASRAE also maintained stronger
performance than the original RAE under the controlled
simulated drift setting and showed improved resistance to
adversarial perturbations. For held-out attack families, the
few-shot stage enabled post-identiﬁcation adaptation using
only a small number of labeled samples while largely pre-
serving benign reconstruction performance.
The results demonstrate the value of combining robust-
ness and adaptation within a reconstruction-based detector,
although several limitations remain. The drift evaluation is
based on controlled feature perturbations rather than natu-
rally occurring temporal drift, and online retraining relies on
model-predicted benign samples, which may include unde-
tected attacks. In addition, few-shot gains decrease at higher
shot counts and the added components introduce moderate
computational overhead.
Future work will focus on chronological drift evalua-
tion, more reliable unsupervised sample selection for online
retraining, stronger constrained adversarial evaluation, and
validation on industrial and IoT network trafﬁc. Model
optimization may also be explored to further reduce com-
putational cost.
