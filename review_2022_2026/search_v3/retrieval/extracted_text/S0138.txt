Abstract
Federated learning (FL) has become a promising paradigm for building privacy-preserving intrusion detection 
systems (IDS) in distributed cloud–edge environments, yet existing reviews do not sufficiently explain which 
model-level optimization choices determine practical deployability under heterogeneity, communication 
constraints, and evolving attacks. This survey addresses that problem by reviewing representative FL-IDS studies 
through a model-optimization lens, with primary emphasis on centralized horizontal FL-IDS in cloud–edge settings, 
and by organizing the literature into three tightly coupled dimensions: aggregation, communication-efficient 
transmission, and personalization. Through comparative analysis of representative methods, we find that these 
dimensions jointly shape not only detection accuracy, communication efficiency, and local adaptability, but also 
rare-attack recall, robustness to unreliable updates, concept-drift handling, and deployment latency. The survey 
further shows that current FL-IDS research remains limited by weak support for structural heterogeneity, static 
compression strategies, insufficient long-term personalization, and a lack of realistic cloud–edge evaluation. Overall, 
the evidence suggests that practical FL-IDS requires co-optimization across model design, communication, and 
deployment orchestration. As a survey, this study is constrained by the inconsistency of published datasets, metrics, 
and experimental protocols; accordingly, we advocate standardized benchmarks, realistic testbeds, coupling-
aware aggregation, attack-aware transmission, and parameter-efficient drift adaptation for scalable and deployable 
federated intrusion detection.

Introduction
With the rapid expansion of cyberspace in the big-data 
era, terminal devices, networked applications, and ser -
vice infrastructures continuously generate massive, het -
erogeneous, and highly dynamic traffic data. At the same 
time, cyber-attacks have become increasingly automated, 
stealthy, and multi-stage, posing serious threats to criti -
cal infrastructures and digital services  [ 1, 2]. As a funda-
mental defense mechanism, intrusion detection systems 
(IDS) have progressively incorporated machine learning 
and deep learning techniques to improve the detection 
of unknown attacks and complex behavioral patterns  [ 3, 
4]. However, building high-quality IDS models typically 
requires large-scale, multi-source, and cross-organiza -
tional data. In practice, privacy risks, regulatory con -
straints, and communication costs make centralized data 
collection difficult, and the long-standing problem of 
data silos remains a major obstacle to collaborative secu -
rity intelligence  [5].
Federated learning (FL) provides a promising alter -
native to centralized IDS modeling by enabling collab -
orative training without directly sharing raw data   [ 6]. 
Through local model training and parameter or gradient 
exchange, FL can reduce privacy exposure while naturally 
matching the distributed characteristics of IoT, mobile, 
and edge environments  [ 7]. This property is particularly 
attractive for IDS, where traffic logs, device behaviors, 
and security events are typically distributed across dif -
ferent organizations, domains, or edge nodes, and where 
data sharing is often restricted by privacy, compliance, 
or operational concerns. As a result, FL has emerged as 
an important direction for building cross-organizational 
and privacy-preserving IDS, with growing applications 
in scenarios such as IoT, edge computing, and resource-
constrained collaborative environments  [8– 10].
Despite this progress, existing surveys on FL-based 
IDS mainly discuss the topic from the perspectives of 
system architectures, application scenarios, communica -
tion workflows, or privacy and security protection. These 
studies provide useful overviews of deployment patterns 
and supporting mechanisms, but they offer limited analy-
sis of the model-level optimization decisions that largely 
determine whether federated IDS can be trained and 
deployed effectively in practice. Recent studies on feder -
ated resource optimization further suggest that practi -
cal deployability is also shaped by communication and 
system-level resource allocation, especially in heteroge -
neous cloud–edge settings. For FL-IDS, practical util -
ity depends not only on whether collaborative learning 
is feasible, but also on whether the resulting models can 
remain accurate, communication-efficient, and adapt -
able under heterogeneous data distributions, resource 
constraints, and evolving attack patterns  [11, 12]. In par-
ticular, several model-centric issues remain insufficiently 
synthesized in the current survey literature, including 
aggregation bias caused by non-IID data, transmission 
cost induced by model scale, and the need for person -
alization across heterogeneous clients, as illustrated in 
Fig. 1.
Motivated by this gap, this paper surveys FL-based IDS 
from a model optimization perspective . Rather than 
treating aggregation, transmission, and personalization 
as isolated topics, we view them as three tightly cou -
pled optimization dimensions that jointly shape detec -
tion quality, communication efficiency, local adaptability, 
robustness, and deployment feasibility in federated IDS. 
This perspective is especially relevant to real-world 
cloud–edge settings, where client devices differ sub -
stantially in data distribution, computational capabil -
ity, network quality, and attack exposure. Accordingly, 
this survey focuses primarily on cloud–edge collabora -
tive FL-IDS, with emphasis on model-level optimization 
strategies that improve practical deployability under 
heterogeneous and resource-constrained conditions. 
The main contributions of this work are summarized as 
follows:
1. We provide a coupling-aware synthesis of FL-IDS 
literature centered on aggregation, transmission, 
and personalization. Instead of organizing 
the literature only by architecture or security 
mechanisms, we identify three deployment-relevant 
and tightly coupled model-level dimensions—
aggregation, communication-efficient transmission, 
and personalization—and use them to structure the 
FL-IDS research landscape.
2. We provide a systematic review of representative 
optimization strategies in each dimension. For 
each dimension, we examine representative methods 
in terms of their design rationale, optimization 
mechanisms, applicability to heterogeneous cloud–
edge environments, and practical limitations. We 
further organize the literature into multiple technical 
sub-directions to reveal current methodological 
trends.
3. We highlight IDS-oriented trade-offs and key 
bottlenecks in federated model optimization.  
Beyond descriptive categorization, we synthesize 
how different optimization strategies affect core 
FL-IDS concerns, including heterogeneity handling, 
communication overhead, robustness to unreliable 
updates, local adaptability under concept drift, and 
deployment constraints in dynamic environments.
4. We identify open challenges and discuss future 
directions for practical FL-IDS deployment. 
Based on the surveyed literature, we outline several 
promising directions, including heterogeneity-aware 
aggregation, compression-aware transmission,

Background on intrusion detection and federated 
learning
Trends and challenges in intrusion detection
As a core component of network security architec -
tures, IDS aim to identify malicious traffic and abnormal 
behavior in real time. In recent years, IDS have gradually 
evolved from rule-based matching schemes toward intel -
ligent, data-driven detection paradigms.
From the perspective of data modalities, IDS inputs 
are becoming increasingly diverse, including network 
flow records   [13, 14], system and application logs   [ 15, 
16], and multimodal cross-source features  [ 17]. Network 
traffic data is structurally well-defined and highly time-
sensitive, making it suitable for detecting typical attacks 
such as scanning and denial-of-service. Log data captures 
detailed traces of system behavior and is better suited for 
identifying insider misuse and long-term stealthy attacks. 
Multimodal fusion further attempts to integrate infor -
mation from different perspectives to improve detection 
accuracy and support attack-chain analysis  [18]. As busi-
ness systems grow more complex, attack behaviors con -
tinue to expand in scale, frequency, and manifestation, 
placing higher demands on the feature extraction, repre -
sentation learning, and cross-source correlation capabili -
ties of IDS.
In terms of model evolution, IDS have progressed from 
statistical learning to traditional machine learning and, 
more recently, to deep learning. Statistical methods rely 
on distributional assumptions and are computationally 
efficient, but struggle to cope with complex and evolv -
ing attack patterns   [ 19]. Classical machine learning 
approaches such as SVMs, decision trees, and random 
forests provide strong discriminative power, yet heavily 
depend on handcrafted features and are difficult to scale 
to large, high-dimensional datasets   [ 20]. Deep learning

models, including CNNs, RNNs, and LSTMs, leverage

anomaly detection, and event correlation analysis, fur -

intelligent response  [22].
Despite these advances, practical IDS deployments still 
face several key challenges:
1. Massive data demand. Centralizing raw data on a 
server incurs substantial communication and storage 
costs, and can degrade system responsiveness and 
real-time performance, particularly in resource-
constrained edge environments.
2. High computational requirements. Centralized 
training often relies on high-performance 
computing resources such as GPU clusters or cloud 
infrastructures, which may exceed the capabilities of 
many organizations or devices.
3. Data security and privacy. Network data typically 
contains sensitive user information, such as access 
behaviors and identity attributes. Centralized 
collection significantly increases privacy leakage 
risks, and organizations are often unwilling or even 
forbidden to share internal data due to regulatory 
and security concerns.
In summary, intrusion detection is evolving toward data-
driven, intelligent, and collaboratively distributed para -
digms  [23]. However, due to constraints imposed by data 
privacy, multi-source heterogeneity, and dynamically 
evolving attacks, traditional centralized modeling strug -
gles to achieve satisfactory performance in real-world 
deployments. These challenges directly motivate the 
introduction of FL into IDS and lay the foundation for 
subsequent research.
Development and applications of federated learning
Workflow and core mechanisms
FL is essentially a distributed learning paradigm that 
enables collaborative model training under the prin -
ciple of “data remains local. ” In contrast to conventional 
centralized learning, where all data are aggregated on a 
central server for unified training, FL performs model 
optimization locally on each participant and only uploads 
model parameters or gradients for server-side aggrega -
tion. This paradigm allows the system to leverage multi-
source data while preserving privacy and reducing data 
transmission overhead  [24].

Typical taxonomy and application scenarios
As FL evolves to accommodate different data character -
istics and system architectures, several organizational 
forms and classification perspectives have emerged. FL 
can be categorized along two primary dimensions: data 
relationships and system structures.
Classification based on data relationships According 
to the overlap in sample space and feature space across 
participants, FL can be divided into horizontal FL, verti -
cal FL, and federated transfer learning  [ 25], as illustrated 
in Fig. 2.

Horizontal federated learning (HFL) . HFL applies 
when participants share similar feature spaces but differ 
in sample spaces, as shown in Fig.  2(a). Each party holds 
data with identical feature dimensions but records dif -
ferent individuals or devices. After local training, model 
parameters or gradients are uploaded for server-side 
aggregation. HFL is the most widely used and representa-
tive form of FL  [25].
Vertical federated learning (VFL) . VFL is used when 
participants share overlapping sample spaces but have 
distinct feature spaces, as illustrated in Fig.  2(b). VFL 
requires privacy-preserving feature alignment and joint 
modeling, commonly combining secure multiparty com -
putation (SMC) or homomorphic encryption to ensure 
confidentiality  [26].
Federated transfer learning (FTL) . FTL applies when 
both sample spaces and feature spaces differ significantly 
across participants, as shown in Fig.  2(c). By leveraging 
transfer learning techniques, FTL enables cross-domain 
knowledge transfer even under strong data heterogene -
ity or minimal sample/feature overlap. Limited shared 
information or intermediate representations are used to 
improve target-domain model performance  [27].
Classification based on system architectures From 
the perspective of system organization, FL can be divided 
into centralized FL and decentralized FL, as illustrated in 
Fig.  3. Centralized federated learning  relies on a central 
server to broadcast the global model and aggregate model 
updates uploaded from distributed nodes, as shown in 
Fig.  3(a). This architecture is structurally clear and easy 
to implement, and thus is the most common form of FL. 
It offers advantages such as centralized aggregation and 
convenient scheduling, but heavily depends on the cen -
tral node and is vulnerable to single points of failure and 
communication bottlenecks  [28]. Decentralized federated 
learning, in contrast, removes the central server and lets 
participants exchange model parameters in a peer-to-peer 
manner, as depicted in Fig. 3(b). This architecture empha-
sizes peer collaboration, provides stronger robustness and 
scalability, and is suitable for scenarios without a trusted 
center or with stringent latency requirements. However, it 
poses greater challenges in terms of consistency mainte -
nance, parameter synchronization, and convergence sta -
bility, and typically leads to higher algorithmic and system 
complexity  [29].
Federated learning-based intrusion detection systems
Modern IDS face persistent challenges in terms of frag -
mented multi-source data, privacy and regulatory con -
straints, heterogeneous deployment environments, and 
continuously evolving attack behaviors. In this context, 
federated learning, with its “data remains local” collab -
orative training paradigm, provides a promising route for 
building privacy-preserving and distributed IDS. In this 
survey, we primarily focus on cloud–edge collaborative 
FL-IDS under a centralized horizontal training set -
ting. Our primary reference deployment is a site-level 
or cross-silo collaboration pattern , in which each cli -
ent corresponds to an organization, subnet, gateway, 
vehicle cluster, or edge site that keeps its own network-
flow records and/or system-log-derived features  
locally, performs periodic mini-batch federated train -
ing, and uploads model updates to a coordinating server. 
The trained model is then deployed back to the same site 
for near-real-time local detection . Therefore, the main 
analytical setting of this review is periodic federated 
training plus local online inference , rather than fully 
streaming end-to-end online learning. Vertical, trans -
fer, decentralized, and fine-grained cross-device variants 
are discussed when they materially change aggregation, 
transmission, or personalization design, but they are not 
the default deployment model assumed in this review.
(1) Distributed attack knowledge and large-scale 
data demand. For intrusion detection, the challenge 
is not merely the large volume of data, but also 
the fragmentation of security knowledge across 
organizations, subnets, and edge sites. Rare or 
emerging attacks may appear only in a subset of 
clients, making isolated local training insufficient for 
broad attack coverage. By keeping raw data on each 
client and uploading only local model parameters or 
gradients, FL significantly reduces data transmission 
and storage overhead compared with centralized 
learning, while enabling multiple participants to 
contribute complementary attack knowledge. For 
example, Lee et al. proposed the TornadoAggregate 
architecture, which leverages ring-based topologies 
to enhance the scalability of FL  [30], while Nguyen 
et al. designed the FedBuff framework, which buffers 
asynchronous updates so that the system maintains 
good elasticity even under highly concurrent 
participation  [31]. These studies suggest that FL can 
support collaborative IDS training at scale without 
requiring raw cross-organizational data sharing.
(2) Resource-constrained deployment and 
computational pressure. The computational issue 
in FL-IDS is closely tied to deployment. In practical 
cloud–edge environments, edge devices often need 
to support both local model updates and timely 
intrusion detection, while operating under limited 
computation, memory, and energy budgets. Under 
the FL framework, model training is distributed 
across multiple clients, reducing the dependence 
on centralized high-performance resources and 
making collaborative IDS more feasible in resource-
constrained settings. Abd Elaziz et al. offloaded

FL with differential privacy and homomorphic

DP-PFL framework  [34], and Cai et al. designed the 
SecFed framework  [35]; both enhance resistance 
to privacy leakage while maintaining competitive 
model performance, thus providing stronger privacy 
guarantees for cross-organizational collaborative IDS 
training.
Overall, FL alleviates several key bottlenecks of tradi -
tional IDS in terms of data sharing, distributed compu -
tation, and privacy-preserving collaboration, and has 
gradually become an important technical route for intel -
ligent intrusion detection. At the same time, the FL-IDS 
literature has expanded rapidly across system design, 
model optimization, and heterogeneous deployment 
scenarios. Accordingly, a number of survey papers have 
emerged to summarize this research landscape. To better 
position the present work, Table  1 compares representa-
tive existing surveys with this study.
Existing surveys provide valuable perspectives on FL-
based intrusion detection, but they differ substantially in 
analytical focus. Some studies are primarily system- or 
workflow-centered, emphasizing architectural design, 
deployment patterns, communication settings, and pri -
vacy mechanisms  [ 36, 38, 41]. These surveys are useful 
for understanding how FL-IDS systems are organized, yet 
they provide limited synthesis of the model-level opti -
mization choices that directly affect practical detection 
performance. Other studies are more scenario-specific, 
such as IoT-oriented or resource-constrained FL-IDS 
surveys   [ 37, 39, 42]. Their strength lies in capturing 
domain characteristics, but their analytical scope is often 
narrower and does not fully connect aggregation, trans -
mission, and personalization into a unified optimization 
framework. A third line of work adopts a more task-
centered perspective, such as collaborative anomaly 
detection   [40], which is helpful for understanding spe -
cific detection pipelines but still does not systematically 
explain how model optimization decisions shape deploy -
ability across heterogeneous FL-IDS environments.
Taken together, existing surveys have substantially 
improved the understanding of FL-IDS from the perspec-
tives of architectures, scenarios, and privacy/security 
mechanisms. However, they still provide limited analysis 
of the following question: under federated and heteroge -
neous cloud–edge settings, what model-level optimization 
decisions most strongly determine IDS deployability and 
detection quality? In particular, issues such as aggregation 
bias under non-IID attack distributions, transmission

Recent adjacent studies further suggest that practi -
cal FL-IDS deployability depends on more than model 
design alone. On the systems side, resource optimization, 
traffic-aware offloading, and adaptive service orchestra -
tion in cloud–edge environments directly affect latency, 
communication efficiency, and system robustness   [ 43–
45]. On the security side, recent cloud-security studies on 
VM threat prediction, malicious-agent identification, and 
federated malicious-user prediction indicate that infra -
structure risk and participant trustworthiness should also 
be considered when evaluating real-world FL-enabled 
security systems  [ 46– 49]. Although these works do not 
directly focus on FL-IDS model optimization, they high -
light important deployment-side factors that motivate 
broader and more realistic evaluation settings.
From this perspective, the goal of FL-IDS is not only 
to enable collaborative learning, but also to determine 
how the resulting models should be aggregated, trans -
mitted, and adapted to remain effective under heteroge -
neous attack distributions, communication constraints, 
and local deployment diversity. In this survey, aggrega -
tion, transmission, and personalization are therefore not 
treated as isolated axes, but as three tightly coupled 
model-optimization dimensions . Aggregation deter -
mines how distributed attack knowledge is integrated, 
transmission constrains how much model information 
can be exchanged under bandwidth and latency limita -
tions, and personalization defines how much knowledge 
should remain local to preserve client-specific detection 
capability. These dimensions interact strongly in practice: 
aggressive compression may alter aggregation bias, while 
personalization may change what should be shared glob -
ally and what should be retained locally.
To achieve practical FL-IDS deployment, the following 
three challenges are particularly critical:
(1) How can the global model be aggregated robustly 
under heterogeneity? Aggregation is the defining 
step that distinguishes FL from centralized learning 
and directly affects how heterogeneous attack 
knowledge from multiple clients is integrated. In 
FL-IDS, differences in client data distributions, 
computational capabilities, and update reliability 
can significantly distort aggregation quality. If not 
handled properly, global aggregation may suppress 
minority attack patterns, dilute rare but security-
critical signals, or amplify the impact of malicious 
and low-quality updates. Therefore, designing 
robust and efficient aggregation strategies remains a 
primary challenge for FL-IDS  [50].
(2) How can model transmission be made 
communication-efficient without losing security-
critical information? Communication efficiency is 
a major bottleneck in practical FL-IDS deployment. 
Deep detection models often contain a large 
number of parameters, and frequent uplink and 
downlink updates can incur high latency, bandwidth 
consumption, and device-side overhead, all of which 
undermine real-time and large-scale detection. 
At the same time, excessive compression, sparse 
transmission, or overly aggressive update reduction 
may discard subtle anomaly cues that are important 
for detecting stealthy or low-frequency attacks. It is 
therefore essential to optimize transmission through 
model compression, sparse updates, and lightweight 
communication strategies while preserving 
detection-relevant information  [51].
(3) How can federated models adapt to local 
environments and evolving attack patterns? 
Attack distributions, service contexts, and traffic 
characteristics vary substantially across devices 
and organizations, meaning that a single global 
model often cannot satisfy all clients equally 
well. This problem becomes more severe in edge 
environments, few-shot settings, and non-stationary 
scenarios where local traffic patterns evolve over 
time. Without personalization, FL-IDS models 
may experience increased false positives, false 
negatives, and degraded robustness under concept 
drift. Consequently, enabling effective adaptation 
to local data distributions and resource constraints, 
while preserving the benefits of global collaboration, 
constitutes the third major challenge  [52].
In summary, aggregation, transmission, and personal -
ization should not be understood as one-to-one prox -
ies for accuracy, efficiency, and adaptability. Rather, they 
form three tightly coupled optimization dimensions that 
jointly shape detection fidelity, communication cost, 
robustness to unreliable updates, adaptation under con -
cept drift, and deployment latency in FL-IDS. As illus -
trated in Fig.  4, these dimensions define the analytical 
framework adopted in this survey. Motivated by this 
coupling-aware view, the remainder of this paper sys -
tematically reviews existing studies from three perspec -
tives: aggregation optimization, communication-efficient 
transmission, and personalized modeling.

Model optimization techniques for federated 
intrusion detection
FL offers a promising paradigm for addressing data, com-
putation, and privacy constraints in IDS. However, prac -
tical federated IDS deployment faces challenges beyond 
data locality, including non-IID attack distributions, 
limited communication resources, heterogeneous client 
capabilities, and evolving local traffic patterns. These fac-
tors do not create isolated trade-offs; rather, they impose 
coupled optimization pressures on global knowledge 
integration, communication efficiency, local adaptation, 
robustness, and deployment latency.
As a result, recent research has shifted from system- 
and architecture-centric designs toward model-level 
optimization. Following the coupling-aware framework 
introduced in Section  “ Federated learning-based intru -
sion detection systems ” , this section reviews existing 
work from three closely related perspectives: aggregation 
optimization, communication-efficient transmission, 
and personalized modeling. Rather than treating them as 
independent categories, we emphasize how these strate -
gies interact in determining FL-IDS deployability and 
detection quality.
To strengthen the analytical lens of this review, we not 
only compare method families, but also track a set of 
recurring control parameters that reappear across Sec -
tions  “Global aggregation optimization ”–“Personalized 
modeling” . These parameters determine how local 
updates are weighted or delayed before aggregation, how 
much model information is transmitted under bandwidth 
constraints, and which subsets of parameters remain 
globally shareable or locally adaptive. In FL-IDS, their 
effects should be judged not only by convergence speed 
or communication reduction, but also by whether they 
preserve minority attack signatures, maintain robustness 
to unreliable updates, and support long-term adaptation

Across the surveyed literature, several FL-IDS-specific 
patterns emerge. First, aggressive communication reduc -
tion—through low-bit quantization, high sparsity prun -
ing, sketching, or infrequent update transmission—is 
most beneficial when bandwidth is the dominant deploy -
ment bottleneck, but these strategies may suppress weak 
gradients and low-amplitude feature shifts associated 
with rare attacks or stealthy anomalies. Second, distil -
lation-based transmission and heterogeneous aggrega -
tion improve architectural flexibility, yet their reliance 
on soft targets, proxy data, or representation alignment 
may smooth minority attack signatures and introduce 
additional privacy or robustness concerns. Third, full-
parameter personalization offers the strongest response 
to abrupt concept drift , but it also increases communi -
cation cost, model drift, and alert latency. By contrast, 
selective fine-tuning, adapters, prompts, and LoRA usu -
ally provide a better long-term trade-off for cloud–edge 
FL-IDS, although overly small trainable budgets may 
underfit sudden local attack shifts.
These observations indicate that client-weighting rules, 
staleness control, pruning rates, quantization bit-widths, 
update intervals, rank choices, and mask granularity 
should be treated as coupled control knobs  rather than 
isolated hyperparameters. Conservative settings often 
improve convergence stability and poisoning robustness, 
whereas aggressive compression or highly local personal -
ization may improve efficiency or client specificity at the 
cost of rare-attack recall, historical-attack retention, or 
cross-client generalization. Therefore, the key question in 
FL-IDS is not which single method family is universally 
best, but which parameter regime best matches the joint 
constraints of bandwidth, heterogeneity, threat rarity, 
drift intensity, and deployment latency.
Global aggregation optimization
Aggregation is a defining component that distinguishes 
FL from centralized training, determining how effectively 
heterogeneous multi-source attack knowledge is inte -
grated. In FL-IDS, its role goes beyond convergence and 
average accuracy: inadequate aggregation may amplify 
non-IID bias, suppress minority attack patterns, dilute 
rare but security-critical signals, and increase vulner -
ability to malicious or stale updates. Therefore, design -
ing robust aggregation strategies is critical for preserving 
detection coverage and reliability in heterogeneous IDS 
environments.
This section reviews progress in aggregation optimiza -
tion from three perspectives: classical aggregation algo -
rithms, asynchronous and semi-synchronous aggregation 
mechanisms, and heterogeneous model aggregation. A 
summary of representative studies is provided in Table  3.

Classical aggregation algorithms
Classical FL algorithms generally assume a central -
ized architecture where all clients share the same model 
structure. The central server aggregates client model 
parameters each communication round. FedAvg   [ 6]—
now widely adopted in privacy-sensitive domains such as 
healthcare, finance, and energy  [ 74, 75]—constructs the 
global model via data-size-weighted averaging.
To address these limitations, various extensions to 
FedAvg have been introduced. INA-FedAvg  [ 54] moves 
aggregation into intermediate routers within multi-hop 
wireless networks, significantly reducing communica -
tion cost—an important advantage for bandwidth-con -
strained IDS environments. SGX-FedAvg  [ 53] enhances 
security by performing aggregation inside Intel SGX 
enclaves and utilizing blockchain for verifiable consen -
sus, though scalability is constrained by enclave memory. 
Robustness-oriented methods such as SEAR  [57] remove 
anomalous or Byzantine updates through sampling-based 
outlier detection and coordinate-wise robust aggregation. 
FAIR   [55] and FEDLAW   [ 56] introduce quality-aware 
and learnable weighting schemes to enhance fairness and 
mitigate non-IID drift. In contrast, FedAF   [ 58] elimi -
nates parameter alignment altogether by using synthetic 
data and soft-label distillation, enabling reliable global 
training even under severe statistical heterogeneity.
Although classical aggregation remains effective in 
small-scale, homogeneous IDS deployments, its reliance 
on strict synchronization and uniform model structures 
fundamentally limits its applicability in complex network 
environments characterized by diverse communication 
protocols and rapidly evolving attack surfaces.
Asynchronous and semi-synchronous aggregation 
mechanisms
In synchronous FL, the server must wait for a sufficient 
number of clients, making the training process vulner -
able to “stragglers. ” Asynchronous aggregation addresses 
this by immediately incorporating client updates upon 
arrival. FedAsync  [ 59] adjusts update weights based on 
staleness to balance convergence speed and variance. 
FedFa   [60] introduces fully asynchronous updates with 
a sliding historical buffer, reducing oscillation caused by 
instantaneous updates. AREA  [61] further removes strict 
bounded-delay assumptions and introduces memory 
compensation to improve convergence stability under 
arbitrary delays. ARTU-FL   [ 62] incorporates statistical 
filtering (e.g., Kalman filtering) to maintain stable esti -
mates even under packet loss and delayed transmissions.
Semi-synchronous aggregation aims to strike a bal -
ance between efficiency and stability. FedBuff  [ 31] trig-
gers aggregation once a buffer of size K is filled, applying 
time-decay weighting and supporting secure aggregation 
and differential privacy. Time-slot–based aggregation   
[63] assigns weights by update freshness, suppressing 
variance under wireless constraints. Other works incor -
porate client selection based on gradient norms, compu -
tational capability, or link quality  [ 64– 66]. AS-AFL  [ 68] 
combines intra-group synchronization with inter-group 
asynchronous gossip to improve scalability in dynamic 
networks.
Asynchronous methods excel in handling bursty traf -
fic and unstable connections but often suffer from model 
staleness that degrades detection accuracy for rare 
attacks; semi-synchronous methods mitigate oscillation 
but require complex triggering policies. Consequently, 
improving robustness under strong non-IID traffic and 
optimizing scheduling latency to ensure timely threat 
response remain open challenges.
Heterogeneous model aggregation
FL increasingly encounters heterogeneous device capa -
bilities and model architectures. Traditional parameter-
level aggregation breaks down when clients do not share 
the same model structure, necessitating new aggregation 
strategies.
Knowledge distillation is a prominent approach. Fed-
ET  [70] builds soft-label consensus using variance-aware 
weighting of client predictions, enabling collaboration 
across heterogeneous models. FedGen   [ 73] introduces 
a global generator that mediates shared representation 
learning without requiring structural alignment. pFed -
Know  [69] leverages structure-aware weighting and pre-
trained teacher logits, combined with uncertainty-driven 
pseudo-label selection, making it suitable for constrained 
IoT deployments.
Another line of work decomposes client models into 
shared and private components. HPFL   [ 71] averages 
public parameters using validation-performance weight -
ing while privately aggregating cluster-level prototypes, 
enabling personalized yet privacy-preserving FL.
Partial-training approaches, such as FedRolex   [ 72], 
cyclically assign different sub-models to clients so that 
global parameters are fully updated over multiple rounds. 
This reduces local memory requirements and supports 
secure aggregation while maintaining compatibility with 
large global models.
While heterogeneous aggregation enables flexible 
cross-architecture collaboration, many methods depend 
on public datasets or large pre-trained models, increasing 
deployment cost and potential privacy exposure. Further-
more, complex aggregation workflows lead to additional 
computation and communication overhead. For feder -
ated IDS—characterized by dynamic traffic patterns and 
strong heterogeneity—improving generalization against 
zero-day attacks and reducing reliance on auxiliary data 
remain important research directions for robust IDS 
aggregation.

Model transmission optimization
Communication and computation overhead consti -
tute major bottlenecks in deploying federated intrusion 
detection systems. Deep detection models typically con -
tain millions of parameters, and frequent uploading and 
downloading not only con- sume bandwidth and intro -
duce significant latency but also increase energy usage on 
resource-constrained edge devices. In FL-IDS, however, 
transmission is not purely a systems issue: overly aggres -
sive compression or sparse communication may also dis -
card subtle anomaly cues that are important for detecting 
stealthy or low-frequency attacks. Therefore, reducing 
communication load while preserving detection-relevant 
information is essential for real-time and large-scale IDS 
deployment.
This section reviews transmission-optimization meth -
ods from three perspectives: model update compression 
and sparsification, pruning- and structure-based model 
lightweighting, and knowledge-distillation-based trans -
mission. A summary of representative studies is provided 
in Table  4.
Model parameter compression
Quantization-based compression is a classical and effec -
tive strategy for reducing communication load in FL. By 
lowering the numerical precision of model updates or 
gradients, transmission size can be significantly reduced 
with limited impact on accuracy. AdaQuantFL  [76] adap-
tively adjusts bit-width across training stages, using low 
precision in early rounds for rapid descent and gradually 
increasing precision later to control error accumulation. 
Its effectiveness, however, depends on accurate error 
scheduling and noise estimation.
Dithered-QFL  [ 78] and UVeQFed  [ 79] introduce sto -
chastic lattice-based quantization and universal vector 
quantization, respectively. Both methods preserve con -
vergence under extremely low-bit settings (e.g., 2–4 bits) 
by statistically neutralizing quantization noise, but they 
rely on synchronized randomness and simplified channel 
assumptions. Hier-Local-QSGD   [ 77] embeds quantiza -
tion into hierarchical FL and highlights a fundamental 
principle: when quantization noise becomes dominant, 
communication frequency should be reduced to main -
tain stability.
A second line of work reduces redundant communi -
cation via delta transmission . LFL   [ 80] transmits only 
global increments and employs error compensation, 
achieving convergence comparable to full-precision FL 
under strong convexity. Reisizadeh et al.   [ 81] combine 
delta transmission with periodic aggregation, signifi -
cantly reducing communication rounds in large-scale 
deployments, although long aggregation periods can 
intensify non-IID drift.
Beyond quantization, structured encoding aims for 
more aggressive compression. FedMUD   [ 82] distills 
model updates into compact tensor sequences, achieving 
bidirectional compression while relaxing dependence on 
specific model architectures. FedZip  [ 83] adopts multi-
stage compression—sparsification, clustered quantiza -
tion, and entropy coding—achieving high compression 
ratios with accuracy loss typically below 1%, though 
maintaining masks and codebooks increases system 
complexity.

For large-scale distributed IDS, quantization offers 
simplicity and rapid deployment but risks obscuring 
fine-grained features of stealthy attacks under high-fre -
quency updates. Structured encoding excels in extremely 
bandwidth-constrained settings but imposes additional 
encoding latency on resource-limited edge nodes. Delta 
transmission is often more suitable for IDS, as it lever -
ages the high redundancy of stable network traffic to 
minimize transmission. Consequently, designing attack-
aware adaptive delta-upload and compensation mecha -
nisms is a promising direction for balancing detection 
accuracy, communication efficiency, and real-time threat 
mitigation.
Model architecture optimization
Another important communication-optimization direc -
tion focuses on lightening the model itself via pruning 
and structural compression, thereby reducing parameter 
size at the source. Existing methods can be broadly cat -
egorized into pruning-driven subnetwork training and 
Sketch-based model representation.
Structured pruning removes channels, kernels, or 
attention heads while preserving tensor layouts, making 
it more hardware friendly. FedMP  [84] adaptively assigns 
pruning rates across clients to mitigate heterogeneous 
device capabilities, while still relying on residual recon -
struction to ensure convergence. FederatedPruning  [ 85] 
employs phased masks and hierarchical sparsity alloca -
tion, enabling large-scale speech tasks to maintain both 
accuracy and convergence. DF2-MPC  [ 87] extends per -
sonalized pruning to decentralized settings and com -
bines it with neighbor selection, improving privacy and 
stability. FedPE   [ 87] further alleviates information loss 
through alternating prune–expand cycles and residual 
compensation.
PruneFL   [ 88] dynamically reconfigures subnetwork 
structures to enhance sparse training adaptivity and 
inference efficiency, although sparse computation and 
mask management introduce extra complexity. FedPAGE  
[89] tightly couples pruning rate with communication 
delay, integrating model slimming into global efficiency 
optimization to mitigate the straggler effect in heteroge -
neous systems.
Hybrid pruning schemes combine structured and 
unstructured sparsification. Sub-FedAvg  [ 90] aggregates 
clients via subnetwork intersection, simultaneously sup -
porting personalization and communication reduction. 
FedPaI  [ 91] pushes extreme sparsification to the initial -
ization stage, removing the need for iterative pruning but 
requiring hardware support for sparse operators.
Sketch-based methods compress model representa -
tions without altering model architecture. Comfetch   
[92] employs CountSketch to compress both uplink and 
downlink updates, enabling lightweight clients that need 
not store the full model. FetchSGD  [ 93] exploits the lin -
earity of Sketch to aggregate momentum and error com -
pensation on the server, supporting stateless clients and 
large-scale one-shot participation, though high compres -
sion ratios may degrade accuracy and increase server 
memory demand.
Structured pruning emphasizes hardware efficiency 
and system throughput but may lack flexibility for highly 
non-IID and personalized IDS tasks. Unstructured and 
hybrid pruning better preserve client-specific features 
but depend on efficient sparse computation and mask 
management. Sketch-based methods offer architecture-
agnostic compression, reducing communication and 
storage costs, yet raise concerns regarding accuracy and 
server-side resource consumption. For IDS scenarios 
characterized by multi-source non-IID traffic and high-
frequency updates, striking a balance between flexibil -
ity, resource usage, and detection latency remains a core 
challenge.
Knowledge distillation transmission
Knowledge distillation can also be leveraged for model 
compression and communication reduction. Unlike het -
erogeneous-model aggregation in Section “ Global aggre-
gation optimization ” , where distillation mainly enables 
cross-architecture collaboration, here the focus is on 
transmitting knowledge from larger local models to light-
weight communication models.
In large–small model distillation frameworks, clients 
maintain a high-capacity local model for learning rich 
representations and a lightweight student model for 
communication. FedKD   [ 94] adopts a mentor–mentee 
design: the large model focuses on personalized learning, 
while the small model acts as a shared communication 
carrier, with bidirectional distillation maintaining con -
sistency between them. MH-pFLID   [ 95] introduces an 
ultra-lightweight “Messenger” model dedicated to infor -
mation exchange, naturally supporting heterogeneous 
client architectures and significantly reducing bandwidth. 
FedMKT   [ 96] further enables bidirectional knowledge 
transfer between client and server models, allowing the 
client-side model to gain global generalization while 
feeding back local characteristics to the server model.
These approaches, however, typically require clients to 
maintain multiple models, which may be impractical for 
weak devices, and often rely on public or auxiliary data, 
introducing privacy and robustness risks.
Compression-enhanced distillation further reduces 
communication load. QuPeD  [ 97] trains quantized stu -
dent models on clients and aligns them with the global 
model via distillation, achieving robust performance 
under heterogeneous data and resource constraints, 
albeit with inevitable degradation under aggressive quan -
tization. FedCompress   [ 98] combines client-weight

Overall, distillation-based transmission methods fol -

as communication proxies for larger models, effectively 
reducing communication while retaining much of the 
detection capability. This paradigm is particularly attrac -
tive for federated IDS: lightweight students can be rapidly 
deployed on edge devices to handle high-frequency traf -
fic, while high-capacity models continue to learn com -
plex attack patterns locally or in the cloud, enhancing 
long-term robustness and coverage.
Personalized modeling
Due to substantial heterogeneity in network environ -
ments, service types, and attack distributions, a single 
global model often fails to satisfy diverse detection 
requirements, particularly for edge nodes, few-sample 
domains, and non-stationary environments. In practical 
IDS deployment, local traffic patterns and attack surfaces 
may evolve over time, making concept drift and client-
specific adaptation central concerns. Personalized mod -
eling addresses this limitation by adapting local models 
on top of shared global knowledge, thereby enhancing 
the practicality, stability, and long-term usability of feder-
ated IDS across heterogeneous clients.
This section reviews representative approaches from 
four perspectives: full-parameter fine-tuning, selective 
fine-tuning of layers or modules, adapter/prompt-based 
personalization, and low-rank adaptation. We also dis -
cuss the trade-off between personalization capability and 
global collaboration. A summary of related work is pro -
vided in Table  5.
Full-parameter fine-tuning
Full-parameter fine-tuning updates all model parameters 
end-to-end on top of a pre-trained model. In a federated 
setting, each client locally updates the complete model 
and periodically uploads its parameters or gradients for 
aggregation. This paradigm usually offers the strongest 
adaptation capability but incurs substantial communica -
tion and computation costs, and imposes strict memory 
and compute requirements on client devices.
FedKSeed   [ 99] is a representative method for com -
pressed random reconstruction. By sharing random 
seeds to generate perturbation vectors, clients only need 
to upload seeds and scalar gradients, while the server 
reconstructs approximate updates, significantly reducing 
communication cost. Ferret  [100] further combines first-
order optimization with low-dimensional projections to 
retain the convergence speed of gradient-based meth -
ods while preserving communication efficiency, allevi -
ating the slow convergence issue of purely zeroth-order 
schemes. These methods achieve extremely compact 
communication and are well-suited to large models and 
bandwidth-constrained environments, but they require 
carefully maintained shared randomness and must bal -
ance reconstruction accuracy against efficiency.
OFMS-FT   [ 101] adopts direct full-parameter fine-
tuning with full-gradient uploads: clients select candidate 
models under limited memory, perform full-parameter 
updates, and then upload gradients or parameter dif -
ferences for bandwidth-aware aggregation. This type of 
method can deliver accuracy close to ideal centralized 
fine-tuning and is suitable for highly demanding tasks, 
but still entails considerable communication and compu -
tation overhead. Building on this line, FTFA and RTFA   
[102] first obtain a global model via FedAvg and then 
perform client-side full-parameter fine-tuning; RTFA 
additionally introduces regularization to constrain model 
drift and balance global and local objectives.

Fed-POE   [ 103] focuses on online personalization in 
non-stationary environments. It integrates locally fine-
tuned models with current and historical global models 
through a product-of-experts ensemble, effectively miti -
gating catastrophic forgetting. This improves adaptability 
in dynamic distributions at the cost of additional storage 
and inference computation.
Full-parameter fine-tuning offers superior local adapta-
tion capability and can fully exploit local data in highly 
non-IID and dynamic environments. However, it suffers 
from two fundamental issues: frequent full-parameter 
updates incur substantial communication and storage 
overhead, and intensive local training may lead to severe 
deviation from the global model, increasing the risk of 
catastrophic forgetting. Recent work partially alleviates 
these bottlenecks through perturbation reconstruction, 
low-dimensional projection, regularization, and online 
ensembling, but how to balance communication cost 
with adaptability without compromising the detection 
baseline for historical attack signatures remains an open 
problem.
Selective fine-tuning
Selective fine-tuning updates only a subset of parameters 
while freezing the rest of the model, such as output lay -
ers, biases, normalization statistics, or a few deeper or 
intermediate layers. Compared with full-parameter fine-
tuning, this paradigm trades reduced updates for signifi -
cant savings in computation and communication, making 
it naturally suitable for heterogeneous devices and band -
width-constrained federated scenarios. Existing research 
mainly progresses along two lines: structural decoupling 
and adaptive selection.
From a structural-decoupling perspective, the model is 
explicitly partitioned into shared and personalized com -
ponents, yielding stable personalization gains with mini -
mal protocol changes. FedPer   [ 104] treats lower layers 
as shared feature extractors participating in aggregation, 
while upper layers remain local to capture user-specific 
patterns. PartialFed  [106] observes that full initialization 
with global parameters may overwrite local memory, thus 
only selected layers are synchronized while the remaining 
parameters continue from local history. FedAlt  [107] fur-
ther splits parameters into shared and personalized parts 
and optimizes them in an alternating fashion, improving 
stability and memory efficiency.
Adaptive-selection methods instead let data and train -
ing dynamics determine which parameters to update, 
aiming to achieve near full-parameter adaptation under 
limited budget. Selective Layer Fine-tuning   [ 105] uses 
local gradient norms and consistency regularization to 
select the most beneficial layers under a compute budget 
and introduces light coordination to avoid aggregation 
bias. FedSelect   [ 108], inspired by the gradient lottery 
ticket hypothesis, tracks parameter-change magnitudes 
and gradually expands a personalized subnetwork, allow -
ing different clients to form bounded yet distinct person -
alization masks.
Selective fine-tuning thus achieves efficient person -
alization with limited updates: it reduces the load on 
resource-constrained devices while still capturing site-
specific attack variants on top of shared global represen -
tations. However, it faces common challenges. Overly 
rigid layer choices may limit adaptation to new threat 
patterns, whereas adaptive schemes introduce extra over-
head and potential instability. As layer-level and parame -
ter-level selection strategies mature, selective fine-tuning 
is expected to further strengthen the trade-off between 
lightweight deployment and personalization, especially in 
IDS scenarios facing frequent concept drift and polymor-
phic attacks
Adapter and prompt-based fine-tuning
Adapter-based fine-tuning freezes the backbone and 
only trains small, pluggable modules (e.g., adapters or 
prompts), achieving task alignment and personalization 
with minimal parameter overhead. In FL, this paradigm 
is particularly attractive for heterogeneous and resource-
limited environments due to its low communication foot-
print and high composability.
Adapter-based approaches emphasize the pattern of 
“frozen backbone + small trainable modules. ” FedTT   
[110] employs tensor-decomposed adapters to compress 
updates, and further freezes part of the factors adaptively 
to improve robustness across institutions. FedDAT  [109] 
introduces bidirectional distillation between shared and 
local adapters to reduce knowledge forgetting, while 
FedCLIP  [ 111] uses attention-based adapters for cross-
domain generalization and personalization. AdaFedSe -
lecKD  [ 112] combines selective distillation with adapter 
updates, updating local adapters only when global knowl-
edge is deemed reliable. These methods aim to use small 
modules to support transfer, striking a balance between 
communication efficiency and stable aggregation.
Prompt-based methods compress trainable capac -
ity even further into a small number of vectors injected 
into inputs or hidden layers, enabling fast personaliza -
tion without modifying backbone weights. MetePFL   
[113] leverages spatio-temporal prompts together with 
graph-based aggregation to mitigate non-IID effects. 
FedBPT   [114] applies zeroth-order evolutionary search 
over prompt distributions in LLM, achieving extremely 
low communication and storage overhead. pFedPG  [115] 
allows the server to generate personalized prompts and 
refine them using client feedback, enhancing cross-
domain generalization. Prompt methods are highly 
lightweight and often compatible with black-box mod -
els, but their expressive power and convergence stability 

Page 17 of 22
Ma et al. Journal of Cloud Computing            (2026) 15:88 
frequently require additional support from distillation or 
generative mechanisms.
Some work, such as Sun   [ 125], unifies bias tun -
ing, adapters, and prompts into a single framework, 
allowing systems to flexibly choose parameter subsets 
given resource constraints and task requirements. This 
modular design offers a “switchboard” for deployment: 
prompts can be preferred when bandwidth is tight, while 
adapters or hybrid schemes can be activated when stron -
ger expressiveness is needed.
Overall, adapter and prompt-based fine-tuning lever -
age small parameter increments to unlock transfer ben -
efits: adapters provide structured adjustments, while 
prompts encode local traffic contexts. In multi-source, 
dynamically evolving IDS scenarios, this allows fast adap-
tation under tight bandwidth and preserves local threat 
landscapes through modular memory. The key challenges 
lie in maintaining stable convergence and long-term 
adaptability under extremely small parameter budgets, 
and in designing mechanisms for adaptive module and 
granularity selection across resource-constrained IoT 
and edge devices.
Low-rank adaptation
Low-rank adaptation (LoRA) inserts trainable low-rank 
decompositions into weight matrices while keeping back-
bone parameters frozen. Unlike adapters, LoRA does not 
introduce additional forward paths; instead, it adds resid-
ual low-rank updates directly to existing weights, which 
can be merged at inference time. This “low-rank patch -
ing” paradigm enables parameter-efficient adaptation 
without altering model structure and has strong potential 
for federated fine-tuning of large models.
Existing LoRA-based federated methods can be 
grouped into three categories. The first focuses on homo-
geneous ranks , where all clients adopt LoRA modules 
with the same rank, making aggregation straightforward. 
FedIT  [116] applies LoRA for instruction tuning of large 
language models on clients and aggregates LoRA param -
eters via FedAvg, significantly reducing the cost of fed -
erated LLM training. Kim  [ 117] and Guo  [ 118] further 
refine aggregation: the latter observes that LoRA matri -
ces A and B exhibit different behaviors (with A more 
global and B more personalized), and aggregates only A 
while retaining B locally to mitigate aggregation error 
and improve stability under non-IID distributions.
The second category targets heterogeneous ranks , 
allowing clients to choose different ranks based on their 
resources and data, at the cost of dimension mismatch 
and aggregation noise. SLoRA  [119] uses sparse fine-tun-
ing followed by singular value decomposition to provide 
better low-rank initialization, alleviating convergence 
issues in heterogeneous environments. HeLoRA   [ 120] 
proposes two complementary solutions: context-based 
padding with adaptive weighting to align different ranks 
for aggregation, and distillation-based deep mutual 
learning to achieve “parameter-agnostic” collaboration. 
FLoRA  [121] further addresses aggregation noise by pro-
posing a stacking-based aggregation scheme that stabi -
lizes LoRA integration under heterogeneous ranks.
The third category considers personalized ranks , tai -
loring LoRA configurations to both resource and task 
characteristics. ReCIT   [ 122] demonstrates that even 
low-rank gradients can leak sensitive training data 
through reconstruction attacks, highlighting the need for 
stronger privacy mechanisms in parameter-efficient fine-
tuning. FLFT   [ 123] showcases LoRA-based federated 
tuning in sensitive domains such as defense, using limited 
communication rounds to achieve cross-institution opti -
mization. FDLoRA  [ 124] decomposes LoRA into global 
and local components and adaptively fuses them, balanc -
ing shared knowledge and personalized adaptation.
LoRA-based tuning uses rank as a central control 
dimension to flexibly trade off communication overhead, 
personalization, and stability: homogeneous ranks facili -
tate deployment and sharing, heterogeneous ranks bet -
ter match real-world resource diversity, and personalized 
ranks align with task-specific needs. However, challenges 
remain in noise-free aggregation under heterogeneity, 
preserving expressiveness at extremely low ranks, and 
defending against privacy leakage. For the emerging gen -
eration of LLM-based IDS, LoRA offers a critical solution 
to the memory bottleneck on edge devices. By freezing 
the massive backbone parameters, it effectively prevents 
Out-of-Memory errors, making it feasible to deploy 
foundation-model-level detection capabilities—previ -
ously reserved for cloud centers—directly onto resource-
constrained edge gateways.
Conclusion and outlook
This survey reviews recent advances in FL-based IDS 
from a model optimization perspective. Distinct from 
architecture-centric studies, we organize the literature 
around three tightly coupled methodological dimensions: 
aggregation for heterogeneous knowledge integration, 
communication-efficient transmission under deployment 
constraints, and personalization for client-specific adap -
tation in non-IID and dynamic environments. Across 
these dimensions, we compare representative meth -
ods and highlight how their interactions jointly shape 
detection fidelity, communication cost, robustness, and 
deployability in FL-IDS.
Aggregation mechanisms: conclusion and outlook
Aggregation is fundamental to federated IDS and directly 
affects global model accuracy and convergence stability. 
Classical synchronous aggregation methods are simple 
and theoretically well grounded, but they struggle with 

Page 18 of 22
Ma et al. Journal of Cloud Computing            (2026) 15:88 
scalability in the presence of computational heteroge -
neity, non-IID distributions, and unstable communica -
tion. To address these limitations, a series of improved 
strategies—such as asynchronous aggregation, semi-
synchronous aggregation, and heterogeneous-model 
aggregation—have been proposed   [ 126, 127]. These 
methods alleviate the impact of stragglers, enable cross-
model collaboration, and enhance robustness, but they 
also introduce new challenges, including performance 
oscillation, increased algorithmic and system complexity, 
and in some cases reliance on auxiliary public data  [128].
Transmission optimization: conclusion and outlook
Communication efficiency is a decisive factor for the 
practical deployment of federated IDS. Quantization, 
compression, and delta updates can significantly reduce 
transmitted bits, while distillation and pruning further 
enhance adaptability in heterogeneous environments. 
Pruning, in particular, has shown strong potential in IDS 
tasks due to its high compression ratio and interpretabil -
ity   [129]. Nevertheless, most existing methods rely on 
static compression strategies that do not adapt to evolv -
ing attack traffic patterns or changing task difficulty, 
potentially causing degraded detection performance for 
minority attacks or long-term deployments. Moreover, 
some solutions depend on additional modules or external 
data sources, increasing deployment overhead.
Personalized modeling: conclusion and outlook
Personalization aims to address the inadequacy of a 
single global model under non-IID distributions. Full-
parameter fine-tuning provides the strongest adaptability 
but incurs excessive communication and computation 
costs. Selective fine-tuning strikes a balance between 
efficiency and performance, though its fixed layer-selec -
tion design may limit flexibility. Lightweight approaches 
such as adapter-based tuning and LoRA offer effective 
transfer and adaptation with minimal overhead   [ 130]. 
Overall, personalization has demonstrated clear ben -
efits in improving detection coverage and reducing false 
alarms, yet challenges remain, including instability under 
dynamic environments, sensitivity to local data quality, 
and limited cross-domain generalization.
Future research directions
Although aggregation, transmission, and personaliza -
tion provide a useful model-optimization lens for FL-
IDS, practical deployment will increasingly depend on 
how these dimensions are co-designed with resource 
scheduling, task offloading, service orchestration, and 
broader security management in real cloud–edge systems  
[131]. Recent adjacent studies on serverless FL resource 
optimization, traffic-aware edge offloading, SLA-aware 
orchestration, VM threat prediction, and malicious-user 
identification further indicate that model design alone 
is insufficient to ensure robust and deployable federated 
security systems   [ 132]. Future research may proceed 
along the following directions:
Coupling-aware heterogeneous aggregation:  Future 
FL-IDS aggregation should move beyond homogeneous 
averaging and explicitly optimize the interaction among 
structural heterogeneity, asynchronous participation, 
and trustworthiness of updates. Transfer-based aggrega -
tion, shared representation alignment, and utility-aware 
weighting may help integrate heterogeneous models 
without suppressing minority attack patterns. At the 
same time, aggregation policies should become more 
aware of rare-attack coverage, stale updates, and poi -
soning robustness, rather than focusing only on average 
convergence.
Adaptive and attack-aware transmission optimiza -
tion: Communication-efficient FL-IDS should not rely 
on static compression policies. Instead, quantization 
level, pruning rate, delta-update frequency, and sketching 
intensity should adapt to attack intensity, traffic volatility, 
client capability, and latency requirements. In particular, 
future work should study how to reduce communica -
tion load while preserving subtle anomaly cues, so that 
compression does not disproportionately harm stealthy 
attacks, low-frequency events, or long-horizon detection 
tasks.
Drift-aware and parameter-efficient personaliza -
tion: Personalization remains essential because local 
traffic patterns, service contexts, and attack surfaces 
evolve over time. Future research should design adaptive 
module insertion, layer selection, mask update, and rank 
adjustment mechanisms that allow clients to dynamically 
decide how much knowledge should be shared globally 
and how much should remain local. A promising direc -
tion is to combine parameter-efficient tuning with drift 
detection and lightweight cross-client transfer, thereby 
improving local adaptability without incurring the full 
cost of end-to-end retraining.
Resource-aware orchestration and offloading for 
cloud–edge FL-IDS:  Model optimization alone does not 
determine deployability. In realistic cloud–edge systems, 
FL-IDS must also coordinate where training, inference, 
and security services are executed, how communication 
and compute resources are sliced, and when tasks should 
be offloaded or deferred. Recent studies on serverless 
federated resource optimization, traffic-aware mobile 
edge offloading, and SLA-aware adaptive service orches -
tration show that scheduling and orchestration policies 
can directly affect delay, bandwidth efficiency, and system 
robustness. Integrating these mechanisms with FL-IDS is 
therefore an important step toward deployment-oriented 
optimization.

Page 19 of 22
Ma et al. Journal of Cloud Computing            (2026) 15:88 
Realistic testbeds, benchmarks, and broader secu -
rity validation:  Current FL-IDS evaluations are still 
dominated by static datasets and simplified simulations. 
Future studies should move toward realistic cloud–edge 
testbeds, online attack streams, and benchmarks that 
jointly evaluate detection fidelity, communication over -
head, concept-drift adaptation, and robustness to unre -
liable or malicious participants. Adjacent cloud-security 
studies on VM threat prediction, workload-aware secure 
resource management, malicious-agent identification, 
quantum malicious-user prediction, and federated mali -
cious-user prediction suggest that future FL-IDS bench -
marks should also consider infrastructure risk, user 
trustworthiness, and secure data-distribution dynamics 
as part of broader deployment validation  [48, 49].
In summary, the next stage of FL-IDS research should 
move from isolated method improvement toward 
deployment-oriented co-optimization. The central chal -
lenge is not a simple balance among accuracy, efficiency, 
and adaptability, but the joint optimization of detection 
fidelity, communication cost, robustness to unreliable 
updates, adaptation under concept drift, resource-aware 
orchestration, and realistic system validation. Addressing 
these coupled trade-offs is essential for enabling reliable, 
scalable, and privacy-preserving next-generation IDS.
