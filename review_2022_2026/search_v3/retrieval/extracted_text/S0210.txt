 Enterprise cybersecurity programmes are increasingly judged not only by 
whether they satisfy compliance controls, but by whether they can 
preserve essential services during disruptive cyber events. Existing 
maturity assessments remain useful for organizing governance and control 
evidence, yet many rely on ordinal self -assessment, checklist scoring, and 
retrospective audit observations that are weakly connected to real -time 
telemetry, residual risk, and recovery performance. This paper develops a 
mathematically rooted Enterprise Cybersecurity Operational Resilience 
Maturity Index (ECORMI) that integrates artificial intelligence, 
governance, risk, and compliance analytics into a single risk -adjusted 
maturity assessment framework. The proposed approach models maturity 
as a latent enterprise capability estimated from normalized indicators 
across four construct blocks: AI-derived cyber intelligence, GRC evidence, 
residual cyber-risk exposure, and operational resilience performance. The 
methodology formalizes featu re normalization, incident -disruption 
probability estimation, residual -risk computation, service -degradation 
resilience, maturity aggregation, construct reliability, and sensitivity 
testing. A simulated enterprise benchmark is used to illustrate how 
ECORMI-AI-GRC can outperform checklist maturity scoring, C2M2/CSF -
style ordinal assessment, risk -matrix GRC scoring, and an ML -only SIEM 
baseline in predictive accuracy, F1 -score, balanced accuracy, maturity -
ranking stability, and decision interpretability. The results suggest that 
compliance evidence alone can overstate resilience when unresolved

INTRODUCTION 
Cybersecurity has shifted from a technical protection 
problem into an enterprise operational -resilience 
problem. A ransomware event, cloud identity 
compromise, destructive insider incident, supply -
chain breach, or large -scale data exfiltration can 
impair revenue processes, customer service, safety -
critical operations, regulatory reporting, and board 
accountability. For that reason, a high -maturity 
enterprise cannot be evaluated only by the number of 
documented policies, firewalls, or controls it possesses 
(James, 2026) . It must be evaluated by how well it 
anticipates cyber disruption, absorbs degradation, 
recovers critical services, learns from near misses, and 
realigns governance decisions with measured risk. 
NIST CSF 2.0 formalizes this movement by placing 
governance alongside identify, protect, detect, 
respond, and recover functions, while NIST cyber -
resiliency guidance emphasizes the ability to 
anticipate, withstand, recover from, and adapt to 
adverse cyber conditions (National Institute of 
Standards and Technology [NIST], 2024; Ross et al., 
2021). 
The central weakness in many enterprise maturity 
assessments is that the score is still produced through 
interviews, ordinal self -ratings, audit checklists, or 
control-presence evidence. These instruments are 
useful for establishing accountability, but they often 
fail to capture the dynamic exposure created by 
unresolved vulnerabilities, privilege escalation paths, 
delayed incident closure, inconsistent recovery tests, 
control drift, third -party concentration, and threat -
intelligence signals. A business unit can therefore 
appear mature because its compliance folder is 
complete while its actual operational capability 
remains fragile  (Adewale, 2026) . This paper treats 
such inconsistency as a measurement problem: 
maturity should be a quantitative construct inferred 
from cyber telemetry, risk indicators, governance 
evidence, and resilience outcomes rather than a static 
compliance label. 
The paper proposes an Enterprise Cybersecurity 
Operational Resilience Maturity Index, abbreviated as 
ECORMI, supported by artificial intelligence and 
integrated governance, risk, and compliance analytics. 
The core premise is that enterprise cyber -resilience 
maturity is a latent variable that cannot be observed 
directly but can be estimated from normalized, risk -
weighted indicators. AI contributes predictive 
inference, anomaly scoring, feature attribution, and 
probabilistic disruption estimation. GRC analytics 
contributes control ownership, policy coverage, 
audit-trail completeness, risk appetite, regulatory 
evidence, remediation status, and board reporting

This integration is necessary because enterprise 
resilience is not produced by cybersecurity 
technology alone. It is produced by a socio -technical 
control system in which technical detection, 
governance accountability, regulatory evidence, risk 
prioritization, and recovery execution reinforce one 
another. COBIT, ISO/IEC 27001, ISO 22301, NIST SP 
800-53, and the Cybersecurity Capability Maturity 
Model provide valuable control and governance 
language, but they require mathematical 
operationalization when used for predictive maturity 
scoring (Caralli et al., 2011; Department of Energy, 
2022; International Organization for Standardization 
[ISO], 2019, 2022a; ISACA, 2019; NIST, 2020). 
The research problem addressed in this paper is 
therefore as follows: existing maturity frameworks 
provide structured control assessment, but they 
insufficiently integrate AI -driven cyber -risk 
intelligence, real -time residual -risk exposure, GRC 
evidence quality, and operational recovery 
performance into a defensible mathematical scoring 
model. The research gap is especially visible in 
enterprises that maintain strong compliance 
documentation but experience repeated service 
interruption, slow recovery, weak control ownership, 
and inconsistent treatment of cyber-risk appetite. The 
contribution of this paper is a formalized maturity 
assessment model that transforms heterogeneous 
cyber and GRC evidence into a risk -adjusted, 
explainable, and decision-ready score. 
The aim of the study is to develop and discuss a 
quantitative AI-GRC maturity assessment framework 
for enterprise cybersecurity operational resilience. 
The objectives are to define measurable maturity 
constructs, formulate a mathematical index for cyber-
resilience maturity, design an AI -supported GRC 
analytics pipeline, benchmark the proposed algorithm 
against existing scoring methods, and specify 
validation criteria suitable for enterprise audit, risk, 
and board -review contexts. The research questions 
are: how can AI -derived risk intelligence be 
combined with governance and compliance evidence 
to estimate maturity; how should residual risk be 
penalized in maturity scoring; how can service 
degradation and recovery performance be included 
mathematically; and how does the proposed method 
compare with checklist, ordinal maturity, risk-matrix, 
and ML-only baselines? 
The expected contribution is theoretical, 
methodological, and managerial. Theoretically, the 
paper reframes cyber -resilience maturity as a 
multidimensional latent construct governed by 
evidence quality, risk exposure, resilience 
performance, and model interpretability. 
Methodologically, it introduces equations for feature 
normalization, disruption probability, residual risk, 
resilience degradation, index aggregation, reliability 
testing, and sensitivity analysis. Managerially, it gives 
executives a maturity score that is more suitable for 
cyber-investment prioritization, control remediation, 
internal audit planning, regulatory accountability, 
and crisis -readiness monitoring than a single static 
compliance grade.

Literature Review 
2.1 Enterprise Cybersecurity Maturity Models and 
Their Measurement Limits 
Cybersecurity maturity models organize enterprise 
capability into staged, repeatable, managed, and 
optimized states. The intellectual lineage is linked to 
capability maturity modelling in software and process 
improvement, where institutionalization, 
repeatability, measurement, and continuous 
improvement distinguish immature from mature 
organizations (Humphrey, 1988; Paulk et al., 1993). In 
cybersecurity, maturity models adapted these ideas to 
controls, asset management, identity governance, 
vulnerability management, monitoring, incident 
response, and executive oversight. CERT -RMM and 
C2M2 are especially relevant because they connect 
operational resilience and cybersecurity practices to 
enterprise risk management, service continuity, and 
process institutionalization (Caralli et al., 2011; 
Department of Energy, 2022). 
Despite their usefulness, many maturity models 
remain semi-quantitative. Assessment units are often 
scored through ordinal levels, expert judgement, or 
binary control presence. Such methods are easy to 
communicate but difficult to validate statistically. 
They may also hide important differences among 
enterprises with the same maturity level. For instance, 
two organizations may both be rated Level 3 because 
they have documented incident response processes, 
yet one may recover customer -facing services within 
four hours while the other takes several days. This 
suggests that maturity classification should be linked 
to observed cyber outcomes and recovery indicators, 
not merely to documented procedural existence. 
The measurement challenge becomes sharper when 
enterprises integrate cloud platforms, distributed 
identity systems, DevOps pipelines, remote work, IoT 
devices, operational technology, and third -party 
digital services. The attack surface becomes dynamic, 
while governance evidence remains periodic. 
Traditional control -based assessments are therefore 
necessary but insufficient. Maturity scoring must be 
capable of ingesting machine -scale evidence from 
SIEM, EDR, vulnerability scanners, identity providers,

2.2 Operational Resilience as a Cybersecurity 
Outcome 
Operational resilience extends cybersecurity maturity 
beyond prevention. It evaluates whether an 
enterprise can continue delivering critical services 
despite cyber stress. Resilience theory emphasizes 
absorption, recovery, adaptation, redundancy, 
situational awareness, and organizational learning 
(Hollnagel et al., 2011; Linkov et al., 2013; Woods, 
2015). In cyber contexts, these capabilities are 
expressed through recovery -time achievement, 
backup restoration, failover integrity, 
communications discipline, continuity -test 
performance, alternate -process readiness, and cyber -
crisis governance. NIST SP 800 -160 Volume 2 
Revision 1 provides a systems -security engineering 
view of cyber resiliency that is particularly relevant 
because it links risk reduction to survivability and 
mission assurance (Ross et al., 2021). 
A resilience -based maturity score must therefore 
include indicators that reflect service degradation and 
restoration, not only control presence. Recovery Time 
Objective compliance, Recovery Point Objective 
compliance, mean time to detect, mean time to 
contain, mean time to recover, percentage of critical 
applications covered by tested recovery plans, and the 
proportion of resilience exercises closed with verified 
remediation are measurable variables (Onwuzurike, & 
Kpogli, 2025). These variables also allow a 
mathematical link between maturity and operational 
performance. The more directly maturity scoring 
reflects continuity outcomes, the more useful it 
becomes for board -level decision -making, especially 
in regulated sectors such as banking, energy, 
healthcare, telecommunications, and public 
infrastructure. 
Operational resilience also changes the interpretation 
of residual cyber risk. A vulnerability on an isolated 
non-critical asset may not reduce enterprise resilience 
materially, while a lower -severity control weakness 
on a payment -processing identity platform may 
produce major business -continuity exposure. For this 
reason, ECORMI models residual risk as a weighted 
function of likelihood, impact, exposure, and control 
effectiveness. This approach is compatible with risk 
quantification traditions that treat risk as a function 
of probability and consequence but extends them 
with governance and recovery evidence (Aven, 2016; 
Gordon & Loeb, 2002; Hubbard & Seiersen, 2016; The 
Open Group, 2021). 
2.3 Integrated Governance, Risk, and Compliance 
Analytics 
GRC analytics integrates policy, risk, compliance, 
control testing, audit trails, remediation evidence, 
accountability structures, and executive reporting. In 
cybersecurity maturity assessment, GRC evidence is 
important because it shows whether controls are 
owned, tested, documented, remediated, and aligned 
with risk appetite. ISO/IEC 27001:2022 provides a 
management-system basis for information security 
governance, while ISO/IEC 27005:2022 supports risk 
management, and ISO 31000:2018 supplies general 
risk principles (ISO, 2018, 2022a, 2022b). COBIT 2019 
contributes governance objectives and decision rights, 
while NIST SP 800 -37 and SP 800 -39 emphasize risk 
management across organizational, mission, and 
system tiers (ISACA, 2019; NIST, 2011, 2018). 
The weakness of many GRC systems is not the 
absence of data but the inability to convert evidence 
into predictive decision intelligence. Audit findings, 
exceptions, policy attestations, third -party 
questionnaires, and remediation tasks may exist in 
separate repositories. Without integration, executives 
receive a fragmented view of cyber posture. AI -

2.4 Artificial Intelligence for Cyber -Risk Prediction 
and Maturity Intelligence 
AI methods can strengthen maturity assessment by 
estimating cyber -disruption probability, detecting 
abnormal telemetry patterns, predicting incident 
severity, classifying audit findings, and identifying 
features that drive residual risk. Supervised classifiers 
such as logistic regression, random forests, gradient 
boosting, and XGBoost are useful when labeled 
historical incidents are available (Breiman, 2001; 
Chen & Guestrin, 2016; Friedman, 2001). 
Unsupervised and semi-supervised anomaly detection 
is useful where labels are incomplete, which is 
common in enterprise cybersecurity because many 
near misses, unauthorized behaviours, and control 
weaknesses are not formally recorded (Chandola et al., 
2009; Mirsky et al., 2018; Sommer & Paxson, 2010). 
AI maturity analytics must remain interpretable. A 
maturity score that cannot be explained to risk 
committees, auditors, regulators, and business owners 
is operationally weak even when its predictive 
accuracy is high. Explainable AI approaches such as 
LIME and SHAP have become standard methods for 
identifying feature contributions, while broader 
interpretability literature emphasizes accountability 
and domain -grounded explanation (Arrieta et al., 
2020; Doshi -Velez & Kim, 2017; Lundberg & Lee, 
2017; Ribeiro et al., 2016). In ECORMI, explainability 
is not treated as an optional visualization. It is part of 
model governance because maturity classification 
must be traceable to control evidence, residual 
exposure, service recovery, and business impact. 
The literature also warns against ungoverned AI 
adoption in security systems. ML models can suffer 
from dataset bias, adversarial manipulation, drift, poor 
calibration, and hidden technical debt (Biggio & Roli, 
2018; Goodfellow et al., 2015; Sculley et al., 2015). 
Therefore, the methodology includes validation 
criteria that combine predictive performance, 
reliability, sensitivity, and explainability. These 
criteria align with the NIST AI Risk Management 
Framework, which emphasizes trustworthy AI 
characteristics and lifecycle risk management (NIST, 
2023).

2.6 Synthesis and Theoretical Positioning 
The reviewed literature supports three conclusions. 
First, maturity models provide structure, but ordinal 
control scoring is not sufficiently sensitive to dynamic 
cyber exposure. Second, operational resilience 
provides the correct outcome orientation because 
cybersecurity must preserve critical services under 
stress. Third, AI and GRC analytics must be 
integrated because AI contributes predictive 
intelligence while GRC contributes accountability, 
evidence integrity, and governance legitimacy. The 
proposed framework therefore positions enterprise 
cyber-resilience maturity as a measurable construct 
that should satisfy construct coverage, predictive 
association, risk sensitivity, recovery validity, and 
explainability. 
The theoretical foundation of this paper is that 
maturity is not a single observable attribute. It is a 
latent enterprise capability inferred from indicators 
that represent control strength, exposure reduction, 
governance accountability, incident responsiveness, 
and adaptive recovery. The quality of the maturity 
score depends on whether the selected indicators 
cover the construct, whether the weights reflect risk 
appetite, whether the AI component predicts 
meaningful outcomes, and whether the score changes 
logically when residual risk or recovery capability 
changes. This logic leads directly to the mathematical 
specification in Section 3.

Method 
3.1 Research Design and Unit of Analysis 
The study adopts a quantitative design supported by 
mathematical model development, AI -enabled 
predictive analytics, and benchmark validation. The 
unit of analysis may be an enterprise, business unit, 
critical service, region, technology domain, or 
resilience control cluster. The preferred 
implementation unit is the critical business service 
because operational resilience is ultimately judged by 
whether essential services remain available, 
recoverable, and governed under cyber stress. 
However, the model is flexible enough to score an 
entire enterprise or a portfolio of services if the 
evidence is normalized to a common structure. 
The method follows the uploaded outline by 
transforming raw cyber and GRC evidence into a 
normalized feature matrix, classifying indicators into 
construct blocks, estimating AI -derived disruption 
likelihood, computing residual risk, estimating 
recovery resilience, aggregating the maturity index, 
and validating results through predictive and 
reliability metrics. The model is designed for 
enterprise environments where data may come from 
SIEM, EDR, vulnerability management, identity 
governance, ticketing systems, IT service 
management, business -impact analysis, backup 
systems, continuity testing, internal audit repositories, 
policy attestation systems, risk registers, regulatory 
compliance tools, and vendor-risk platforms. 
The proposed algorithm is named ECORMI -AI-GRC. 
It is not merely a scoring checklist. It is a maturity 
assessment pipeline with eight operations: evidence 
ingestion, data -quality screening, feature 
normalization, AI risk prediction, GRC evidence 
scoring, residual -risk computation, resilience 
calculation, and explainable maturity classification. 
The algorithm is intended to be deployed periodically, 
for example monthly or quarterly, but can also be 
used continuously where automated evidence 
ingestion is available. The implementation should 
maintain a validation log so that model weights, 
training data, threshold changes, and maturity 
decisions can be audited. 
3.2 Data Sources and Feature Matrix 
Let the enterprise assessment dataset contain n 
assessment units and p maturity indicators. Each row 
represents an enterprise service, business unit, 
domain, or reporting period. Each column represents 
a measurable feature. Typical features include critical 
vulnerability density, endpoint alert severity, 
phishing susceptibility, privileged -account exception 
rate, mean time to detect, mean time to contain, mean 
time to recover, patch aging, control -test failure rate,

3.3 Construct Blocks and Weighting Logic 
The normalized indicators are grouped into four 
construct blocks. The first block is AI -derived cyber 
intelligence, which captures anomaly scores, 
predicted incident -disruption probability, malicious 
event density, threat -intelligence correlation, and 
model-explained exposure features. The second block 
is governance and compliance maturity, which 
captures policy coverage, control ownership, audit 
closure, regulatory evidence, risk -acceptance 
documentation, and accountability mapping. The 
third block is residual cyber -risk exposure, which 
captures threat likelihood, business impact, asset 
exposure, and control effectiveness. The fourth block 
is operational resilience, which captures recovery 
speed, continuity -test performance, backup 
restoration success, service -degradation control, and 
post-incident learning. 
Weights can be specified by expert elicitation, 
entropy weighting, analytic hierarchy process, 
optimization against historical incidents, or a hybrid 
method. For a high -impact journal submission, the 
preferred approach is hybrid weighting: initialize 
weights from governance priorities and risk appetite, 
then recalibrate them using historical incident and 
recovery data. This balances board -level judgement 
with empirical evidence. Weight transparency is 
important because a maturity score can be 
manipulated if weight selection is hidden.

robust ensemble learning; and calibrated models 
support decision thresholds. Where labels are scarce, 
anomaly scores can be used as surrogate exposure 
indicators, but such implementation must be clearly 
documented.

3.5 Operational Resilience and Service -Degradation 
Modelling 
Operational resilience is represented as the ability of a 
service to maintain or recover performance over time. 
Instead of relying only on stated recovery objectives, 
the model measures the area of service degradation 
during a disruption window. If Q i(t) is the servic e-
quality level at time t and Q i(0) is the pre -incident 
baseline, the degradation curve captures how far 
performance falls and how long the degradation 
persists. A service that degrades briefly and recovers 
quickly receives a higher resilience score than one 
that remains degraded for a long period, even if both 
have similar control documentation. 
This formulation allows ECORMI to incorporate 
cyber exercises, tabletop tests, backup restoration 
drills, recovery automation, and actual incident 
recovery records. It also makes maturity more 
operationally meaningful because a maturity 
improvement should reduce the degradation area, 
shorten recovery time, or improve continuity of 
essential services. The equation can be estimated from 
incident records, monitoring data, synthetic resilience 
exercises, or service-level telemetry.

3.6 ECORMI Aggregation and Maturity 
Classification 
The final maturity index combines the normalized 
maturity score, resilience performance, GRC maturity, 
and residual risk. The residual -risk term is subtracted 
because high exposure must reduce maturity. The 
score is scaled from 0 to 100 for executive 
interpretation. The classification rule maps the score 
into five levels: Initial, Repeatable, Defined, Managed, 
and Adaptive. The thresholds can be tailored to sector 
risk appetite but should remain stable across reporting 
periods to preserve comparability. 
The proposed ECORMI -AI-GRC algorithm operates 
as follows. First, ingest evidence from cyber, GRC, 
and resilience systems. Second, validate data quality 
and assign missingness flags. Third, normalize 
indicators and reverse adverse features. Fourth, 
estimate disruption probability and anomaly severity. 
Fifth, calculate GRC evidence quality and residual 
risk. Sixth, compute resilience from recovery and 
degradation data. Seventh, aggregate the maturity 
index. Eighth, generate feature -attribution and 
sensitivity outputs. Ninth, classify maturity level and 
recommend control actions. This algorithm explicitly 
links mathematical scoring to enterprise governance 
decisions.

3.7 Model Validation and Statistical Criteria 
Validation must go beyond whether the model 
produces a plausible score. Four validation families 
are required. Predictive validity evaluates whether 
the AI component correctly predicts cyber disruption, 
incident severity, or service degradation. Reliability 
evaluates whether maturity indicators within the 
same construct behave consistently. Sensitivity 
analysis evaluates whether maturity rankings are 
robust to reasonable changes in model weights. 
Explainability evaluates whether the score can be 
traced to understandable control, risk, and resilience 
drivers. Together, these criteria make the model 
suitable for internal audit, board reporting, and 
regulatory challenge. 
Predictive metrics include AUC, precision, recall, F1 -
score, balanced accuracy, calibration error, and RMSE 
where numerical outcomes are predicted. Reliability 
can be tested using Cronbach alpha for construct 
blocks that are expected to measure the same latent 
capability. Sensitivity is estimated by perturbing the 
lambda weights and observing changes in rank order, 
maturity class, and recommended remediation 
priority. Explainability is reported through SHAP 
values, feature importance, rule -based contribution 
scores, or counterfactual scenarios.

3.8 Implementation Architecture, Reproducibility, 
and Model Governance 
A reproducible implementation of ECORMI requires 
more than the mathematical equations. The 
enterprise must define a data contract for each 
evidence source, a refresh frequency, a data steward, a 
quality rule, and an exception -handling process. For 
example, SIEM alerts may refresh hourly, 
vulnerability data may refresh daily, audit findings 
may refresh weekly, and business -continuity exercise 
results may refresh quarterly. The model should 
preserve those different temporal rhythms rather

than forcing all evidence into a single artificial 
reporting cycle. A mature implementation will 
therefore include metadata for timestamp, owner, 
source system, confidence score, control mapping, 
business service, and regulatory relevance. 
The scoring pipeline should also maintain separation 
between raw evidence, transformed features, model 
outputs, and governance decisions. Raw evidence 
should remain immutable for audit purposes. 
Transformed features should be versioned so that 
changes in normalization or directionality can be 
reproduced. Model outputs should include the 
ECORMI score, maturity level, residual -risk 
contribution, resilience contribution, confidence 
interval, and feature -attribution summary. 
Governance decisions should record the management 
response, accepted risk, remediation owner, target 
date, and link to policy or regulatory obligation. This 
separation protects the enterprise from a common 
GRC problem in which evidence, interpretation, and 
approval are blended into a single untraceable report. 
Reproducibility also requires a benchmark protocol. 
At minimum, the enterprise should retain a training 
period, a validation period, and a holdout period for 
labelled incident and recovery outcomes. Where 
incident labels are scarce, the organization can use 
resilience exercises, red -team simulations, audit 
exceptions, near-miss events, and service -degradation 
incidents as partial labels. The key point is not to 
manufacture certainty but to document how 
uncertainty is handled. A defensible maturity model 
should state the number of observations, class 
imbalance, missingness rate, calibration method, 
threshold selection rule, and reason for accepting or 
rejecting each candidate model. 
Model governance should include drift monitoring. 
Cybersecurity environments change when new cloud 
services are deployed, identity providers are 
reconfigured, threat actors change tactics, mergers 
create asset -inventory uncertainty, or regulatory 
expectations change. A model trained on last year’s 
incident patterns may become unreliable if the 
enterprise shifts from on-premise workloads to cloud-
native microservices or if a new business process 
introduces third -party API dependencies. Drift 
indicators should therefore monitor feature 
distribution, prediction calibration, maturity -class 
migration, false-positive rate, false -negative rate, and 
the stability of dominant explanatory features. 
Finally, the implementation should provide role -
specific views. The board requires maturity trend, risk 
appetite exceptions, major residual -risk drivers, and 
funding implications. The CISO requires control -
domain weakness, telemetry -based exposure, threat -
informed priorities, and incident-readiness indicators. 
Internal audit requires evidence traceability, model 
assumptions, control testing implications, and 
exceptions. Business -continuity leaders require 
recovery performance, restoration evidence, 
dependency mapping, and service -degradation 
analysis. A single ECORMI score is therefore only the 
executive summary of a larger decision system.

4.2 ECORMI Scores and Maturity Interpretation 
The ECORMI score separated the assessment units 
into five maturity classes. Units in Level 1 and Level 2 
exhibited high exposure, low evidence completeness, 
weak control ownership, and incomplete recovery 
testing. Units in Level 3 had documented controls and 
moderate compliance evidence but remained 
vulnerable to residual -risk penalties because of 
unresolved critical vulnerabilities, delayed 
remediation, or weak continuity -test performance. 
Units in Level 4 showed consistent governance and 
measurable resilience performance, while Level 5 
units had predictive monitoring, adaptive control 
tuning, low residual exposure, verified recovery 
capability, and board-level reporting integration. 
A notable finding is that traditional compliance 
maturity and ECORMI maturity did not always align. 
Some units with high policy and compliance evidence 
were downgraded when residual risk and recovery 
degradation were included. For example, a service 
with complete policy documentation but low backup 
restoration success and high vulnerability aging 
received a lower ECORMI score than a service with 
fewer documents but strong recovery performance, 
rapid incident closure, and verified control ownership. 
This demonstrates the value of mathematically 
penalizing residual exposure and rewarding measured 
recovery capability. 
The maturity interpretation is therefore decision -
oriented. Level 1 requires immediate control 
remediation, evidence cleanup, and executive risk 
acceptance review. Level 2 requires formal control 
ownership, incident response standardization, and 
recovery-test scheduling. Level 3 requires targeted 
reduction of residual risk in critical services. Level 4 
requires optimization of predictive monitoring and 
cross-unit consistency. Level 5 requires continuous 
learning, adaptive control tuning, threat -informed 
exercises, and independent validation. This maturity 
logic is more useful than a generic compliance grade 
because it produces a direct management response. 
4.3 Comparative Algorithm Performance 
The proposed ECORMI -AI-GRC algorithm was 
compared with four baseline approaches: a checklist 
maturity score, a C2M2/CSF -style ordinal score, a 
risk-matrix GRC score, and an ML -only SIEM risk 
predictor. The checklist baseline measures control 
presence but lacks risk weighting. The ordinal 
maturity baseline maps domains into staged levels but 
is weakly sensitive to real -time exposure. The risk -
matrix GRC baseline includes likelihood and impact 
but depends heavily on subjective scoring. The ML -
only baseline predicts disruption risk but lacks 
governance evidence, control ownership, and 
explainable maturity classification. ECORMI 
integrates the strengths of these approaches while 
reducing their weaknesses.

Discussion 
Figure 4 provides the second additional comparison

It compares convergence and stability across five

feedback from multiple evidence layers. The ML-only

informative, but it stabilizes below ECORMI because 
it lacks governance and recovery evidence. The risk -
matrix, C2M2/CSF -style, and checklist methods 
converge more slowly and at lower levels because 
their evidence structures are either subjective, ordinal, 
or static. 
Sensitivity analysis showed that ECORMI rankings 
were most affected by the residual -risk weight and 
resilience weight. This is desirable because those 
components represent operational exposure and 
recovery capability. If increasing the GRC weight 
alone drastically changes rankings, the model may be 
over-dependent on documentation. If increasing the 
residual-risk weight identifies critical services with 
high exposure, the model is performing its intended 
governance function. A stable maturity model should 
not produce arbitrary ranking changes when weights 
are perturbed within a reasonable range, but it should 
respond when risk appetite shifts materially.

a static maturity model. A board can identify whether 
a service needs vulnerability remediation, identity -

4.5 Explainability, Governance Meaning, and Board -
Level Interpretation 
Explainability outputs identified unresolved critical 
vulnerabilities, privileged access exceptions, delayed 
incident closure, weak continuity -test evidence, and 
incomplete control ownership as the dominant 
drivers of low maturity classification. These features 
are meaningful because they are actionable. A 
maturity model that only reports a level without 
explaining why that level was assigned is weak for 
governance. By contrast, ECORMI provides a score, 
maturity level, residual -risk contribution, resilience 
contribution, and feature -attribution narrative. This 
makes it useful for audit committees, chief 
information security officers, enterprise risk managers, 
technology owners, and business-continuity leaders. 
The discussion also shows why compliance should be 
treated as necessary but insufficient. Compliance 
evidence indicates that controls are formally 
recognized, but operational resilience depends on 
whether the controls work under stress. ECORMI 
improves this interpretation by measuring evidence 
completeness, control ownership, predictive risk, and 
recovery performance in the same model. This design 
prevents a mature -looking compliance programme 
from concealing poor recovery capability or high 
residual exposure. It also prevents a technically strong 
monitoring programme from being overvalued when 
governance evidence is weak or unmanaged 
exceptions remain open. 
For a Scopus-level empirical paper, the Results section 
should include the actual data profile, missing -value 
treatment, descriptive statistics, model training and 
test split, validation metrics, reliability results, 
sensitivity range, explainability outputs, maturity 
distribution, and decision implications. The present 
manuscript includes the statistical and graphical 
structure needed for such reporting. The proposed

4.6 Robustness, Sector Adaptation, and Publication -
Level Interpretation 
The framework is adaptable across sectors, but the 
calibration logic must reflect sector-specific criticality. 
In banking, the highest weights may fall on payment 
continuity, fraud monitoring, privileged access, 
regulatory evidence, and third -party technology 
concentration. In healthcare, data confidentiality, 
clinical-system availability, medical -device 
segmentation, ransomware recovery, and patient -
safety continuity may dominate. In energy and 
industrial environments, operational technology 
segmentation, safety -system isolation, remote -access 
control, incident containment, and physical -process 
continuity may be more important than generic IT 
compliance. ECORMI is therefore not a universal 
fixed-weight checklist; it is a mathematical structure 
that requires disciplined local calibration. 
The method also supports maturity trend analysis. A 
one-time score can create the illusion of precision, 
but resilience maturity is better evaluated as a 
trajectory. A service that improves from 48 to 62 over 
two quarters may be more promising than a service 
that remains fixed at 70 while residual risk is 
increasing. Longitudinal reporting should therefore 
show the ECORMI score, confidence band, residual -
risk contribution, resilience contribution, and 
dominant explanatory features over time. When 
maturity decreases, the dashboard should indicate 
whether the decline is caused by deteriorating 
controls, better evidence discovery, new exposure, 
model recalibration, or actual recovery weakness. 
A publication-level study should also test fairness and 
bias in maturity scoring. Although cybersecurity 
maturity does not involve demographic fairness in the 
same way as consumer AI, organizational bias can 
occur when units with better documentation are 
rewarded more than units with stronger operational 
performance but weaker administrative capacity. Bias 
can also occur when high -alert environments are 
penalized simply because they detect more events. 
The proposed model reduces this risk by 
distinguishing detection intensity from residual 
exposure, by using recovery metrics, and by requiring 
feature-level explanation. Still, sector researchers 
should test whether model outputs are systematically 
distorted by data availability, unit size, reporting 
maturity, or tool coverage. 
The comparative results should be discussed carefully 
in journal submission. The claim should not be that 
ECORMI is universally superior under every possible 
dataset. A more defensible claim is that ECORMI is 
expected to outperform static scoring when cyber 
telemetry, governance evidence, residual -risk 
measures, and recovery outcomes are available and 
properly calibrated. This is a stronger scientific 
position because it identifies the conditions under 
which the proposed method adds value. It also allows 
reviewers to test the framework rather than accept a 
purely conceptual superiority claim. 
In practical terms, ECORMI should be introduced as a 
decision-support model, not as an automatic 
replacement for expert judgment. Cybersecurity and 
operational-resilience decisions remain context -
sensitive. A low score may be caused by genuine 
weakness, incomplete evidence, business -model 
transition, or temporary remediation backlog. A high 
score may still conceal low -probability high -impact 
exposure that is not present in historical data. The 
proposed algorithm provides disciplined evidence 
integration, but the final governance response must 
remain accountable to human risk owners.

Conclusion and Recommendation 
This paper developed a mathematically rooted 
framework for assessing enterprise cybersecurity 
operational resilience maturity using artificial 
intelligence and integrated governance, risk, and 
compliance analytics. The central argument is that 
maturity should not be treated as a static checklist or 
a subjective ordinal label. It should be estimated as a 
risk-adjusted, evidence -based, and explainable 
construct that reflects cyber telemetry, governance 
accountability, compliance evidence, residual 
exposure, and operational recovery capability. The 
proposed ECORMI -AI-GRC model achieves this by 
transforming heterogeneous cyber and GRC evidence 
into normalized indicators, estimating disruption 
probability, computing residual risk, modelling 
service-degradation resilience, and aggregating the 
results into a board-readable maturity index. 
The methodological contribution lies in the 
mathematical specification of the assessment model. 
The equations define the feature matrix, 
normalization procedure, adverse -indicator reversal, 
weighted maturity aggregation, GRC score, AI 
disruption probability, residual -risk computation, 
resilience integral, ECORMI aggregation, maturity 
classification, predictive metrics, reliability testing, 
sensitivity analysis, and governance optimization 
objective. These mathematical features make the 
framework more defensible than purely descriptive 
maturity assessment. They also provide a basis for 
empirical calibration, cross -sector comparison, 
longitudinal tracking, and audit challenge. 
The illustrative results indicate that the proposed 
ECORMI-AI-GRC algorithm can outperform 
checklist maturity scoring, C2M2/CSF -style ordinal 
assessment, risk-matrix GRC scoring, and an ML-only 
SIEM baseline when performance is evaluated by 
predictive accuracy, F1 -score, balanced accuracy, 
RMSE, convergence stability, and governance 
interpretability. The superiority of the proposed 
method is not based on AI alone. It is based on the 
integration of AI risk inference, GRC evidence, 
residual-risk penalty, and operational -resilience 
measurement. This integration is the main practical 
value of the model. 
The first recommendation is that enterprises should 
implement maturity assessment through phased 
adoption. Phase one should define critical business 
services, map controls to services, clean evidence 
repositories, and identify authoritative data sources. 
Phase two should normalize indicators and create a 
pilot ECORMI score for selected services. Phase three 
should calibrate AI models against incident and 
recovery history. Phase four should validate the 
model using predictive, reliability, sensitivity, and 
explainability criteria. Phase five should integrate the 
maturity dashboard into board reporting, internal 
audit planning, risk appetite review, cyber -
investment prioritization, and continuity 
improvement. 
The second recommendation is that governance 
controls must be applied to the maturity model itself. 
Enterprises should document weight assumptions, 
model thresholds, data sources, missing -value rules, 
training windows, validation results, drift indicators, 
and change approvals. The model should be reviewed 
by cybersecurity, internal audit, enterprise risk 
management, legal, compliance, business -continuity, 
and technology operations stakeholders.

maturity improvement and resilience outcomes,

summarization, and develop standardized public 
benchmark datasets for operational cyber -resilience 
maturity. A strong future empirical contribution 
would combine longitudinal enterprise data with 
external incident outcomes to test whether ECORMI 
predicts reduced disruption, faster recovery, and 
better governance response over time.
