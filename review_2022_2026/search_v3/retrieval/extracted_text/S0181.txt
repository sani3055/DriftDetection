Abstract 
Public WiFi networks deployed across high -density transit environments — such as train stations, bus terminals, 
and airports — present a largely unaddressed frontier in network intrusion detection research. These venues 
collectively serve tens of millions of users monthly yet exhibit extreme traffic heterogeneity, continuous attack 
pattern drift, and strict data privacy constraints that preclude centralized detection paradigms. Existing federated 
learning-based intrusion detection systems (FL -IDS) fail to simultaneously address the spatiotemporal dynamics 
of evolving network topologies, the interpretability requirements of operational security teams, the concept drift 
inherent to live deployments, and the fairness disparities that arise across heterogeneous venue types. We propose 
ST-FedXIDS, a Spatiotemporal Federated Explainable Intrusion Detection System that unifies five technical 
contributions: (1) a heterogeneous dynamic graph construction pipeline that models multi -protocol WiFi traffic 
as sliding temporal -window graphs; (2) ST -GraphSAGE with multi -head temporal self -attention, replacing the 
LSTM/GRU paradigm used in all prior spatiotemporal GNN -IDS works; (3) Drift -Adaptive Federated Averaging 
(DA-FedAvg) that embeds ADWIN-based graph embedding drift detection directly into the federat ed aggregation 
protocol; (4) a GNN -Explainer module that produces subgraph -level attack attribution explanations for 
operational incident response; and (5) a venue-type fairness regularizer that minimizes the maximum inter-venue 
false positive rate gap across heterogeneous deployment sites. Extensive experiments on three public benchmarks 
— CSE-CIC-IDS2018, TON_IoT, and UNSW-NB15 — against four 2025 state-of-the-art baselines demonstrate that 
ST-FedXIDS achieves 98.21% –99.13% accuracy, outperforming all com petitors by 0.9 –2.4 percentage points, 
reduces the inter-venue FPR gap from 8.5–12.3% to 2.1%, and recovers from concept drift in 8 federation rounds 
versus 19–25 rounds for competing methods. An ablation study validates the independent contribution of eac h 
component. 
Keywords: Intrusion Detection System; Federated Learning; Graph Neural Networks; Spatiotemporal Learning; 
Explainable AI; Concept Drift; Public WiFi Security; Fairness -Aware Machine Learning 
 
 
1. Introduction 
The proliferation of public WiFi infrastructure at mass -transit hubs has created a critical and underserved 
challenge at the intersection of network security and distributed machine learning. Transit WiFi deployments — 
encompassing metropolitan rail system s, inter -city bus terminals, and international airports — collectively 
constitute some of the largest shared network environments in existence. A single rail system can sustain 1 –2 
million authenticated sessions per month across dozens of access points, generating traffic volumes and behavioral 
patterns that differ qualitatively from the enterprise and IoT environments that dominate the IDS literature.  
Three structural properties make transit WiFi networks uniquely difficult to protect. First, traffic heterogeneity is 
extreme: a single venue may simultaneously serve commuters streaming video, tourists browsing maps, vendors 
conducting point -of-sale trans actions, and IoT sensors reporting environmental data. The resulting traffic 
distribution differs markedly from one venue type to another, making shared detection thresholds a source of 
systematic fairness failures. Second, attack patterns evolve continuou sly: adversaries adapt their techniques in 
response to defenses deployed at prior venues, inducing concept drift that degrades models trained on historical

data. Third, privacy regulations in most jurisdictions prohibit centralizing raw traffic records, mandating a privacy-
preserving federated architecture. 
Existing approaches address subsets of these requirements but leave critical gaps. Federated GNN -IDS systems 
such as FedGATSage [1], FedSTGCN [2], and CONTINUUM [3] combine graph learning with federated training but 
omit temporal self -attention, have no dr ift adaptation mechanism, and provide no explainability output. 
Explainability-focused works such as xNIDS [4] and E -GNNExplainer [5] operate on centralized data and produce 
feature-level attributions rather than subgraph -level explanations. Concept drift adaptation approaches such as 
ReCDA [6] and INSOMNIA [7] are neither federated nor graph -based. Fairness-aware federated learning methods 
such as FairFed [8] and q -FFL [9] define fairness across demographic client groups rather than across 
heterogeneous venue types and do not address false positive rate equalization. To the best of our knowledge, no 
prior work simultaneously addresses spatiotemporal graph modeling, federated privacy, temporal drift 
adaptation, subgraph-level explainability, and venue-type fairness within a single system. 
We address this gap with ST -FedXIDS (Spatiotemporal Federated Explainable Intrusion Detection System), an 
integrated architecture motivated by real operational requirements of multi -venue public WiFi deployments. The 
system is designed around five contributions: 
• Heterogeneous Spatiotemporal Graph Construction: WiFi traffic is modeled as a dynamic heterogeneous graph 
with typed edges (protocol-level connections) and sliding temporal windows of 60 seconds, enabling the capture 
of both intra-window structural anomalies and cross-window behavioral continuity. 
• ST-GraphSAGE with Temporal Self-Attention: A novel GNN backbone that augments GraphSAGE neighborhood 
aggregation with a multi-head temporal self-attention layer across historical graph snapshots, learning which past 
temporal states are most predictive of current attack patterns. 
• Drift-Adaptive Federated Averaging (DA -FedAvg): An extension of FedAvg that monitors per -client graph 
embedding drift using the ADWIN algorithm and responds by selectively updating drifted model layers at elevated 
learning rates while freezing stable layers, preventing catastrophic forgetting. 
• GNN-Explainer for Operational Attack Attribution: Each classification decision is accompanied by a minimal 
subgraph explanation identifying the network nodes and edges most responsible for the detection, supporting 
actionable incident response without requiring security staff to interpret raw neural network weights.  
• Venue-Type Fairness Regularization: A loss term that minimizes the maximum false positive rate disparity 
across venue types (e.g., bus terminals vs. rail stations), ensuring equitable protection rather than aggregate 
accuracy maximization. 
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews related work across five dimensions. 
Section 3 presents the system model and threat model. Section 4 describes the ST -FedXIDS architecture in detail. 
Section 5 reports experimental results. Section 6 discusses findings and limitations. Section 7 concludes.  
 
2. Related Work 
2.1 Federated Learning for Intrusion Detection 
The application of federated learning to network intrusion detection has accelerated considerably since 2021. 
Lavaur et al. [10] established a reference taxonomy of 22 FL -IDS systems, identifying communication efficiency 
and non-IID data handling as primary challenges. A subsequent ACM Computing Surveys analysis by Campos et al. 
[11] of over 100 works found that the majority employed unrealistic IID data splits, understating the difficulty of 
federated IDS in real deployments. Adewole et al. [12] reviewed 8 8 studies through 2024 and highlighted graph 
neural networks as significantly underrepresented in the FL -IDS literature relative to their demonstrated 
performance advantages in centralized settings. 
Recent work has begun combining GNNs with federated learning for IDS. FedGAT [13] employs fingerprint -
weighted Jaccard similarity for graph attention in a federated setting. FedGATSage [1] integrates client -side GAT 
with server -side GraphSAGE through Louva in community abstraction, reducing communication by 85% while 
improving detection of coordinated DDoS attacks. FedSTGCN [2] is the first system to integrate spatiotemporal 
GCN with federated learning for IoT IDS, employing a GCN + GRU architecture. FeCoGra ph [14] introduces 
federated graph contrastive learning for few -shot IDS, addressing the label scarcity problem. All of these systems 
lack explicit drift adaptation mechanisms, venue-type fairness constraints, and subgraph-level explainability. 
2.2 Graph Neural Networks for Intrusion Detection 
E-GraphSAGE [15] established the foundational paradigm of applying GraphSAGE with edge features to network 
flow graphs, achieving strong performance on IoT datasets. Subsequent works have extended this paradigm in 
multiple directions: DLGNN [16] uses line graph transformations with GCNII and GRU for evolving spatiotemporal 
graphs; Anomal -E [17] applies Deep Graph Infomax for self -supervised GNN -IDS; and TCG -IDS [18] introduces 
temporal contrastive GNN learning as the first self -supervised spatiotemporal GNN -IDS. Comprehensive surveys 
by Bilot et al. [19] and Zhong et al. [20] identify as major open problems the absence of temporal self-attention (all 

Int. J. Adv. Sig. Img. Sci, Vol.12, No.1, 2026

prior works rely on LSTM/GRU/fixed GCN), the lack of integration with federated learning, and the failure to 
provide node/subgraph-level explanations. 
2.3 Explainable AI for IDS 
Explainability research in IDS has primarily focused on feature-level attribution using SHAP and LIME [21]. xNIDS 
[4] produces actionable defense rules via sparse group lasso on feature dependencies. ROULETTE [22] applies 
learnable visual attention on image-converted network flows. E-GNNExplainer [5] is the closest prior work to our 
approach, adapting GNNExplainer to edge-level intrusion classification, but operates on centralized data and does 
not address federated deployment. Shyaa et al. [23] confirm in a 2025 review that GNN-based explanation methods 
remain underexplored, and no system combines subgraph explanation with federated training for IDS.  
2.4 Concept Drift in Network Intrusion Detection 
Concept drift is a well -documented challenge in deployed IDS systems: attack behaviors evolve, benign traffic 
patterns shift seasonally, and new device types join networks. ADWIN [24] is the dominant streaming drift 
detector, demonstrating robust performan ce in IDS contexts (0.99 accuracy in [25]). ReCDA [26] is the current 
state-of-the-art for drift adaptation, using self-supervised representation enhancement. INSOMNIA [27] combines 
incremental semi-supervised learning with XAI for drift interpretation. However, no existing work integrates drift 
detection with the federated aggregation protocol itself — specifically, no system adjusts per-layer learning rates 
in response to detected embedding drift across federated clients. 
2.5 Fairness in Federated Learning 
Federated fairness research has predominantly addressed demographic group fairness and client -level 
performance equity. Agnostic Federated Learning [28] proposes minimax optimization for worst -case client 
performance. q -FFL [9] applies alpha -fairness-inspired loss reweighting. FairFed [8] introduces server -side 
fairness-aware aggregation for group -level fairness. None of these frameworks define fairness in terms of 
heterogeneous deployment site types, and none target false positive rate equalization as the primary fairness 
objective — a critical requirement in transit WiFi environments where disproportionate false alarms at one venue 
type impose operational costs on security staff and erode user trust.  
 
 
3. System Model and Threat Model 
3.1 Deployment Architecture 
We consider a multi-venue public WiFi deployment comprising V venues indexed v = 1, ..., V, each operating as an 
independent federated client. Each venue maintains a local network of Access Points (APs), user devices, and IoT 
sensors. A central federated coordination server aggregates model u pdates from all venue clients without 
accessing raw traffic data. The deployment matches the architecture of large-scale transit WiFi systems serving 1–
2 million monthly users across 50+ venues. 
Each client v observes a continuous stream of network flows captured by its local WiFi controller. Flows are 
featurized using standard CICFlowMeter features (packet length statistics, inter -arrival times, flag counts, flow 
duration, byte rates) augmented w ith session-level entropy and AP association metadata. The client constructs a 
dynamic heterogeneous graph from these flows within sliding temporal windows of duration T = 60 seconds, with 
W = 10 historical windows retained for temporal reasoning.  
3.2 Threat Model 
ST-FedXIDS targets the detection of seven major attack categories documented in the experimental datasets: (1) 
Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) via volumetric flooding; (2) Denial of Service (DoS) via targeted service 
exhaustion; (3) Network scanning a nd reconnaissance; (4) Botnet command -and-control communication; (5) 
Brute force credential attacks (SSH, FTP, HTTP); (6) Web application attacks (SQL injection, XSS); and (7) 
Advanced infiltration and exfiltration attempts. The system assumes honest-but-curious federated clients and does 
not address Byzantine-resilient aggregation, which is treated as complementary future work.  
We assume attackers may employ concept drift as a deliberate evasion strategy — gradually morphing attack 
traffic to resemble benign patterns — and model this through injected drift experiments in Section 5. We further 
assume attack prevalence and behavior al profiles may differ significantly across venue types, motivating the 
fairness regularization component.

component in detail below.

4.1 Heterogeneous Spatiotemporal Graph Construction  
For venue client v at time step t, we construct a heterogeneous graph G_t = (V_t, E_t, X_t, R) from the current 60 -
second window of observed flows. Nodes in V_t represent active network entities: user devices (classified as 
mobile, laptop, IoT sensor), Acc ess Points, and gateway routers. Edge types in R capture protocol -specific 
interaction patterns: {TCP_flow, UDP_flow, DNS_query, HTTP_request, HTTPS_session, ICMP_packet}. This typed 
edge structure enables the model to learn that ICMP floods, DNS amplifica tion, and TCP SYN floods have 
qualitatively different graph signatures. 
Node feature vectors x_i in X_t (dimensionality d = 79) concatenate: per -device 60 -second aggregate statistics 
(mean/std of packet lengths, inter -arrival times, byte rates), connection state entropy, AP -level load metrics, and 
protocol distribution histogr ams. Edge features e_{ij} encode the flow -level CICFlowMeter features for the 
connection between devices i and j. Cross -window edge persistence is tracked via an edge age decay function: 
edges from window t -k receive multiplicative weight exp( -lambda * k) to model behavioral continuity across 
recent temporal windows. 
A sequence of W = 10 graph snapshots {G_{t -W+1}, ..., G_t} is maintained in a sliding window buffer at each client, 
providing the temporal context for the self-attention layer described in the next section. 
 
4.2 ST-GraphSAGE with Temporal Self-Attention 
The core detection model at each client is ST -GraphSAGE, which processes the temporal graph sequence in two 
stages: spatial aggregation and temporal self-attention. 
4.2.1 Spatial GraphSAGE Aggregation 
For each graph snapshot G_t, we apply L = 3 layers of GraphSAGE neighborhood aggregation. At layer l, the 
embedding of node i is updated as: 
h_i^(l) = sigma( W^(l) · CONCAT(h_i^(l-1), AGG({h_j^(l-1) : j ∈ N(i)})) ) 
where AGG is a mean aggregator, sigma is LeakyReLU activation, and W^(l) are learned weight matrices. Hidden 
dimensionality is set to 256 throughout. To handle the heterogeneous edge types in R, we employ edge -type-
specific weight matrices following the R -GCN formulation, with edge feature concatenation into the aggregation 
step. 
4.2.2 Multi-Head Temporal Self-Attention 
After obtaining graph-level summary embeddings {z_{t -W+1}, ..., z_t} via mean pooling of node embeddings from 
each snapshot, we apply a 4-head temporal self-attention mechanism: 
Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax(QK^T / sqrt(d_k)) · V 
where Q, K, V are linear projections of the temporal embedding sequence {z_{t -W+1}, ..., z_t}. Crucially, we 
incorporate a learnable anomaly -score bias: historical snapshots with higher anomaly scores receive elevated 
attention weights, allowing the model to focus on suspicious behavioral episodes in the recent past when 
classifying the current window. The final representation z_t^* is a weighted combination of all historical snapshots 
and is passed to a softmax classification head for multi-class attack detection. 
This design is architecturally distinct from all prior spatiotemporal GNN-IDS works: DLGNN uses GRU recurrence, 
FedSTGCN uses a standard STGCN with GRU, and CONTINUUM stacks GraphSAGE with LSTM. None employs 
temporal self-attention, which we demonstrate pr ovides superior drift resilience because self -attention weights 
can rapidly down-weight stale historical contexts when attack patterns change.  
 
4.3 Drift-Adaptive Federated Averaging (DA-FedAvg) 
Standard FedAvg aggregates client model updates as a weighted average of local model parameters, with weights 
proportional to local dataset size. This aggregation strategy is insensitive to concept drift: clients experiencing drift 
in their local data distribution still contribute equally to the global model, potentially degrading aggregate 
performance. DA-FedAvg addresses this through three mechanisms. 
4.3.1 Per-Client Graph Embedding Drift Detection 
At each federation round r, client v computes its graph -level embedding distribution D_v^r = {z_i : i in batch} and 
transmits the distribution mean mu_v^r and variance sigma_v^r to the server alongside model parameters. The 
server applies the ADWIN (ADaptive WINdowing) algorithm to the L2-distance sequence ||mu_v^r - mu_v^{r-1}|| 
across rounds to detect when client v has experienced a statistically significant shift in its embedding distribution. 
ADWIN maintains an adaptive -length window over a data stream and raises a drift alert when the difference in 
means across sub-windows exceeds a statistical threshold. 
4.3.2 Selective Layer Isolation 
When a drift alert is raised for client v, DA -FedAvg applies selective parameter isolation: the learning rate for the 
final 2 classification layers of client v is elevated by factor alpha = 5 for the subsequent 3 rounds, while the early 
feature extraction layers are frozen at the global model parameters. This design is motivated by the observation

877 
that concept drift in attack traffic primarily manifests as changes in high -level feature combinations (captured by 
deep layers) rather than low -level structural properties (captured by early GNN layers), which are more stable 
across time. 
4.3.3 Drift-Weighted Aggregation 
The server aggregates client model parameters using weights that discount high -drift clients: 
w_v = n_v / N · (1 - lambda_drift · drift_score_v) 
where n_v is the local dataset size, N is the total size, drift_score_v in [0,1] is the ADWIN -normalized drift 
magnitude, and lambda_drift = 0.3 is a regularization coefficient. This prevents heavily drifted clients from 
corrupting the global model while their local models adapt. 
 
4.4 GNN-Explainer for Operational Attack Attribution 
For each detected intrusion, ST-FedXIDS generates a subgraph-level explanation using an adapted GNN-Explainer. 
The explainer identifies the minimal connected subgraph G_S = (V_S, E_S) that maximally influences the 
classification decision for the flagged flow. Formally, the explanation optimizes: 
G_S* = argmin_{G_S ⊆ G} H(Y | G_S) + beta · |E_S| 
where H(Y | G_S) is the conditional entropy of the predicted label given the subgraph and the second term penalizes 
explanation size for compactness. We extend the standard GNNExplainer formulation to heterogeneous graphs by 
applying edge -type-specific mas ks and adapting the mutual information objective to preserve type -conditional 
explanations. 
The explanation output for each alert is presented in human-readable form to security operators: "Detection: DDoS 
— Origin subnetwork: AP -Sector-7 → Device cluster [192.168.x.x/24], abnormal 3 -hop broadcast pattern, 127s 
duration, 840 Mbps peak, matching h istorical DDoS behavioral template." This design addresses a well -
documented gap in operational IDS deployments where security teams cannot act on black -box alerts without 
understanding which network entities are involved. 
 
4.5 Venue-Type Fairness Regularization 
In multi-venue federated IDS deployment, optimizing aggregate detection accuracy can produce systematically 
different false positive rates across venue types. A model calibrated on the high -bandwidth, session-dense traffic 
of a rail station may flag normal IoT sensor communications at a smaller bus depot as suspicious, creating disparate 
alarm burdens across venue operators. 
We define venue-type fairness as the constraint that the maximum inter-venue false positive rate gap DELTA_FPR 
= max_{v,v'}|FPR_v - FPR_{v'}| should not exceed a threshold epsilon (set to 5% in our experiments). To enforce 
this during training, we add a fairness regularization term to the local classification loss at each client:  
L_total = L_cls + gamma · max_{v' in N_v} |FPR_v - FPR_{v'}| 
where gamma = 0.1 is the fairness weight and N_v is the set of venue types sharing the current global model. The 
server communicates per -venue FPR estimates from the previous round to all clients at each aggregation step, 
enabling this penalty without shar ing raw traffic data. This formulation is inspired by FairFed [8] but differs 
fundamentally in its operationalization: FairFed targets demographic group equity, whereas our constraint targets 
operational FPR equity across deployment sites — a distinction with significant practical consequences for multi-
venue WiFi operators.

report the number of federation rounds required to recover 95% of pre-drift accuracy after injected concept drift. 
For explainability, we report GNN-Explainer fidelity score (accuracy of explanation subgraph relative to full model 
decision) and sparsity (fraction of edges retained).

6. Discussion 
6.1 Why Temporal Self-Attention Outperforms GRU/LSTM 
The ablation study confirms that replacing the standard GRU temporal encoder with multi -head self -attention 
contributes the largest individual accuracy gain (+1.72 points). We attribute this to three properties of self -
attention that are particularly advan tageous in the transit WiFi context. First, self -attention directly models 
dependencies between any two temporal windows, regardless of their position in the sequence, whereas 
GRU/LSTM progressively compress older information. For multi-stage attacks that begin with reconnaissance and 
escalate to exploitation over 5–7 temporal windows, this long-range dependency modeling is critical. Second, the 
anomaly-score bias in the attention weights enables rapid re-focusing on suspicious historical episodes when new 
evidence arrives, enabling faster detection of re -emerging attack patterns. Third, self -attention enables faster 
adaptation after concept drift because attention weights can rapidly re -weight the historical context distribution, 
whereas GRU hidden state de cay is controlled by fixed learned gate parameters that change only through 
backpropagation. 
6.2 Operational Deployment Considerations 
The venue -type fairness results have direct operational implications. A 12.34% FPR gap (FedGATSage) across 
venue types means that security staff at higher -FPR venues process approximately 2× as many false alarms per 
day as their counterparts at lower -FPR v enues, creating unsustainable workload disparities in multi -venue 
operations centers. ST-FedXIDS reduces this gap to 2.14%, bringing all venues within a narrow operational band. 
The GNN-Explainer subgraph attributions further reduce operational burden by p roviding actionable context for 
each alert without requiring security staff to query raw logs or understand GNN internals.  
6.3 Limitations and Future Work 
ST-FedXIDS has several limitations. First, the current implementation does not address Byzantine -resilient 
aggregation: a compromised client could poison the global model through manipulated parameter updates. 
Integrating robust aggregation mechanisms such  as Krum or coordinate-wise median is planned for future work. 
Second, the GNN-Explainer operates post-hoc and requires a forward pass through the explainer module per alert, 
adding latency (approximately 12ms per explanation on the test hardware). For rea l-time high-frequency alert 
environments, a distilled explanation module with reduced latency may be necessary. Third, the venue -type 
fairness regularizer requires per -venue FPR estimates to be communicated to the server each round; in 
deployments where this meta-information is privacy-sensitive, differential privacy mechanisms should be applied 
to these estimates.

7. Conclusion 
We have presented ST -FedXIDS, the first intrusion detection system to simultaneously address spatiotemporal 
graph learning, federated privacy preservation, drift -adaptive aggregation, subgraph -level explainability, and 
venue-type fairness for high -density public WiFi deployments. Experiments on CSE -CIC-IDS2018, TON_IoT, and 
UNSW-NB15 against four 2025 state-of-the-art baselines demonstrate consistent performance advantages: 0.93 –
2.19 percentage points in accuracy over the best competitor on each dataset, 75 % reduction in inter -venue FPR 
gap, and 57% faster drift recovery (8 rounds vs. 19). Ablation studies validate the independent contribution of 
each architectural innovation. 
The system addresses a practically important but academically underserved deployment context: transit WiFi 
environments serving millions of users monthly, where security guarantees must be provided equitably across 
heterogeneous venue types while respectin g strict data privacy constraints. We believe the venue -type fairness 
formulation introduced here — optimizing for FPR equalization across deployment site types rather than 
demographic or client -level equity — opens a new research direction in fairness -aware federated IDS that 
warrants further investigation. 
Future work will address Byzantine robustness in the DA -FedAvg aggregation, distillation of the GNN -Explainer 
module for real -time deployment, and evaluation on proprietary traffic captures from live transit WiFi 
deployments.
