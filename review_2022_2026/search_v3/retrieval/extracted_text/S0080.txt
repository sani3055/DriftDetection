Abstract
The evolving nature of the threat landscape against cloud services is outpacing the 
capabilities of traditional security measures. Current firewall implementations in cloud 
services may provide a foundational layer of security, but they have significant limitations 
regarding their ability to respond to recently identify zero day vulnerabilities and to protect 
sensitive information from potential attacks utilizing quantum computing, as well as 
validating audit logs in multi-tenanted, shared cloud service landscapes. In this research, 
we present an innovative integrated approach to addressing each of these limitations by 
combining AI driven Anomaly detection techniques with post quantum cryptography 
authentication, a Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA), and blockchain based audit logging. Our 
proposed AI enhanced cloud firewall uses a Long Short Term Memory (LSTM) deep learning 
model to analyze and classify traffic patterns across IaaS), Platform-as-a-Service (PaaS), 
and Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) environments and dynamically creates adaptive firewall 
policies with sub-second response times. Experimental testing on simulated cloud traffic 
sets demonstrated that our proposed framework achieved a detection rate of 94.7% and a 
False Positive Rate (FPR) of 2.1%, representing improvements of 24.7% and 12.9%, 
respectively, when compared to traditional rule-based firewalls. Additionally, the 
blockchain anchored audit logging mechanism will create tamper proof audit logs, and the 
post-quantum cryptography layer will prevent attacks using the CRYSTALS-Kyber and 
CRYSTALS-Dilithium algorithms. These test results demonstrate that our proposed 
framework is a scalable, resilient, and security hardened solution for future generations of 
cloud computing landscapes.
Keywords: Threat Detection, Intelligent Firewall, Tenant Isolation, Zero Trust, 
Blockchain Audit, Deep Learning, LSTM, Anomaly Detection.

Cybersecurity has shifted its focus toward securing cloud environments, 
specifically IaaS, as cloud computing has progressed. Because extremely dynamic 
environments characterize IaaS, both rapid scalability and fast deployment are 
necessary for the new types of security needed to defend them. As many of today's 
most critical workloads are migrated to the cloud, there is an increasing need for 
robust security mechanisms, such as cloud firewalls, to secure these workloads. 
Therefore, a cloud-computing environment has been identified as vulnerable to 
serious threats, such as unauthorized access, data breaches, and distributed 
denial-of-service (DDoS) attacks; thus, proper tenant separation, resource 
allocation, and data privacy require strong management of IaaS environments 
while ensuring that performance is maintained. Cloud firewalls have been 
developed over the years to meet the evolving requirements of cloud security using 
features specifically designed to operate in cloud environments [1,2].
Cloud firewalls were originally created for use in dynamic, multi-tenanted cloud 
environments. Unlike traditional firewalls, which typically sit at the edge of a 
perimeter-based network, cloud firewalls exist within virtualized environments 
where they can grow with the cloud and accommodate the flexibility of the cloud. 
They protect virtual machines and containers, isolate tenant data, and block policy 
violators of different workloads. In addition to managing network traffic flow 
between multiple Virtual Networks, these devices monitor both inbound and 
outbound traffic to detect and prevent unauthorized access and malicious activity 
from entering into the organization's systems. In addition to monitoring network 
traffic, cloud firewalls are designed to support cloud compliance by providing the 
ability to log security related events, which enables organizations to meet their 
respective regulatory requirements [3].
Cloud Firewalls that utilize artificial intelligence (AI), enhance and adapt an 
organization's security posture. Cloud Firewalls utilizing AI are able to analyze 
vast amounts of information, identify potential incidents in real-time, and respond 
to new threats as they appear. This capability creates a powerful cloud security 
tool. Organizations will be able to proactively implement security measures prior 
to vulnerabilities being identified; versus reacting to vulnerabilities after they have 
been discovered. In addition, the incorporation of AI into cloud firewalls is 
expected to develop an adaptive security framework that will allow cloud firewalls 
to evolve over time and provide increased resiliency in increasingly complex cloud 
environments.AI-enriched firewalls align well with modern cloud security 
objectives, including real-time monitoring, faster response times, and automated 
threat management [4,5].The growing use of cloud computing services is creating 
an entirely new range of cybersecurity issues that require new solutions quickly. 
A report from IBM on "Cost of a Data Breach" (2023) reports that the average cost 
of a cloud-related data breach was $4.45 million. This represents an increase of 
15% over the last three years. Additionally, Gartner estimates that by 2026, 95% 
or more of all new digital workload deployments will occur on cloud native 
platforms, thus greatly increasing the number of possible attack surfaces for 
adversaries. Infrastructure-as-a-service (IaaS), which utilizes shared resource 
pools and virtualized network stack architectures, creates multi-dimensional 
vulnerabilities, such as hypervisor escape attacks, cross tenant data leakage, and

1.1 Research Gap Statement
Although cloud security has made significant advancements in terms of research, 
many important areas remain untouched by contemporary cloud firewall 
technologies. In particular, (1) current AI-based firewalls have been primarily 
trained using centralized datasets that pose a risk to tenants' data privacy when 
used in multi-tenanted environments, as it is unethical to collect tenant traffic data 
for aggregation purposes; (2) although the potential impact of quantum computing 
is well documented in the cryptography community with respect to post-quantum 
computing threats, no one has proposed or evaluated its inclusion into cloud 
firewall authentication pipelines; (3) even though regulatory compliance requires 
firewalls to maintain auditable and unalterable audit logs, current solutions 
relying on conventional file-based logging do not provide sufficient protection 
against insider tampering; and (4), nearly every AI-based firewall system is 
designed to measure performance independently, and none considers the 
collective security and performance implications of the simultaneous deployment 
of deep learning detection, zero trust authentication, adaptive policy changes, and 
blockchain logging. This study addresses these four gaps using an integrated, 
experimentally validated framework that combines all the aforementioned 
features into a single cloud firewall architecture.
1.2 Research Objectives
The main goals of this study can be formally defined as follows: First, to create 
and deploy a deep learning-based anomaly detection engine using LSTMs 
networks and isolation forests to identify and flag both known and unknown 
malicious activities on cloud traffic with high accuracy and very low false positives. 
Second, to develop and deploy a zero trust architecture (ZTA) layer in a cloud 
firewall infrastructure that dynamically authenticates users or devices requesting 
access to various infrastructure as a service (IaaS), platform as a service (PaaS), 
and software as a service (SaaS) services, while enforcing access policies based

from the advent of quantum computers. Fourth, to develop a blockchain-based 
audit logging tool that utilizes a permissioned distributed ledger to verify the 
integrity, immutability, and authenticity of all firewall-related event logs, including 
changes to the rules governing how firewalls operate. Fifth, to simulate and test 
the full functionality of the integrated systems through a series of controlled

2 Motivation for Cloud Firewalls in Cloud Computing
The speed and impact of cloud computing have dramatically transformed how 
companies approach data storage and management. This transformation, while 
providing great opportunities, has also brought many serious security challenges. 
As cloud systems become increasingly complex and connected, the potential for a 
wide range of cyber threats increases. Therefore, organizations must develop and

implement new, enhanced security processes to ensure the integrity of their data 
and infrastructure. The use of cloud firewalls in protecting cloud environments is 
a critical component to safeguarding them from unauthorized access and 
malicious attacks (i.e., data breaches). Due to the need for responsive real time 
changes to emerging threats, next generation cloud firewalls are needed. Many 
current methods are passive and do not adequately respond to the dynamic 
environment of clouds. To address these limitations, advanced technologies, 
including AI-driven anomaly detection, deep learning and integration with block 
chain, are being developed and integrated into next generation cloud firewalls. 
Advanced technologies provide capabilities that include proactive detection of 
emerging threats, auto-scaling policies and advanced data integrity capabilities 
that can be used to detect and prevent emerging vulnerabilities in a cloud firewall. 
In addition to the technology advances, the growing trend toward adopting a 
"zero-trust" model for cybersecurity has significantly increased the rate at which 
companies require continuously authenticated adaptive security policies, which in 
turn requires significant scalability improvements in cloud firewalls to defend 
against emerging threats across larger areas of cyberspace [6,7].
The drive to develop an enhanced cloud firewall was additionally supported by 
empirical evidence based on threats in today's environment. As reported in the 
Verizon Data Breach Investigations Report (2023), 74% of breaches identified 
involved some type of human factor (e.g., social engineering, misuse, error), all of 
which could be prevented through the use of adaptive, behaviorally aware firewall 
policies. Cloud computing systems, however, provide a prime target for advanced 
persistent threats (APTs). These represent types of prolonged and targeted 
cyberattacks in which an adversary gains unauthorized network access and 
remains undetected over a long period. Signature-based and rule-based firewalls 
used traditionally cannot detect APTs, as they are designed to mimic legitimate 
traffic flow characteristics. In addition, the increasing usage of micro service 
architectures and containerized workloads, created using technologies such as 
Kubernetes and Docker, has significantly increased the number of internal 
network communications being monitored in cloud computing systems; from a few 
dozen in legacy data center networks to thousands in many modern cloud 
computing systems. This large increase in intra-cloud traffic complexity 
necessitates an entirely new approach to designing firewalls, with a foundation in 
machine learning, behavioral analysis, and continuously adapting policies versus 
statically enforced rules.

3.1 AI and Machine Learning in Cloud Firewall Security
Several applications employing Artificial Intelligence (AI), and Machine Learning 
(ML), are being employed in cloud firewalls to enhance their security capabilities. 
This is primarily due to the inability of traditional signature-based detection 
mechanisms to effectively identify polymorphic and zero-day attacks. The first 
study examined the effectiveness of three ML classifier algorithms (Naïve Bayes, 
Support Vector Machines, and Decision Trees) that can be applied to classify 
threat types within a network intrusion dataset. It reported that such classifiers 
achieved threat detection accuracies of approximately 80%–92%. Although these 
early studies showed great potential for utilizing ML for threat detection, they also 
had a significant drawback. They suffered from extremely high false positive rates 
(>10%) during their live deployments. Subsequent studies focused on developing 
deep learning architectures to improve the performance of ML models for threat 
detection. Deep learning architectures include Recurrent Neural Networks 
(RNNs) and their Long Short-Term memory (LSTM) variant. RNN or LSTMs are 
well suited for utilizing sequential traffic analysis. Their architecture can capture 
the temporal dependencies present within sequential data streams, such as those 
generated by network packets. An example of how these architectures can be used 
in practice is the adaptive ML-augmented cloud firewall proposed. This adaptive 
ML-augmented cloud firewall utilizes LSTMs to generate real-time rules and 
adaptive anomaly scores for traffic patterns in real time. The authors reported a 
22% increase in the detection rate over pure rule-based systems. Recent 
developments in the field have involved applying transformer architectures, 
originally developed in the natural language processing (NLP) community, to 
network traffic classification. Researchers have shown that using attention 
mechanisms to classify network traffic allows them to better identify subtle 
anomalies in packet header sequences than convolutional approaches. However, 
one major issue common to all these architectures is the computational overhead 
associated with performing complex model inference. The computational overhead 
associated with performing complex model inference can be a significant source 
of delay in many cloud production environments; therefore, careful management 
of the overhead will be critical in determining whether AI/ML solutions will be 
viable in a cloud environment.
3.2 Zero Trust Architecture in Cloud Environments
Zero Trust Architecture (ZTA) has become a central security paradigm for cloud 
environments and represents a departure from the traditional perimeter based 
security model. In essence, it is based on the idea that all users, devices, and 
network segments should not be inherently trusted, regardless of their physical or 
logical location. The National Institute of Standards and Technology formally 
defined the zero trust framework in NIST Special Publication 800-207 with seven 
core tenets, including continuous authentication, least privilege access, micros 
segmentation, and real-time trust evaluation. In cloud computing environments, 
ZTA dictates that every access request made to a resource, regardless of whether 
it comes from an internal service or an external user, must be authenticated, 
authorized, and encrypted, with access decisions being made in real-time using

location, and time of access. The proposed system identified that organizations

combination of ZTA and AI-driven firewalls creates a synergistic security model

3.3 Quantum Cryptography for Cloud Security
The emergence of quantum computing can potentially lead to a viable and 
imminent risk to the cryptographic frameworks used by most cloud security 
architectures today. All three of the most commonly used public-key cryptographic 
schemes within cloud infrastructures (RSA, ECDH, and DSA) are considered 
susceptible to Shor's algorithm and, therefore, at risk of being broken by a 
sufficiently advanced quantum computer. Because the cost and development time 
to build large-scale fault-tolerant quantum computers are still considerable, 
adversaries will likely implement "harvest now, decrypt later" attacks; that is, they 
will obtain and store encrypted cloud data today to then decrypt it when sufficient 
quantum computational resources become available. Therefore, the urgency to 
address post-quantum cryptography security is far from being merely a matter of 
a future issue. The National Institute of Standards and Technology completed the 
process of standardizing post-quantum cryptographic (PQC) algorithms in 2024. 
PQC standardization resulted in two new standards: CRYSTALS-Kyber (FIPS 203) 
for key encapsulation and CRYSTALS-Dilithium (FIPS 204) for digital signatures. 
Both were selected as primary recommendations for use by the U.S. government. 
The additional overhead associated with implementing CRYSTALS-Kyber into 
cloud-based authentication protocols and found that the latency associated with 
generating keys using this protocol was 0.082 ms an insignificant amount of time 
for cloud applications and provided quantum attack resistance similar to that of 
AES-256. A hybrid classical-quantum cryptographic architecture for protecting 
data stored on cloud servers and provided a mechanism for migrating existing TLS 
infrastructure to incorporate post-quantum key encapsulation. Thus, post-
quantum cryptography can be integrated into cloud firewall authentication 
pipelines, both practically and strategically.
3.4 Blockchain for Security Audit and Log Integrity
Blockchain technology was initially designed to facilitate peer-to-peer financial 
transactions in a decentralized manner; however, researchers have thoroughly 
investigated how blockchain technology can provide a secure method for 
documenting the integrity of security logs and audit trails in cloud computing 
environments. One of the inherent properties of blockchain technology is its 
immutable nature; that is, once a transaction is documented and verified in the

blockchain, it cannot be modified without compromising all subsequent blocks in 
the blockchain. Consequently, blockchain technology is inherently suitable as a 
tamper-proof logging substrate. A blockchain-based cloud firewall auditing system 
that utilized a permissioned Hyperledger Fabric blockchain network to document 
each firewall event as a chain code transaction with a cryptographic hash of the 
event payload. The authors' evaluation results demonstrated that the system could 
identify log tampering within 1.2 seconds of the occurrence of the tamper event 
with 99.8% certainty; whereas traditional file integrity monitoring systems utilize 
periodic batch scanning resulting in detection delays of up to 24 hours. The prior 
research by developing a smart contract-based rule validation mechanism for 
firewalls; wherein proposed firewall rule modifications are submitted to the 
blockchain for consensus-based approval before they take effect to prevent rogue 
rule modifications from being made by compromised administrators. A significant 
gap identified in prior research is the performance overhead associated with 
implementing blockchain consensus protocols in high-throughput firewall logging 
environments, where thousands of events may need to be logged per second 
without introducing unacceptable latency. The proposed framework in this study 
addresses the performance gap identified above through the utilization of an off-
chain batching mechanism, whereby events are locally aggregated into batches 
and only the Merkle root hash of each batch is periodically added to the 
blockchain, thereby significantly reducing the number of on-chain transactions 
required and maintaining full tamper detectability.
3.5 Research Gap Analysis
A systematic review of the extant literature reveals four significant gaps in the 
current research that this body of work has not fully addressed. First, while each 
of the individual components (AI-based anomaly detection, zero trust 
authentication, Post Quantum cryptography, and blockchain logging) has been 
individually examined, no previous research has proposed or evaluated an 
integrated framework that uses all of these components in a single cloud firewall 
architecture. These individual components also interact with one another in 
complex manners. For example, the latency of post quantum cryptographic 
handshakes will affect the decision making process of zero trust access, and the 
compute resource utilization of long short-term memory inference will need to be 
balanced with the throughput needs of packet level firewall processing. Second, 
the privacy implications of training AI-based firewall models using data from 
multiple tenants in cloud computing systems have not yet been addressed. Current 
proposals for training AI-based firewall models are based on the assumption that 
there will be centralized access to training data from all tenants. Under GDPR and 
similar data protection laws, this is illegal and unethical. Third, the resilience of 
AI-based cloud firewalls to Adversarial Machine Learning (AML) attacks, in which 
sophisticated attackers create network traffic specifically designed to evade 
machine-learning classifiers, has not been given sufficient consideration in the 
cloud specific literature, even though it has been thoroughly researched in the 
broader community of AML researchers. Fourth, the vast majority of existing 
experimental evaluations utilize dated benchmark datasets that do not represent 
the traffic patterns of today’s containerized and micro service-based cloud

4 Background Details
A cloud firewall is a virtualized, software defined firewall placed in a cloud-
computing environment or at its edge and uses dynamic rules to filter and monitor 
all inbound and outbound network traffic. An anomaly is an abnormality that 
represents a statistically significant difference in the normal behavior of network 
traffic. This could include unusual packet arrival time intervals, irregular port 
access times, excessive spikes in data volume, or protocol usage errors. A Security 
Rule is a conditional statement defining a rule of the type: If (condition C exists in 
the network traffic) then (action A should take place, with priority P). Where C is 
a combination of characteristics of the network traffic and A can be one of the

4.1 Threat Model
The threat model used in the research assumes that there are many tenants in a 
cloud environment hosted on IaaS infrastructure using a variety of workloads, 
including web applications, databases, and micro services. The model used to 
describe an adversary is an outside attacker capable of performing network level 
attacks, such as port scanning, DDoS flooding, man-in-the-middle attacks, and SQL 
injection via application layer payloads. An adversary may also be an inside threat 
a cloud administrator or a compromised service account capable of modifying 
firewall rules, accessing audit logs, or stealing tenant data by making use of 
internal traffic channels that appear normal and legitimate. Additionally, we 
assumed that the adversary has limited computational power and does not have 
Quantum computing capabilities at the point of deployment; however, the 
adversary could collect encrypted data to decrypt later using quantum decryption, 
which is why the post-quantum cryptographic layer was added. We further 
assumed that the adversary cannot gain access to the private keys of honest 
tenants, cannot compromise the consensus mechanism of the blockchain-based 
audit ledger, and cannot directly access the parameters of the trained machine-
learning model. This threat model provides the basis for the following security 
guarantees provided by the proposed framework: (1) detection of external network 
anomalies with high precision and low false positive rate, (2) prevention of 
unauthorized lateral movement between tenants, (3) cryptographic assurance of 
long-term confidentiality of tenant data against future quantum attacks, and (4) 
tamper-evident verification of all events of firewall audits.

4.2 Deep Learning for Traffic Analysis
The deep-learning aspect of the cloud-firewall concept is rooted in the 
mathematically representing sequential anomaly detection. Each network-traffic 
sequence can be considered a series of feature vectors X = {x1, x2, ..., xt} where 
each feature vector xi includes the attributes of a given "traffic-window" (e.g., 
number of packets, total bytes transferred, flow-duration, source or destination 
entropy, etc. protocol-distribution, and access-frequency of port). The LSTMs 
process these sequences by passing them through a series of "gated-memory-
cells," which selectively hold onto and discard prior history; thus allowing the 
model to recognize long-term dependencies in traffic patterns, which other models 
would miss, for example, a slow rate port scan that develops over minutes instead 
of seconds. During inference time, the LSTMs output an anomaly-probability score 
s(xt) for each "traffic-window," which is then compared to a dynamically adjusted 
threshold theta, generated by the recent distribution of scores; when s(xt) > theta, 
the anomaly-detection engine triggers the rule generation module to create a 
specific mitigation rule. The threshold theta is updated using an exponential 
moving average of the most recent scores; thus, it can adapt to changes in the 
baseline traffic characteristics, such as traffic-volume increases during business 
hours without manual recertification. The adaptive thresholding mechanism is 
important for reducing false positives due to natural variability in traffic patterns 
within different environments, whereas fixed-threshold anomaly-detection 
systems often do not have the ability to handle this type of variance.

6.1 Multi-Layer Adaptive Firewall (MLAF) Model
The proposed Multi-Layer Adaptive Firewall (MLAF) model creates a three-layered 
architecture for the cloud-based firewall, in which each layer assesses a different 
aspect of network activity individually and contributes a portion of its assessment 
to a combined decision-making function. The first layer, the network layer, 
assesses OSI layers 3 through 4 and assesses packet level attributes, such as 
source and destination IP addresses, port numbers, protocols, packet size, and 
time of arrival between packets. The network layer utilizes a random forest 
classifier trained on network flow data to generate a Network Threat Score (NTS) 
in the range of [0, 33]. The second layer, the application layer, assesses OSI layer 
7 and applies deep packet inspection (DPI) to analyze HTTP/HTTPS, DNS, and 
application-specific protocol payloads. The application layer utilizes a 
convolutional neural network trained on the byte sequence of the payload to 
identify application-layer attacks (such as SQL injection, cross-site scripting, 
command injection, and malicious API calls), generating an Application Threat 
Score (ATS) in the range of [0, 33]. The third layer, the behavioral layer, maintains 
a continually updated behavioral profile of each authenticated identity (user, 
service account, or automated process) and generates a Behavioral Threat Score 
(BTS) in the range of [0, 34], by comparing the current session behavior of that 
identity against their established behavioral profile utilizing a long short term 
memory (LSTM) model. The Total Threat Score (TTS) is calculated as follows:
TTS = (w1 * NTS) + (w2 * ATS) + (w3 * BTS)
where w1, w2, and w3 are the weights assigned to each layer, which are 
dynamically adjusted based on the current threat intelligence context. The TTS 
then drives a four-tiered response mechanism: Scores in the range [0, 25) will 
result in a MONITOR status and log the traffic; scores in the range [25, 50) will 
result in a THROTTLE status and apply a rate limit to the traffic source; scores in 
the range [50, 75) will result in a BLOCK status and drop all traffic coming from 
the traffic source until reviewed manually; and scores in the range [75, 100] will 
result in a QUARANTINE status and isolate the virtual machine or container 
associated with that traffic source, and notify the Security Operations Center.

One of the main privacy concerns in multi-tenant cloud firewall deployments is 
that training a reliable shared anomaly detection model using traffic data from 
multiple tenants violates regulatory guidelines for the protection of sensitive data, 
such as those of GDPR and HIPAA, as it prohibits the sharing of network traffic 
between organizations. However, the authors propose a Federated Learning (FL) 
framework that enables the collaborative improvement of a shared anomaly 
detection model for cloud firewall deployment without collecting or storing any 
raw traffic data centrally. The FL framework is designed such that each tenant 
runs a local long short-term memory (LSTM)-based anomaly detection model on 
its own traffic data that is maintained locally in the tenant's private cloud 
environment. At a defined time interval (the authors provide an example default 
interval of every hour ), each tenant's local model computes the gradient updates 
based on the most recent traffic observations and transmits those gradient vectors 
to a federated aggregator, which does not receive any raw traffic data. The 
federated aggregator uses the federated averaging algorithm to produce a single 
global model that is a combination of the gradients from all tenant models that 
participated in the previous learning cycle. That global model is then sent back to 
all tenants, who use it as their new shared model. To protect against gradient 
inversion attacks that could leak the content of the gradient vectors, the authors 
apply Local Differential Privacy (LDP) at the source (i.e., at each tenant) by adding 
calibrated Gaussian noise to each gradient vector before transmitting the gradient 
vectors to the federated aggregator; the amount of noise added to each gradient 
vector is determined by a privacy budget parameter called epsilon. Using LDP 
provides a formal mathematical guarantee that the model developed using the 
proposed FL framework satisfies ε-differential privacy, thereby bounding the 
amount of information leaked about each tenant's traffic patterns per federated 
learning round. Overall, the FL framework allows the cloud firewall's anomaly 
detection capabilities to take advantage of the collective intelligence of all tenants 
while protecting the privacy of each tenant's individual traffic pattern 
characteristics, thus closing one of the major research gaps identified in the 
literature review.

Byzantine attacks can be used to attack federated learning systems, where the

geometric median is immune to up to 50% Byzantine participants, as opposed to

tenant is clipped when ||∇ᵢ|| > 2·median({||∇ⱼ|| : j ∈ all tenants}), which ensures 
that the injected gradients are not unbounded. Third, we propose the idea of 
reputation-based weighting, with each tenant i having a reputation score Ri [0,1],

6.3 Post-Quantum Zero Trust Authentication Layer
The proposal includes a Post-Quantum Zero Trust Authentication (PQ-ZTA) layer 
using quantum-resistant cryptographic methods to perform all identity verification 
and access authorization activities within the cloud firewall pipeline. Once a user, 
service, or device attempts to connect to a cloud resource, the PQ-ZTA layer first 
challenges the connecting entity with a CRYSTALS-Kyber key encapsulation 
challenge to establish a session key that is secure against both classical and 
quantum-based attackers. Once the session key has been established, the identity 
of the connecting entity is verified through a CRYSTALS-Dilithium digital 
signature, rather than the RSA/ECDSA signatures that have traditionally been 
used to verify identities within cloud-based authentication systems. In addition to 
initial authentication, the PQ-ZTA layer implements a Continuous Adaptive 
Authentication (CAA) method that continuously evaluates the trust level of each 
ongoing active session at fixed time intervals (default = 90 s). Each active session 
is evaluated based on two factors. First, original cryptographic identity 
verification is performed, and second, the current risk level of each session is 
determined using a real-time behavioral risk signal from the behavioral layer of 
the model. When an authenticated user’s risk score is higher than the defined 
threshold that has been set by the CAA, the CAA will send a second (step-up) 
authentication request to the connected party requesting re-authentication before 
providing access to company resources. The CAA will terminate all connections 
where the connected party does not successfully authenticate after being 
prompted for additional authentication or if the connected party chooses to ignore 
the prompt for a second level of authentication. All terminated sessions will be 
sent to the Security Operations Center for further review. The combination of 
quantum-resistant cryptographic authentication methods and real-time behavioral 
evaluations provides a new and enhanced method for authenticating users within 
cloud environments, providing protection against both long-term quantum threats 
and real-time session hijacking attacks, thus representing a significant 
advancement beyond the authentication methods discussed in previous cloud 
firewall literature.

request to access is verified and undergoes multiple levels of threat identification, 
real-time monitoring, adaptive response, tamper-free logging and joint 
intelligence and can be handled through a coherent architecture of seven phases 
to safeguard the sensitive data of the person requesting access.
6.5.2 Component Dependencies and Data Flow
The components of the framework have well thought-out dependencies and data 
flows to be correct and performant. The PQ-ZTA layer is used to issue the identity 
assertions (user_id, device_id, timestamp, signature) cryptographically verified, 
that are fed into the MLAF trust score computation and blockchain audit log. 
MLAF system takes three streams of data: (1) Raw network packets of the traffic 
capture interface, (2) authenticated identity metadata of PQ-ZTA and (3) threat 
intelligence feeds of third parties (IP reputation databases, CVE announcements). 
LSTM anomaly detector and the three-layer classifier 
(network/application/behavioral) are run simultaneously on the same series of 
packets, but at different granularity: the LSTM runs time-windowed sequences to 
learn temporal patterns, and the three-layer classifier runs each packet/sessions 
to learn instantaneous features. The policy engine consumes the outputs of such 
detection modules (anomaly scores, threat scores) and has a rule database, or 
inserts new rules into the database in case thresholds are exceeded. The 
blockchain logging system acts as an observer, which is notified of events by all 
other components (authentication events by PQ-ZTA, detection events by MLAF, 
policy events by rule engine) but it does not affect the execution of those events, 
making audit logging a single point of failure. The federated learning coordinator 
runs on a distinct clock and receives gradient updates on local models, averaging 
them with Byzantine-robust averaging, and transmitting updated global models, 
but does not rely on the real time traffic processing pipeline to introduce an extra 
delay. The data persistence is at three levels, which are (1) short-term memory (10

7 Cloud Firewalls in AI systems overview
Figure 1 illustrates the mechanism by which a cloud firewall protects both 
applications and resources within a cloud environment from cyber threats. All 
users access all cloud services (e.g., databases, CRM, and email) via a private 
network. The cloud firewall acts as a security barrier to screen traffic entering and 
leaving the private network and to thwart any malicious attempts. Additionally, it 
protects against attacks (e.g., DDoS, hacking, and malware) in the form of 
incoming/outgoing traffic inspection to provide a secure and reliable operation for 
cloud environments and enable organizations to operate in the digital world [17, 
18]. This represents a significant advancement in cloud firewalls and was 
developed to combat the increasing number of emerging threats targeting AI 
models and applications. Unlike traditional firewalls, which examine incoming and 
outgoing network traffic based on established criteria (rules), AI-based firewalls 
use sophisticated algorithms or machine learning models to analyze a much larger 
amount of network activity in order to detect and identify potential security risks 
and threats. These firewalls are able to process vast amounts of data in real-time 
to detect and eliminate various types of cyber threats such as ransomware, 
malware and more complex, persistent threats that may be difficult for more basic 
security tools to identify. One of the greatest benefits of using an AI firewall is its 
ability to learn how to defend itself and adapt to changing conditions over time. 
The AI firewall analyzes the way a network behaves and develops patterns, then 
uses this analysis to develop its own proactive defense strategies and tactics. 
These AI firewalls have the capability to both identify known threats, as well as 
unknown and/or evolving threats, through the identification of abnormal traffic 
patterns. In addition to their ability to identify normal and abnormal traffic 
patterns, AI firewalls are also capable of conducting deep packet inspection (DPI), 
which allows them to look at the actual content of data packets that pass through 
the firewall, rather than just examining the header field(s) of the packet. As a 
result, AI firewalls are able to provide better protection against advanced attacks, 
including zero-day exploits. With the constantly evolving nature of cyber threats 
in today's world, many traditional security methods are no longer effective.
Another significant advantage of implementing AI within cloud firewalls is the 
ability to automate the responses to potential threats. Cloud firewalls utilize real-
time threat intelligence to dynamically update security policies and rules based on 
the latest available threat data. By automating responses to potential threats, 
organizations are able to respond to a threat in a timely manner before a 
significant incident occurs. Furthermore, AI firewalls also include additional

8 Implementation of Cloud Firewalls in Cloud Computing
The structured implementation of cloud firewalls is demonstrated by a systematic 
framework as shown in Figure 2, that outlines six distinct steps to provide an 
efficient and comprehensive methodology to protect network-based 
infrastructures on the cloud. The six phases build on each other to develop an 
adaptive security solution that can sustain itself against threats [19, 20]. A key 
component of Phase 1 is to completely identify your organizations network needs 
(i.e. what type of information will be transmitted across the network, which 
applications you have running, etc.) to ensure that all future security initiatives 
are tailored to meet those specific identified risk areas. Following the completion 
of the network requirements, step 2 addresses the definition of formal rules for 
accessing firewalls, where policies for filtering traffic and granularly controlling 
access to the network are defined. These policies determine which 
communications are allowed or denied according to the organization’s predefined 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

security requirements. To move forward with defining the policies for the firewall, 
step 3 focuses on isolating tenants, which is especially important in multi-tenant 
cloud environments. Role-based Access Control (RBAC) enforces strict 
segregation of data between tenants to prevent unauthorized access from other 
tenants and preserve the confidentiality of the data by segregating it throughout 
the shared infrastructure. Step 4 addresses managing the flow of traffic, in which 
all incoming and outgoing traffic is routed through the firewall to enable real-time 
continuous monitoring of traffic. This enables the rapid identification and 
containment of unusual or unauthorized traffic patterns to protect the larger 
infrastructure from being compromised by malicious activity. Step 5 introduces AI 
driven automation as a transformative addition to firewall intelligence, in which 
machine learning is used to automatically update rules based on a real-time feed 
of threat intelligence, enhance anomaly detection, and decrease false positives 
using predictive analytics. The automated AI driven firewall increases the 
response time, accuracy, and ability to adapt to the evolving nature of cyber 
threats. The final step, step 6, prescribes a service model specific configuration, 
as the three service models of IaaS, PaaS, and SaaS have different security needs 
and require different approaches to securing the environment. For example, IaaS 
requires the hardening and securing of VMs and network configurations, PaaS 
requires the directing of security controls to the application platform layer and 
enforcing checkpoints, and SaaS requires the securing of user access credentials 
and the maintaining of robust data privacy through tight access management and 
monitoring. Together, these six steps form a unified and scalable cloud firewall 
implementation strategy that allows organizations to provide meaningful 
improvements to their cybersecurity posture across various and ever changing 
cloud computing environments [21].

9.1 Experimental Setup
We admit that, although the CICIDS-2017/2018 datasets are adopted by the 
largest number of studies in the intrusion detection field, they do not reflect the 
traffic patterns of the modern cloud-native infrastructure with micro services, 
containerized workloads (Docker/Kubernetes) and service mesh architecture 
(Istio, Linkerd) fully. To overcome this weakness, we supplemented the benchmark 
datasets with artificial-generated cloud-native traffic patterns with the 
CloudTrafficGen tool. This augmentation comprises: (1) gRPC traffic patterns with 
protobuf-encoded payloads of the type found in microservice communication (15% 
of total traffic volume), (2) Kubernetes control plane traffic, such as API server 
requests, etcd synchronization, and pod lifecycle events (8% of traffic), (3) service 
mesh sidecar proxy traffic with its characteristic load balancing and circuit-
breaker patterns (12% of traffic), The augmented dataset will contain 22 million 
records (compared to 14 million in the original CICIDS datasets) and 12 cloud-
native attack situations: pod escape exploits, API server privilege escalation, 
service mesh policy bypass, and exploitation of container image vulnerabilities. 
We checked the reasonableness of synthetic traffic by comparing it with our pilot 
deployment packet captures (Section 9.5), which showed that statistical 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

properties (packet size distribution, inter-arrival times, protocol mix) of synthetic 
cloud-native traffic exhibit less than 12% variation with production traffic 
characteristics. Although such augmentation enhances the representativeness of 
datasets, we acknowledge that it has not yet been possible to completely capture 
the complexity and diversity of production cloud environments, and we suggest 
future research to create complete, publicly accessible cloud-native traffic 
datasets with ground-truth labels of both benign operational patterns and realistic 
attack patterns against modern cloud architectures.
Simulations were performed in a simulated cloud-computing environment created 
using CloudSim Plus 7.3.0, which replicated a three-zone IaaS configuration with 
500 virtual machines distributed throughout the three zones. Each zone had 50 
physical hosts with 16 vCPUs, 64GB of RAM, and 10 Gbps networking 
interconnects. The traffic flow through the simulated network was generated by 
combining the CICIDS-2017 and CICIDS-2018 intrusion detection datasets. These 
combined datasets contained more than 80 unique attack types (including, but not 
limited to, DoS/DDoS/botnets/infiltration/web attacks/port scans), along with 
approximately 14 million records representing benign background traffic [22,23]. 
For added realism, the datasets were processed prior to the simulation to include 
time-based variance representative of normal cloud workloads; specifically, the 
volume of traffic was modeled after a bimodal distribution with peak volumes at 
09:00 and 14:00 simulated local time, and all attacks were introduced randomly 
into the benign traffic with varied durations of the attack event ranging from 30 s 
to 4 h. The LSTMs were implemented in Python 3.10 using the TensorFlow 2.13 
library, with a multi-layered neural network of two stacks of LSTMs, each 
containing 128 units, followed by a single dense layer with a sigmoid activation 
for the binary classification of anomalies. Training was accomplished using 70% 
of the total dataset, validation was completed using 15% of the total dataset, and 
testing was accomplished using the final 15% of the dataset. Class imbalance was 
mitigated using SMOTE to oversample the minority attack class. The blockchain 
audit system was developed on a Hyperledger Fabric V2.4 network with five 
ordered nodes and ten peer nodes. Timing metrics for each simulation were 
collected on a physical test bed that consisted of a server with an Intel Xeon Gold 
6148 CPU (20 cores @ 2.40GHz) and 256 GB ECC RAM, and running Ubuntu 22.04 
LTS. Each simulation was executed ten times with a random seed, and the results 
are reported as the mean with 95% confidence intervals.

10. Performance metrics
Evaluating a cloud-based firewall framework is dependent on an entire array of 
performance metrics as a means to evaluate its effectiveness, efficiency and 
operability in today’s ever changing cloud environment. Traditional rule-based 
firewalls are generally limited to a static signature comparison and therefore may 
only be assessed against these two dimensions. However, AI enhanced firewalls 
such as MLAF will require multiple performance dimension evaluations including 
but not limited to; detection accuracy, false alarms, response time, throughput, 
and auditing of the firewall configuration. This research utilized a multi-metric 
assessment approach to evaluate the proposed multi-layered adaptive firewall 
(MLAF) against a traditional rule-based firewall. Performance analysis was 
conducted using six primary metrics as follows: Detection Rate, False Positive 
Rate, Policy Response Time, Throughput Overhead, Log Tampering Resistance, 
and Post Quantum Cryptography Key Generation Latency. Collectively, these six 
metrics allow for a comprehensive review of the firewall’s capabilities when 
deployed within a cloud infrastructure. These performance metrics are designed 
to evaluate both the effectiveness of the firewall at identifying malicious traffic 
and activity within the network including but not limited to; zero-day exploits, 
Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks and Advanced Persistent Threats, 
and the operational performance of the firewall, which includes the systems’ 
responsiveness, and any potential degradation of network throughput. Utilizing 
both quantitative experimentation and comparative visualization methods this 
evaluation demonstrated how the combination of deep learning algorithms, 
Adaptive Policy Mechanisms, Blockchain Based Audit Logging, and Post Quantum 
Cryptographic Techniques have significantly contributed to a more robust, 
scalable, and resilient firewall architecture capable of supporting next generation 
cloud computing environments.

10.2 False Positive Rate (FPR)
A False Positive Rate (FPR) is one of the key operational performance metrics for 
evaluating a firewall and is defined as the ratio of legitimate (benign) network 
traffic that has been falsely identified as malicious, thereby causing the traffic to 
be stopped by the firewall. A high FPR is extremely disruptive, as it results in many 
unnecessary alerts, service disruptions for legitimate users, and a significantly 
increased workload for security teams to investigate and clear false alarms. The 
traditional rule-based firewall has a false positive rate (FPR) of 15.0%. This means 
that out of 7 legitimate traffic transactions, about 1 transaction will be wrongly 
identified as malicious. Conversely, the proposed Machine Learning Adaptive 
Firewall (MLAF) has an FPR of 2.1%. This represents a 12.9% decrease in FPR

compared to the traditional approach and a corresponding 86% increase in 
precision. The reason the MLAF achieves such a low FPR is due to its adaptive 
thresholding methodology, which continuously updates its decision boundary with 
an exponential moving average (EMA) of the most recent Anomaly Probability 
Scores (APS). Therefore, the MLAF dynamically adjusts its threshold upwards 
during periods of high volume traffic (i.e., during peak business hours) and 
downwards during low-volume traffic periods, thereby eliminating "normal" traffic 
fluctuations from being misclassified as malicious traffic. Traditional firewalls, by 
comparison, use static, rigid rules that cannot distinguish between typical yet 
abnormal traffic patterns and actual malicious traffic. As a result, traditional 
firewalls are often producing a large number of false alarm messages which 
negatively impacts both the effectiveness of security personnel, as well as the 
overall availability of cloud services.

10.3 Policy Response Time

corresponding mitigation firewall rule is created, validated, and completely 
enforced throughout the entire cloud environment. Because the cloud can be 
severely impacted by threats (such as DDoS floods and lateral movement attacks) 
that can potentially occur in a matter of seconds without appropriate action, this

10.8.2 Inference Overhead
The inference overhead is the constant cost of computation in the process of 
operational deployment. On a typical Intel Xeon Gold 6148 (2.40GHz) CPU (no 
GPU acceleration) which operates as an LSTM model, it takes 0.034 ms to process 
a single traffic window (100 packets, each reduced to 18 feature dimensions) by 
forward propagation through the LSTM layers, and 0.006 ms to classify the final 
result using a sigmoid. Scaled to high-throughput conditions, this framework can 
handle about 29,400 traffic windows per second per CPU core, or analyse 2.94 
million packets per second with the traditional 100-packet aggregation window. 
Nonetheless, this computational capability reaches a bottleneck in extreme cases: 
a 10 Gbps network connection at peak packet rate (around 14.88 million 
packets/sec when using minimum-size Ethernet packets) would need about 5 CPU 
cores full of LSTM inference, without considering other network components of a 
framework. Zero Trust authentication layer introduces 12.3 ms to authenticate 
request (8.1 ms to verify CRYSTALS-Dilithium signature, 2.4 ms to encapsulate 
key in CRYSTALS-Kyber, 1.8 ms to compute trust score). The blockchain logging 
batching model has a low overhead: 0.14 ms to insert a Merkle tree into local 
memory, and periodic blockchain commits (30 s interval) need 180 ms to compute 
the batch Merkle root and send the transaction to the Hyperledger Fabric 
network. Summing up all of the components, the total processing power to process 
1,000 simultaneous network flows is about 3.2 vCPUs, 8 GB RAM, and 120 Mbps 
network bandwidth of the blockchain network.

11. Limitations
Several limitations of the current study should be considered before drawing 
conclusions about the reported results. The limitations include the following: (1) 
The experimental evaluation of the results is based on a simulated cloud 
environment using benchmark datasets and not on actual data from a cloud 
environment in use today. Although CloudSim Plus can provide a realistic 
representation of cloud resource management and networking behavior at the 
time the data are collected, it is possible that production environments could have 
different types of traffic patterns, varying numbers of tenants, and different attack 
strategies compared to the simulations. Therefore, the results may not apply 
directly to real-world scenarios. (2) The number of tenants involved in each round 
of FL was limited to 20 tenants per round. In addition, the overhead associated 
with communication in the federated averaging method used to implement FL in 
this study may differ when hundreds of tenants are involved. This is an area of 
interest for further research. (3) The post-quantum cryptography layer was 
implemented using reference software versions of CRYSTALS-Kyber and 
CRYSTALS-Dilithium. As these layers are expected to be optimized for hardware-
based implementations in future cloud processors, the 12-ms authentication 
handshake overhead measured in this study is likely to be reduced significantly in 
the near term. (4) The threat model assumed in this study did not address 
adversarial ML attacks, in which a sophisticated adversary attempts to craft 
network traffic to evade the LSTM anomaly detection mechanism used to detect 
anomalies. A potential area of focus for future development of the proposed 
framework would be the incorporation of mechanisms to prevent such attacks.
11.1 Adversarial Machine Learning Considerations
The present deployment of the MLAF system lacks explicit countermeasures to 
adversarial machine learning (AML) attacks, in which a high-tech attacker 
carefully crafts network traffic data in a manner that avoids detection by the LSTM 
anomaly detection model. There are three major adversarial threat scenarios that 
we accept. First, evasion attacks are those in which a malicious actor (who knows 
the model architecture and parameters) creates malicious traffic that appears to 
follow benign patterns. For example, an attacker could spread a port scan across 
a longer time window with carefully tuned inter-packet delays that produce LSTM 
hidden states similar to legitimate network reconnaissance activities (e.g., 
network discovery by IT administrators). Second, adversarial examples attacks 
whereby a malicious manipulates the training data or federated learning 
procedure by compromising the training data with mislabeled samples or 
ACCEPTED MANUSCRIPT
ARTICLE IN PRESSARTICLE IN PRESS

malicious gradients to reduce model performance or establish back doors. Third, 
model extraction attacks, in which an adversary repeatedly asks the deployed 
system to generate a decision, in order to determine the decision boundaries and 
decision parameters of the model, and evade it later. The LSTM architecture is 
especially sensitive to adversarial examples in the time domain: even slight 
changes to the arrival time of packets or protocol sequences can cause the hidden 
state trajectory to change without causing anomaly scores, a phenomenon we have 
also found in adversarial examples to sequence model adversarial attacks on 
natural language processing and time-series forecasting.
11.2 Preliminary Defense Mechanisms:
Although full AML protection is beyond the scope of this first framework, we can 
identify some of the promising defense mechanisms that can be included in 
forthcoming implementations. To achieve evasion resistance, a well-known 
method named adversarial training is used, during which the adversary model is 
trained on a combination of benign data, real attacks, and synthetically created 
adversarial samples. We performed initial experiments where we augmented the 
training data with adversarial samples produced by Fast Gradient Sign Method 
(FGSM) and Projected Gradient Descent (PGD) attacks that increased the 
resistance to test-time adversarial examples by 28 percent (in terms of the 
percentage of adversarial samples correctly classified despite adversarial attacks). 
To resist poisoning, the Byzantine-robust aggregation scheme of Section 6.2 
(geometric median, clipping, reputation weighting) partially defends against data 
poisoning attacks in federated learning, but fails to stop label-flipping attacks 
where a malicious attacker labels malicious traffic as benign. More sophisticated 
defenses (like RONI (Reject On Negative Influence)) and gradient clustering would 
be able to detect poisoned batches by identifying statistically abnormal effects on 
model loss. To extract models, rate limiting and query obfuscation strategies may 
be used: rate limiting of queries by a single source IP and controlled randomness 
in anomaly scores (uniform noise to ±0.05) can be used to ensure that the 
adversary cannot accurately predict the decision boundary without dramatically 
affecting the legitimate classification of traffic.
12. Future Directions
Future research directions for the proposed MLAF framework include 
investigating how the framework's components address the dynamic needs of 
distributed cloud-edge networks, intelligent automation, resilient cyber defense 
architectures, and cloud firewall systems. With increasing reliance on 
decentralized edge computing environments and Internet of Things (IoT), the 
operational boundaries of cloud firewalls expand dramatically, and there is a 
pressing need for lightweight, adaptive security mechanisms that can perform 
effectively under resource-constrained operating conditions. Therefore, it is 
crucial to develop the most efficient versions of the long short-term memory 
(LSTM) anomaly detection module for deployment on edge devices (which 
generally have limited hardware capabilities, for example, 256 MB memory and 
processors at approximately 1 GHz). Techniques like model compression, 
knowledge distillation, quantization-aware training and neural architecture

search allow building of compact models of deep learning while keeping detection 
performance of the models. Furthermore, the combination of federated learning 
mechanisms offers an interesting way to establish cooperative threat intelligence 
across various nodes of a network while at the same time avoiding to send 
sensitive operational data to central cloud environments (which could especially 
be important for industrial control systems and critical infrastructure networks 
which have to meet very high cybersecurity standards, such as IEC 62443). One 
of the main research areas related to the proposed framework is to utilize the 
emerging capabilities of large language models to automatically convert human-
readable threat intelligence into machine-executable firewall policies so that less 
dependency exists on the time-consuming and often prone-to-error manual 
creation of firewall policies.
Future systems can therefore generate firewall policies that are syntactically 
correct and context-aware using structured datasets containing threat intelligence 
reports associated with validated firewall-rule sets. Subsequently, these 
generated firewall policies can be tested for their correctness using blockchain-
based validation frameworks to ensure rule origin, validity and accountability 
before they are being deployed. Large Language Models can also serve as 
Explainable Artificial Intelligence interfaces. They can convert complex anomaly 
detection results from deep learning models into understandable natural-language 
explanations for security analysts, thereby improving transparency, trust and 
oversight of AI-driven cybersecurity decisions. Expanding the MLAF framework 
toward an Autonomous Security Operations Center (AutoSOC) architecture 
presents another key research direction and development path for future firewall-
detection mechanisms to act as intelligent agents in an automated incident-
response system consisting of SIEM platforms, threat-intelligence feeds and 
endpoint-detection systems.
Reinforcement-learning techniques can be utilized to represent incident-response 
workflows as Markov decision processes; therefore, automated agents can develop 
optimized response-strategies that find the optimal trade-off between rapid threat-
containment and minimal business-disruption. Blockchain-based audit trails can, 
in addition, ensure that all responses initiated by automated agents are 
documented in tamper-evident ledgers, thereby enabling AI-driven cybersecurity 
operations to meet both legal accountability and regulatory compliance 
requirements. Beyond the previously mentioned extensions to the overall 
architecture, future cloud-firewall systems should include advanced AI-driven 
traffic-analysis functions to analyze large amounts of network-flow data in near-
real-time and improve the precision of anomaly-detections while minimizing false 
positive rates using adaptive learning-models. As well, Post-Quantum-
Cryptographic mechanisms will be more important than ever to protect 
communication data from possible vulnerabilities due to quantum computing 
capabilities in the future. In addition, the introduction of Zero Trust-Security 
principles will enhance cloud-firewall architectures by introducing continuous-
authentication, behavior-based-access-control and context-aware-policy-
enforcement to guarantee that network-resources can only be accessed when the 
accessing entity has been authenticated.

12.1 Impact Statement
The larger scale implications for society and business of the new framework 
outlined above far exceed the technical support for the research being done in this 
paper. Cloud computing is now part of many types of critical national 
infrastructures and its uses can be found across various sectors including; 
healthcare information systems, financial transaction processing, government 
data services and emergency communications networks. Failures in the security 
of the cloud platforms can lead to financial loss and even harm to human lives and 
safety. The framework will create a roadmap that will significantly enhance the 
overall resiliency of the cloud platform by showing how four separate technologies 
(AI-based detection, Quantum Resistant Authentication, Privacy Preserving 
Federated Intelligence, and Blockchain Anchored Audit Integrity) can be 
integrated together to form one operational cloud firewall. Furthermore, the open 
publication of the design, algorithms, and experimental methodologies used to test 
the framework will enable researchers and practitioners in the field of cloud 
security to leverage these results to further develop, validate and extend the 
findings, and ultimately accelerate the transition from theoretical security models 
to deployable security assurance solutions for cloud computing infrastructures

robustness systems designed specifically to the cloud firewall scenarios. There are 
four research directions that we discern. First, certified defenses that can offer 
provable robustness guarantees: methods like randomized smoothing can certify 
that the prediction of a model will not change in an L2-ball of radius r about any 
input, which can provide formal guarantees against bounded adversarial 
examples. Second, ensemble diversity strategies involving a combination of 
heterogeneous anomaly detection models (LSTM, Isolation Forest, Autoencoder-
based reconstruction, Transformer-based attention mechanism) trained on various 
feature representations, which means that it becomes exponentially harder to 
avoid all models at once. Third, runtime observation of model behavior: identifying 
distributional shift in real-time by comparing the statistical characteristics of

observed traffic with training distribution, alarming when there is a potential 
adversarial manipulation due to distributional shift. Fourth, joint threat 
intelligence in which multiple deployments exchange (privacy-preserving) 
signatures of observed adversarial patterns, and then use them to jointly protect 
against shared adversarial strategies. These defenses can only be empirically 
evaluated using curated adversarial datasets that are specific to cloud network 
security, and these are not available in the repositories of publicly available 
benchmarks at present. We suggest the creation of standardized AML assessment 
procedures of cloud firewalls, similar to current adversarial robustness 
benchmarks in computer vision (ImageNet-A, Robust Bench) and NLP (Text 
Attack), to allow comparative evaluation of defensive methods across research 
groups.
13. Conclusion
Modern cloud computing environments are increasingly at risk due to the 
migration of applications and data into distributed cloud computing architectures; 
therefore, cloud firewalls have become a key part of the security of many 
organizations using the cloud. This study presents an integrated cloud firewall 
framework that enhances security against current and emerging cyber threats by 
integrating artificial intelligence (AI) methods, adaptive security models, and 
secure infrastructure mechanisms. Anomaly detection and deep learning models 
utilize AI to scan large amounts of network traffic for anomalies in real-time, 
allowing for proactive threat identification and rapid responses to malicious 
activities. In addition, post-quantum Cryptography mechanisms will provide long-
term protection for the encryption of data and address the vulnerabilities 
associated with future Quantum computing capabilities. Additionally, the 
integration of zero trust security principles provides continuous authentication, 
context-aware access control, and adaptive policy enforcement to minimize 
unauthorized access to cloud environments and increase the overall security 
posture of cloud environments. The proposed architecture utilizes dynamic threat 
intelligence integration for the purpose of updating firewall policies in real-time 
based on evolving patterns of emerging attacks; thereby allowing organizations to 
rapidly react to changing cyber threats and decrease their likelihood of breach 
success.
In addition, the proposed Multi-Layered Firewall Architecture utilizes multiple 
layers of defense in order to enhance security while preserving performance 
metrics (latency and throughput) of the existing firewall system. In addition, the 
proposed framework utilizes blockchain-based audit mechanisms to support 
tamper-resistant logging, secure rule validation, and transparent event auditing 
of security-related events in cloud environments; thereby enhancing trust, 
accountability, and regulatory compliance in cloud security operations. A 
quantitative assessment of the proposed system has demonstrated a detection rate 
of 94.7%, false positive rate of 2.1%, and an average threat response time of 87 
ms, which represents a relative improvement of 35.3%, 86.0% and 89.8%, 
respectively when compared to traditional firewalls. An ablation analysis was 
conducted to assess how each architectural component of the proposed framework 
contributed to measured performance improvements. Specifically, it was

determined that AI-based anomaly detection, Zero Trust Authentication 
Mechanisms, Threat Intelligence Updates, Federated Learning Collaboration, 
Blockchain Audit Logging and Post Quantum Cryptographic Protection were all 
contributing factors in achieving the most significant performance improvements. 
Most importantly, the blockchain-anchored audit system supported a reduction of 
the time required to detect tampering of logs from 24 hours to 1.2 seconds in 
traditional systems, thus supporting the compliant requirements of modern 
regulations and near real-time monitoring of security-related events. Overall, the 
results of this study indicate that the proposed framework is a significant 
improvement over current state-of-the-art cloud firewall technology, as it supports 
the development of a scalable, intelligent and resilient security architecture 
capable of protecting next-generation cloud infrastructure from increasingly 
complex cyber threats while maintaining operational reliability and efficiency.
