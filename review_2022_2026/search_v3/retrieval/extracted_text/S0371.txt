Abstract 
The concept of artificial intelligence (AI) has been increasingly used in the field of cybersecurity, especially in the 
development of intrusion detection systems (IDS). The conventional approach to developing intrusion detection 
systems involves the use of rule-based systems as well as signatures of attacks. However, this approach has been 
criticized for its inability to deal with emerging threats such as zero-day exploits. This has led to the development 
of intrusion detection systems that incorporate the concept of machine learning in the analysis of network activities. 
This approach has been effective in the detection of anomalies that could be indicative of malicious activities. 
However, the use of this approach in the development of intrusion detection  systems has raised ethical issues. This 
paper seeks to discuss the ethical issues surrounding the application of AI-based intrusion detection systems by first 
presenting an overview of the technical basis of the systems and the technical benefits they off er. Secondly, the 
paper will discuss the ethical issues surrounding the application of the system. Some of the ethical issues discussed 
will include the lack of explainability of machine learning algorithms, the possibility of biased decision-making by 
the system, and the ethical issues surrounding the collection of large amounts of personal information. Additionally, 
the paper will discuss the technical solutions to the ethical issues surrounding the application of the system. Some of 
the technical solutio ns to the ethical issues will include the application of explainable AI, federated learning, 
differential privacy, and synthetic data. Lastly, the paper will discuss the regulatory frameworks adopted by the 
European Union, the US, and South Korea regarding the application of the system. The analysis identifies the gaps 
in the regulations as well as the technical limitations in the ethical use of AI -based IDS. The study offers 
recommendations to improve the design of AI -based IDS. The study emphasizes the ne ed to strike a balance 
between the increasing technical capabilities of AI systems and the need to have proper ethical and regulatory 
checks in the use of AI in cybersecurity. 
Keywords:  cybersecurity, Artificial intelligence, intrusion detection systems, ethical challenges, network activities 
 
 
1. Introduction  
Due to the rapid digitalization of modern societies, the role of cybersecurity has gained considerable 
significance. Organizations across various industries are heavily relying on interconnected systems, cloud services, 
and large-scale data infrastructures to support day-to-day operations [1]. Although these emerging technologies 
are helping to improve the efficiency and innovation of the systems, they are also making the systems more 
vulnerable to cyber -attacks.  Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) play an important role in identifying malicious 
activities within computer networks and computer systems. Traditional Intrusion Detection Systems' techniques mainly 
depend on signature-based or rule-based detection techniques. These techniques can successfully identify malicious 
attacks on computer networks and computer systems. However, traditional techniques may not be effective in

identifying new attacks or malicious activities on computer networks and computer systems [2]. In addition, the 
complexity and volume of network traffic make traditional detection techniques less effective and make manual 
detection more difficult to implement. 
In order to overcome the limitations of the above-mentioned methods, artificial intelligence and machine learning 
techniques have been widely used in modern cybersecurity systems. AI -based intrusion detection systems can

3. Technical Foundations of AI-Based Intrusion Detection Systems 
3.1. IDS Fundamentals 
Definition of IDS  
An intrusion Detection System (IDS) is a security tool that can be implemented in either hardware or software. It 
monitors a network or system for malicious activity by analyzing network traffic, system activity, and carried data. 
When anomalous behavior is detected, the IDS generates alerts that provides metadata such as the detection time, 
source and destination IP addresses, and sometimes segments of packet payloads [2] – [17]. 
IDS vs. IPS  
IDS should be distinguished from Intrusion Prevention Systems (IPS). An IDS does not stop a threat; it merely detects 
the suspicious activity and notifies administrators. On the other hand, an IPS not only detects the attack and alerts 
the administrator, b ut also intervenes by blocking the attack. IDS and IPS are usually used together, working as 
complementary parts of a layered security setup. Importantly, an IDS cannot use the same settings for all 
organizations, as it should be configured based on the sp ecific requirements of that organization as uniform 
configuration cannot effectively handle different network setups. 
Types of IDS: NIDS / HIDS / CIDS  
IDS architectures can be classified based on their deployment location and what they monitor. According to [2], a 
Network-Based IDS (NIDS) monitors the entire network traffic and is usually deployed at strategic points such as 
the Demilitarized Zone DMZ, w hich allows it to have a broad view of network activity. It captures and analyzes 
inbound and outbound protocol packets from all devices in order to detect malicious traffic. Many organizations 
use NIDS together with firewalls and application -layer protection mechanisms to enhance web server security. A 
Host-Based IDS (HIDS) focuses on a specific host and monitors both inbound and outbound packets at the individual 
device level. It runs on servers, intermediate devices, and endpoints systems, and has access to both the internet and

the local network. HIDS monitors log files, running programs, and system files, and alerts the user when suspicious 
behavior occurs. It can also help prevent malware from spreading across the network. A Cloud-Based IDS (CIDS) is 
designed to monitor cloud e nvironments and detect malicious behavior in cloud traffic. Together, NIDS and HIDS 
provide a layered defense strategy by covering both networkwide and host-level threats. However, traditional IDS 
approaches mainly rely on rule -based detection, which makes  them less effective against new or evolving cyber 
threats [16] 
Signature-based / anomaly-based / hybrid  
Initially, IDS mainly used two categories of detection methods, which are Signature -Based and Anomaly -Based 
Detection. Signature-Based Detection uses a database of known attacks or a series of attacks. It matches the current 
network activity with the datab ase. In case of a match, then the activity is considered a threat. Anomaly -Based 
Detection uses a different technique. First, normal behavior is identified, and then this information is used to identify 
unknown attacks, which are not normal. Third, there i s the Hybrid Approach, which combines Signature -Based and 
Anomaly-Based Detection.  
IDS tools examples 
Several IDS tools are commonly used in practice.  
SolarWinds Security Event: Manager integrates log data from different parts of the IT infrastructure and analyzes 
them centrally, using both signature-based and anomaly-based detection methods.  
McAfee LiveSafe: combines a signature-based IPS to prevent known attacks with an anomaly -based IDS to detect 
and alert on unknown or unusual behavior.  
Blumira: monitors IT infrastructure for suspicious activities that could lead to data breaches and is able to respond 
to attacks in real time to stop malicious actors. 
 
Evolution from Traditional IDS to AI-Based IDS 
Traditional IDS limitations 
Traditional IDS methods, including signature -based and rule -based approaches, are often inadequate against 
evolving attack techniques. Traditional intrusion detection systems cannot identify new threats if there is no existing 
known signature, while anomaly-based systems may fail to distinguish between malicious and legitimate anomalies, 
leading to a high number of false positives [16] 
Traditional IDS rely on static rules, whereas modern attacks use irregular patterns [2]. [1] demonstrated that known 
Indicators of Compromise (IoCs) are commonly used in traditional threat detection methods, but conventional rule -
based systems often fail to respond in real time. Additionally, [2] reported that the massive volume of data makes 
manual detection impossible, and traditional SOC operations require human analysts to monitor large amounts of 
network traffic continuously, manually detect anomalies, and generate reports, which is highly ti me-consuming and 
resource-intensive. Security managers usually try to prevent intrusions from external sources; however, web services 
in the internet DMZ (such as HTTPS, DNS, and FTP) must remain accessible, meaning firewalls cannot simply block 
them. Therefore, distinguishing legitimate acce ss from malicious activity is essential, making intrusion detection a 
major research field in network security to protect internal networks from unusual access. One major trend that is 
reshaping the cybersecurity landscape is the development of AI-enhanced threat detection mechanisms. 
Signature based limitations  
Signature-based detection relies on a database of known attacks or predefined attack sequences and compares 
current network activity against this database. If a match is found, the activity is classified as a threat. However, 
traditional intrusion detection systems cannot identify new threats when no existing signature is available for them 
[16]. These systems also depend on static rules, while modern attacks often use irregular and evolving patterns. 
Furthermore, known Indicators of Compromise (IoCs) are widely used in traditional threat detection methods [1].  
False Positives 
Anomaly-based detection systems often struggle to distinguish between malicious and legitimate anomalies, which 
results in a high number of false positives  [16]. To address this issue, AI can flag potentially malicious activities for 
further investigation instead of treating every deviation as a confirmed threat. In addition, AI can prioritize 
vulnerabilities based on their potential impact, allowing security personnel to focus on the most critical issues [1]. 
Concept Drift  
[1] reported that Cyber threats are evolving in both complexity and speed, encompassing polymorphic malware 
and zero-day exploits. AI has the capability to continuously learn and adapt to new data and emerging threats, 
which enhances the robustness of security measures over time. AI-based intrusion detection systems leverage machine 
learning and deep learning to learn from these evolving attack behaviors; however, this intelligence also introduces 
unique ethical and governance challenges. Consequently, there i s a growing need for autonomous and adaptive 
defense mechanisms. 
Need for Adaptive Systems  
Cybersecurity has changed a lot with the rise of AI. Instead of relying only on humans to watch over systems, AI now 
works alongside people, handling the heavy lifting like processing large amounts of data fast, while humans focus 
on understanding the cont ext and making ethical decisions. AI can take over repetitive tasks, such as monitoring 
network traffic or analyzing alerts, which lets security teams work smarter and spend more time on complex 
problems [1]. In traditional Security Operations Centers (SOCs), analysts have to watch tons of network activity all 
the time, spot unusual behavior, and write reports, which is very time-consuming. AI-based intrusion detection systems 
(IDS) help reduce this workload by spotting threats quickly and letting humans focus on bigger decisions [2].

Machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) are at the core of modern IDS because they can handle huge datasets 
and detect complex patterns in real time [16]. These systems not only find anomalies but can also catch new types 
of attacks that older network - or host-based systems might miss. By monitoring user activity and network traffic 
constantly, AI-driven IDS can reduce the time between spotting a threat and responding to it, making the whole 
process faster and more efficient. AI also helps with automatic responses. When it detects a problem, AI systems 
can isolate infected devices, block suspicious traffic, and start fixing the issue almost immediately. Autom ated 
systems make it easier to contain threats and get systems back to normal quickly. Security Orchestration, Automation,

On the threat intellig ence side, AI can make sense of data from different sources like threat feeds, social media,

Why AI Became Necessary  
There has been rapid growth in the integration of AI into the domain of cybersecurity. This has, in turn, changed the 
way organizations are dealing with the overall security of their digital assets. Thus, the integration of AI into the 
existing cybersecurity framework can be seen as a fundamental shift in the overall strategy. AI can process enormous 
amounts of data at extremely high speeds, thus helping identify anomalies and potential threats, which might be 
extremely difficult for humans to identify in r eal time. AI can also be used in conjunction with predictive analysis, 
where data can be used to predict potential future attacks [1].  
Today, almost every industry requires data flow in order to function properly. This, in turn, leads us to an essential 
question regarding the overall safety of the data. With the rise of cyber-attacks and data breaches, failing to take 
proper care of the o verall cybersecurity framework only leads to more chances of cyber -attacks. Although AI is 
being used at the highest levels in terms of productivity and innovation, it is also being used by cyber attackers to 
create sophisticated, self-encrypting malware and exploit code, which can be difficult to trace. 
 
3.2. Machine Learning in IDS 
Overview 
Machine learning (ML) is a branch of artificial intelligence (AI) that allows systems to learn from data, recognize 
patterns, and make decisions with little to no human intervention. ML can be classified into two main categories: 
supervised learning and unsupervised learning. Various approaches: supervised, unsupervised, and semi-supervised, 
have been explored.  
Supervised ML  
In supervised learning, models are trained on data sets that are already labeled with the correct answers to enable 
the model to make predictions based on particular characteristics of the data. In the context of intrusion detection 
systems, this implies that the model will be trained on historical data that has been classified as normal or malicious 
and will use this to predict the outcomes based on particular characteristics of the data. In the context of 
cybersecurity, supervised learning is an important  tool in the field because it can be used to solve classification 
problems. It can be used to classify whether incoming emails are legitimate or spam or whether particular network 
activities are good or bad. It can be used to classify data using decision trees, support vector machines (SVM), and 
logistic regression because it can recognize patterns in the data effectively [1] 
Unsupervised ML  
Unsupervised learning, on the other hand, does not rely on labeled data. Instead, it seeks to identify patterns and 
anomalies within the dataset without prior knowledge of what constitutes normal or malicious behavior [28] 
Reinforcement Learning 
According to [11], in contrast to traditional ML techniques, reinforcement learning (RL) aims to find an optimal 
learning policy strategy during the model-building phase. 
Traditional ML relies upon an input and output pair, where an ML model receives a given event feature vector as 
input and outputs its estimated class value. RL -based approaches are significantly different. RL -based techniques 
are performed through an entity called the agent. RL-based techniques are not given the event class value. Instead, 
after an action is performed, the agent only receives the reward for its action and the subsequent environment 
state. Reinforcement learning has also been explored in IDS  to enable adaptive and self -optimizing detection 
mechanisms. [10] propose a reinforcement learning–based intrusion detection model that maintains high detection 
accuracy over extended periods without frequent model updates by combining long -term learning with transfer 
learning, significantly reducing computational resou rces while outperforming traditional ML and deep learning 
schemes in F-measure.  
Common Machine Learning Algorithms  
Traditional ML methods including Artificial Neural Networks (ANN), Support Vector Machines (SVM), fuzzy 
approaches, swarm intelligence, and evolutionary computation techniques have been extensively employed for IDS. 
ANN: Inspired by the structure of the human brain with interconnected layers of neurons, can learn complex patterns 
from data and have proven effective for intrusion detection tasks [18].  SVM: One notable approach is the use of 
genetic algorithms combined with support vector machines (SVM) for intrusion detection. [9] used a genetic algorithm

to select key network features for an SVM classifier, improving anomaly -based IDS accuracy over traditional 
methods. Fuzzy Approaches: Leveraging fuzzy logic, these methods handle ambiguous or imprecise data, making 
them effective for analysing uncertain o r noisy network traffic. Swarm Intelligence: Inspired by social insect 
behaviour, algorithms like Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) and Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) optimize feature 
selection and rule generation for IDS [19]. 
Applications of Machine Learning in Intrusion Detection Systems 
Using machine learning, IDS can learn patterns and create baselines of normal activity, known as trust models, to 
compare against current traffic. Numerous studies have explored this approach to develop IDS models that more 
efficiently detect security threats. For example, [8] proposed Passban, an anomaly-based IDS for IoT edge devices. 
By using edge computing and lightweight hardware  accelerated logic gates, Passban  identifies deviations from 
normal behavior directly on IoT gateways, showing how AI -based IDS can detect novel attacks in resource-limited 
environments beyond traditional SOCs. Unlike signature-based IDS, it can catch unknown threats by spotting unusual 
patterns. In a more advanced application, Behavioral Analytics extends anomaly detection to user and entity 
behavior. Machine learning models create baselines of typical activity, such as login patterns, data access 
frequency, and activity contexts. By dete cting deviations from these baselines, organizations can identify 
compromised accounts or insider threats that might evade conventional anomaly-based detection. 
3.3. Deep Learning in IDS 
Overview 
Deep learning has received a lot of attention in recent years due to its ability to effectively identify intricate intrusion 
patterns within network traffic [53]. This is attributed to the fact that deep learning models consist of multiple layers, 
which learn increasingly abstract representations of data over time. This has, in turn, led to significant milestones in 
the field of computer vision, such as speech recog nition [12]. This has led to the implementation of deep learning 
within intrusion detection systems, which can identify intricate patterns of intrusion within network traffic. As such, 
numerous cybersecurity solutions implemented over the years have incorporated deep l earning, particularly within 
anomaly-based intrusion detection systems. For example, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and Recurrent 
Neural Networks (RNN) have significantly contributed to the improvement of intrusion detection. Furthermore, deep 
learning models can identify spatiotemporal patterns within network traffic, which is often difficult for traditional 
models [26] – [27]. 
Most of the deep learning models used for intrusion detection purposes are trained on labeled network traffic 
datasets, such as CIC IDS, KDD Cup 99, NSL KDD, and UNSW NB15, which provide valuable insights for improving 
intrusion detection capabilities.  
Core Architectures 
CNN 
Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) are commonly used for the spatial analysis of network traffic, while 
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) and Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks are applied to learn temporal 
sequences in the data [13]. Previous studies have shown that, compared with other IDS classifiers, intrusion 
detection models based on CNNs often achieve higher detection rates and better precision [3]. 
RNN/LSTM  
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), particularly their advanced variant Long ShortTerm  Memory (LSTM) 
networks, are well suited for analyzing time -dependent data. Their ability to capture temporal relationships 
makes them useful for tracking network activity patterns and detecting potential intrusions through sequential 
behavior analysis. LSTM networks, in particular, are effective at handling sequential data, which allows them to 
identify temporal patterns and anomalies in network traffic that develop over time [15]. Some studies have also 
proposed RNN-based IDS models and compared them with other machine learning approaches such as ANN, 
Random Forest (RF), and SVM, showing that RNN -IDS models can achieve high classification accuracy [4]. In 
addition, hybrid approaches that combine CNNs and RNNs have shown promising results, as they leverage the 
strengths of both architectures to improve accuracy and detect more complex attack patterns [20]. 
DNN  
While CNN and RNN architectures have shown strong performance, other deep learning approaches such as 
Deep Neural Networks (DNNs) have also been applied to IDS.  
Several studies have proposed DNN -based IDS models, including [5], who validated their approach on 
benchmark datasets, and [6], who enhanced cloud -based anomaly detection using a hybrid optimization 
framework 
Advantages  
Deep learning models provide a vital advantage in intrusion detection systems in terms of their ability to learn 
features from data automatically. Models such as Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Recurrent Neural 
Networks (RNNs), and even more recent m odels such as transformers have shown promising results in detecting 
unforeseen attack patterns in intrusion detection systems. Several researchers have also shown that these models 
can learn spatiotemporal features in network traffic flows, which is usually hard for traditional models to learn [26] 
– [27]. For instance, [5] designed a DNN-based IDS model for detecting unforeseen attacks in intrusion detection 
systems and evaluated their model using various benchmark datasets. The datasets used included NSL-KDD, UNSW-
NB15, Kyoto, WSN-DS, and CICIDS2017. Using different datasets improved the accuracy of their model and made

it robust against various types of cyber -attacks. [6] designed a DNN model for detecting anomalies in cloud 
computing systems using a hybrid optimization technique involving an Improved Genetic Algorithm and Simulated

It is, however, important to note that NLP is not widely used in traditional IDS, as the data used is mostly structured, 
such as network traffic data, which is not usually associated with NLP. Instead, other techniques such as decision

rather than solely relying on numerical features of data for analysis.  
Limitations and Deployment Challenges  
Although these methods are accurate there are a number of challenges that need to be addresses. 
 
Computational cost: These challenges include the computational cost of training these models using gradient descent-
based algorithms such as Adam or RMSprop. in this respect, data augmentation, dropout, and batch normalization 
are often employed for the reduction of overfitting. However, the training of deep learning models for use in IDS 
systems is often associated with high computational costs that can only be addressed using high -performance 
hardware such as GPUs or TPUs [13]. Deep neural networks (DNNs), although powerful, face additional challenges 
as computational complexity grows with the number of hidden layers and neurons, which can make real -time 
detection less efficient. Recurrent architectures like LSTMs and RNNs are particularly useful for capturing long-term 
dependencies in network traffic but come with long training times due to their intricate structures. RNNs, in particular, 
can be computationally intensive and are prone to vanishing or exploding gradients, which can hinder performance 
on longer sequences [14]. 
 
Need for large labeled datasets: Supervised models achieve high accuracy but require large labeled  datasets, 
whereas unsupervised methods can work with unlabeled data, often at the cost of lower accuracy. Semi-supervised 
techniques reduce the need for labeling but may perform inconsistently on smaller datasets. Despite their strengths, 
these approaches are limited because they depend on carefully labeled training data, which restricts their ability 
to generalize across different traffic types and attack scenarios and makes them inefficient due to the manual effort 
needed to label network flows [44] 
Relying on only a few datasets may fail to capture all real -world intrusion scenarios, so training ML models on 
diverse datasets representing various types of network traffic and attack patterns allows IDS to generalize better 
and detect a broader range of threats. Using diverse datasets exposes models to multiple attack scenarios, though 
challenges such as data imbalance and domain adaptation must be addressed to fully benefit from this approach. 
For example, an XAI model demonstrated high predictive perfo rmance, achieving an accuracy of 87%, but it 
required the UNSW-NB15 dataset, which contains over 2.5 million records and nine different attack types [39]. 
 
Data imbalance: In AI -based IDS, data imbalance is one of the key issues that can significantly impact the 
performance of IDS models. Normally, the majority of data instances tend to be "normal" traffic, while anomalous 
attack data instances tend to be les s frequent. This can cause the IDS model to be biased towards the majority -
class instances during training, ignoring the minority -class instances. To deal with this problem, data imbalance 
handling techniques such as oversampling, undersampling, and Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs) have been 
proposed. For example, oversampling -based techniques, such as the Synthetic Minority Over -sampling Technique 
(SMOTE) [46], create new data instances to increase the size of the minority -class instances. Similarly, GANs have 
also been found to be promising for handling data imbalance, although training such models is known to be 
challenging, expensive, and requires careful hyperparameter adjustment. These techniques should be supported by 
large datasets [53]. 
 
Real-world scalability issues: While many AI -based IDS solutions perform well in labs or controlled environments, 
they often struggle when deployed in large, diverse, and constantly changing production networks. Combining new 
technologies, like edge comput ing, with existing infrastructure can create extra complexities and requires careful 
planning. Traditional setups may not handle increasing network traffic well, and making sure new AI tools integrate 
smoothly with current systems is another challenge. On top of that, staying effective against constantly evolving 
cyber threats means systems need regular updates, including threat intelligence feeds and periodic retraining of 
machine-learning models [28]. Many models also aren’t fully ready for real -world conditions because they’re 
usually tested on benchmark datasets, like KDD Cup 99, which don’t reflect the full complexity and dynamics of live 
network traffic [2]. 
 
 
4. Ethical Challenges in AI-Based IDS 
Despite impressive performance, ML and DL -based NIDS have shown over 95% detection accuracy in controlled 
tests [51], their real-world deployment faces important limitations. Most current models assume that training and 
testing data follow a static distribution, but network environments are constantly evolving. This concept drift, where 
the statistical behavior of both benign and malicious traffic changes over time, can cause models trained on original

datasets to misclassify or miss emerging threats. Beyond technical performance, these challenges raise important 
ethical questions: How transparent are the models’ decisions? Could biases in the data lead to unfair or 
disproportionate detection? And how is  sensitive network data being protected during training and operation? 
Exploring these issues is crucial for designing AI -based IDS that are not only accurate but also responsible and 
trustworthy  
4.1. Transparency and Explainability 
Problem 
Machine learning -based intrusion detection systems (IDS) are usually characterized as “black boxes systems 
meaning that their decision -making process is opaque and difficult to understand. This lack of transparency not 
only limits trustworthiness but also raises ethical concerns around accountability and fairness. While state-of-the-art 
machine learning models are known to perform exceptionally well in various tasks, their level of complexity makes 
them less explainable. Consequently, a security analyst may  find it difficult to validate or trust. Deep learning 
models, like DNN and Ensemble, work on the principle of learning complex patterns from large amounts of data. 
However, the inherent opacity of deep learning -based IDS systems poses a major ethical and operational 
challenges for security analysts. The black-box nature of deep learning models makes it hard for security analysts 
to assess the correctness of the detection alerts, since they do not have sufficient information on the features and 
factors affecting the model’s decisions [22]. Evaluating the validity of IDS alerts and taking appropriate remedial 
actions requires clear insights into the rationale behind the system’s decisions, yet black-box models rarely provide 
such transparency [39]. The complex architectures of deep learning models have added to the problem in terms of 
interpretability, with analysts struggling to understand how the system arrives at its conclusions [21]. The major 
issues in this regard have been due to the lack of interpretability in the models, which has limited analysts’ ability 
to understand, trust, and act upon decisions made by the system and also raise ethical concerns, which include 
undetected errors, inability to audit decisions, and lack of accountability .In case any untoward or incorrect results 
are obtained, it has been found to be complex in terms of diagnosing the problem, which has led to sub -optimal 
performance or incorrect results going unn oticed. The problem has been compounded in industries like finance, 
health care, and government, which require transparency and accountability in decision -making processes. In such 
industries, black-box models have been found to be inadequate in meeting regulatory requirements, owing to the 
inability to audit explanations for predictions or actions made by the system [23]. 
 
Solutions:  
To address the transparency issues of black -box models in IDS, a new technology called Explainable Artificial 
Intelligence (XAI) has been proposed. XAI is a field of research in artificial intelligence aimed at increasing the 
interpretability and trustworthiness of complex models, making them more comprehensible to humans [39] - [24]. 
The inclusion of XAI in forensic cybersecurity helps security professionals gain more insights from the models by 
providing clear explanations of model decisions, increasing their trust, enhancing technical validation, and 
strengthening ethical accountabil ity, while also optimizing the performance of IDS. Recently, the field of XAI has 
gained more research, and various XAI technologies have been proposed. Among them, Local Interpretable Model-
Agnostic Explanations (LIME) and Shapley Additive Explanations (S HAP) suggested by [37] have gained more 
recognition for their global and local interpretability capabilities.  
LIME generates explanations for the black-box model by perturbing the instance and creating a weighted model 
of the nearest neighbors to the instance, providing a local approximation of the black -box model for a specific 
prediction. SHAP, on the other hand , assigns importance values to each feature using Shapley values from game 
theory, indicating positive or negative contributions while satisfying fairness properties such as Efficiency, Symmetry, 
Dummy, and Additivity. Incorporating XAI techniques into IDS  pipelines can also help reduce false positives, 
enabling analysts to better validate flagged threats; for example, a 15% accuracy improvement has been 
reported when using LIME with ensemble models [42]. 
 
Weaknesses  
Despite these advances, XAI methods face several limitations. LIME and SHAP explanations can be unstable under 
small input changes and may provide misleading attributions for complex temporal features [38]. Both techniques 
require significant computational resources, limiting their use in real -time IDS [39]. Moreover, the absence of 
standard evaluation criteria and measures also makes it hard to evaluate the reliability and reproducibility of the 
explanations [38]. XAI may also show correlations rather than cause -effect relations, which may lead to wrong 
interpretations in forensics. In the last few years, research on XAI has been increasing, and as a result, several 
techniques have been developed for XAI. Among all, Local Interpretable Model Agnostic Explanations (LIME) and 
Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) are notable for providing both global and local interpretability of the black 
box models. LIME achieves this by modifying the input and creating weighted interpretable models based on 
proximity to the instance, providing a local approximation of the black box for each prediction [37]. 

 
INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF APPLIED ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE AND ROBOTICS 
8 
 
SHAP, despite its high theoretical accuracy, also faces major computational challenges. For instance, the approach 
demands the evaluation of the model several times to obtain the values for feature attribution. However, this results 
in high computation tim e, especially for the model -agnostic approach, KernelSHAP , which is known to have an 
exponential increase in computation with the number of features [38][39]. Therefore, the approach is less 
applicable for real -time intrusion detection systems, making the approach more appropriate for post -intrusion 
analysis. Mo reover, the approach is mathematically complex, making it hard for non -experts to understand. 
Additionally, the approach is limited in its applicability to high -dimensional data, especially for deep learning 
models, as obtaining values or explanations can be computationally expensive, making the approach challenging 
[39]. 
LIME provides faster and more intuitive explanations compared to SHAP, making it useful for quick diagnostics and 
exploratory analysis. However, its results are highly sensitive to the perturbation technique and sample generation 
process, meaning repeated analyses of the same instance can yield different explanations. LIME also lacks formal 
guarantees such as completeness or consistency, which reduces its reliability in legal or forensic reporting. Moreover, 
its reliance on local linear approximations may misrepresent complex model behavior, particularly in deep learning 
contexts. While these characteristics make LIME convenient for initial investigations, it remains unsuitable for 
applications that demand high stability, fidelity, and legal defensibility.  
Unsolved Problems / Research Gaps  
Many studies have explored explainable AI (XAI) in intrusion detection systems (IDS), focusing on how these methods 
can improve interpretability and effectiveness. However, a major challenge remains: the lack of standardized 
metrics and benchmarks to evalu ate explanation quality specifically for IDS tasks. Although numerous works 
propose XAI techniques, there is currently no universally accepted framework for assessing robustness, stability, or 
domain-specific utility in security analysis. Existing surveys highlight this absence of agreed-upon evaluation metrics 
as a significant gap in explainable intrusion detection research [54]. 
The main challenge in implementing XAI for network intrusion detection lies in validating these tools, testing their 
quality, and evaluating relevant security metrics. Without universally accepted metrics, it becomes difficult to 
compare and benchmark diff erent XAI methods. Reliable metrics are crucial, particularly in high -stakes domains, 
to prevent misleading explanations and ensure that outputs can be trusted for securitycritical decision-making [55]. 
Another important research gap exists at the intersection of XAI performance and deployment feasibility. 
Optimizing XAI for high -frequency and resource -constrained network environments is critical. Popular post -hoc 
techniques like SHAP and LIME, while effective for explanation, come with computational costs that hinder real-time 
applicability in high-traffic networks. For example, SHAP provides comprehensive feature attributions but is often 
too slow for rapid deployment, especially in high -speed networks where latency must be minimized [39]. LIME is 
somewhat more efficient but still presents challenges in such environments 
Recent systematic analyses [56] highlight that the impact of XAI on detection performance and computational 
efficiency in resource-constrained IoT networks is still not fully understood. These studies show a significant trade -
off between achieving high explainability and maintaining com putational efficiency, which can make real -world 
deployment infeasible. Furthermore, the lack of standardized reporting of computational metrics remains a major 
obstacle to practical progress. Addressing these gaps, through standardized evaluation, efficie ncy optimization, 
and careful deployment design will be essential to ensure that XAI methods enhance both the technical and ethical 
performance of IDS in real-world settings. 
4.2. Bias and Fairness 
Problem 
One of the major ethical and technical challenges in AI -based IDS is bias, often resulting in high false -positive or 
false-negative rates. This problem largely stems from imbalanced datasets, where attack instances are far fewer 
than benign traffic, causing models to favor the majority class. Despite advances in machine learning, many systems 
remain biased because most available data represent normal behavior, and attack data are scarce. Class 
imbalance directly contributes to incorrect detection, while the  laborintensive process of labeling network traffic, 
especially evolving or rare attacks, makes it difficult to prepare balanced datasets for effective adaptation [33] 
– [34]. 
False alarms are another consequence of this bias. Anomaly-based AI IDS models often learn only normal behavior, 
so any harmless change in normal traffic may be misclassified as malicious. This not only wastes IDS resources but 
also reduces overall system effectiveness. Traditional misuse detection relies on predefined signatures, which work 
well for known attacks but fail for unknown ones, often producing high false-positive rates similar to anomaly-based 
approaches [2]. Consequently, human analysts are still required to verify AI alerts, limiting operational efficiency 
and raising ethical concerns regarding trust and accountability in automated intrusion detection. 
Solutions 
To mitigate bias and class imbalance, several strategies have been proposed. Conventional techniques include 
undersampling and oversampling [36] – [45]. Random Undersampling (RUS) is a widely used technique to address 
class imbalance in machine learning datasets. For instance, it balances datasets by randomly removing samples 

 
INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF APPLIED ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE AND ROBOTICS 
9 
 
from the majority class, which can reduce bias toward normal traffic [47]. Oversampling methods, such as SMOTE 
(Synthetic Minority Over -sampling Technique), generate synthetic samples for minority classes by interpolating 
existing instances, improving representation in training data [46]. 
More sophisticated approaches have also emerged. PROVSYN (2025) is an automated framework that synthesizes 
provenance graphs using graph neural networks, large language models, and rule-based refinement. By generating 
realistic attack data, it balances nor mal and malicious samples, ensuring underrepresented attack types are 
adequately represented. This augmentation improves detection accuracy, reduces false positives, and enriches 
underrepresented labels, particularly in smaller datasets like Nodlink [43]. 
Self-supervised transformer-based contrastive learning techniques have also shown promise [44]. These models 
learn useful representations from both common and underrepresented classes, reducing reliance on imbalanced 
labeled data. Using packet -sequence and flow -statistics datasets, transformer encoders trained with contrastive 
learning can capture minority or rare patterns, improving generalization across diverse and imbalanced 
distributions. This approach leverages a BERT-based architecture and a contrastive learning process that maximizes 
similarity between related data points while minimizing it between dissimilar ones, ensuring that even rare attack 
patterns are effectively represented. 
Generative active adaptation methods, such as NetGuard [50], further address bias by combining density -aware 
dataset selection with conditional data augmentation. These frameworks help NIDS adapt to concept drift and class 
imbalance, generating more diverse data and enhancing model robustness for rare attacks like  web attacks and 
botnets. NetGuard identifies dataset priors and synthesizes new data conditioned on them, allowing models to 
generalize better when training and testing distributions differ over time. The framework has been tested on both 
simulated IDS data and real -world ISP datasets, de monstrating significant improvements in intrusion detection 
performance in dynamic environments while reducing labeling effort and adaptation costs. By prioritizing the most 
informative samples, NetGuard  also alleviates the challenges of uncertainty -based active learning, helping the 
system respond faster to evolving threats. 
Weaknesses 
Although the solutions provided can help mitigate Bias, limitations remain. Undersampling methods like RUS risk 
data loss in small datasets, potentially discarding important information and causing overfitting. Random selection 
may also remove critical pat terns, leading to inconsistent results. Oversampling techniques, such as SMOTE, may 
introduce noise and fail to capture complex data structures, sometimes amplifying borderline cases or class overlap. 
While PROVSYN overcomes some of these issues, it still approximates temporal modeling rather than explicitly 
representing timestamps, generates smaller-than-realistic graphs, and relies on sequential text attribute generation 
that limits efficiency in larger graphs. Its applicability to other domains or downst ream algorithms also remains 
underexplored. Generalization to real-world environments remains a challenge. Most solutions, including PROVSYN, 
SSL-based models, and NetGuard, are evaluated on specific benchmark datasets (e.g., CIDDS, NSL-KDD, Nodlink) 
that are either outdated or artificially balanced. Consequently, these models often fail to generalize to unseen 
networks or novel attack types. Additionally, bias mitigation mo dels often lack transparency and explainability. 
SSL and transformer -based IDS models, as well as PROVSYN, remain black boxes. They improve balance and 
detection accuracy but provide little insight into how minority classes are represented or how bias is r educed. This 
opacity limits trust and makes it difficult to identify hidden biases learned during pretext tasks [44] – [53]. 
Unsolved Problems / Research Gaps: 
Future work in this direction would involve the integration of Explainable AI (XAI) with imbalance -aware and 
generative techniques to provide informative and meaningful knowledge about the model's behavior, enabling 
security experts to know the features us ed by the SSL model to represent attacks. There is a lack of a framework 
to interpret and visualize the representations learned by the SSL model in the context of IDS. Moreover, the 
authenticity of the synthetic data used in the provenance needs to be vali dated, and metrics should be developed 
to assess the realism and diversity of the generated data [50]. Moreover, there is a need to develop self-evolving 
models that are capable of learning and adapting to concept drift and rebalancing the minority samples in near -
real-time, which is still an open problem in the field [52].  
All these issues are critical in the context of ensuring that the mitigation of biases in IDS systems is not only effective 
in improving the system's performance but also ensures the ethical and moral integrity of the system. 
 
4.3. Privacy and Data Protection 
Problem 
In the case of Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) using AI, there is a need to collect large amounts of data in the form 
of logs and telemetry data, including packet captures, flow records, and host logs, which may contain personal 
data. The use of IDS invo lves the analysis of packet headers and sometimes payload data, which is a form of 
surveillance and breach of user confidentiality. This involves the continuous monitoring of the network, the system, 
and the user. Since the Intrusion Detection System learn s patterns, large amounts of data collection are necessary. 
For the purpose of experiments, packet captures in the form of pcap, NetFlow, and benchmark data are used, and 
the model cannot work without data.  
However, there are some challenges that are associated with the use of data, as the amount of data collected can 

 
INTERNATIONAL JOURNAL OF APPLIED ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE AND ROBOTICS 
10 
 
rise to terabytes per day, thus posing a problem for storage. Network -based IDS use the flow of data passing 
through the network device to detect intrusions, while host -based IDS use the processes and files accessed on a 
single device to reveal patterns of user behavior. For better anomaly detection, the IDS have to use data from the 
kernel level and the network level, sometimes combining the data from the kernel level and the network level for 
better performance [57]. Deep learning -based methods, such as the Deep defense model, have been seen to 
produce better results than traditional machine learning methods, especially when the data set is large [58]. These 
methods use the data collected from the network, such as the data from the packets, to produce better performance, 
thus the suitability of the methods for use with the extensive data passing through the network 
Altogether, AI-based IDS rely on extensive data collection and deep system visibility, making privacy monitoring 
and processing challenging. Inspecting network traffic and file activity in detail can reveal patterns of user behavior, 
increasing privacy risks.  
Solutions 
One promising solution is Federated Learning (FL). Centralizing network data for AI model training can violate 
privacy regulations such as GDPR [59]. FL has emerged as a privacy-preserving approach that allows distributed 
model training across multiple IoT devices without centralizing data [29]. Devices train local models independently 
and share only model updates with a central server, minimizing privacy risks while enabling collaborative model 
learning. The central server then aggregates updates and redistributes them to participants. Hybrid cryptographic 
approaches further enhance privacy: for instance, homomorphic encryption (HE) can secure data offloaded to a 
central server, allowing the server to learn from encrypted data without accessing its content. This maintains 
confidentiality and improves efficiency in FL-based IDS, such as in IoV networks [30]. 
Another approach is Differential Privacy (DP). In federated learning settings, sharing model gradients or updates 
may expose sensitive data through gradient leakage or model inversion attacks [31]. DP is a mathematical privacy-
preserving technique that mitigates this by adding carefully calibrated noise to data or model updates before 
sharing, reducing the risk of reconstructing original data. It provides mathematical privacy guarantees, making it 
difficult for adversaries to in fer individual network traffic records or user behavior while enabling collaborative 
learning across organizations without exposing raw data. 
Weaknesses 
Although such advancements have been made, it is not entirely flawless, and this has led to ethical issues. When it 
comes to AI-based IDS, where gradients are computed based on sensitive network traffic logs, there is still room for 
privacy leakage, even if it is decentralized. Distributed training and collaborative learning are widely adopted in 
privacy-sensitive scenarios, where raw data never leaves local devices. In such systems, only gradients are 
exchanged, and it is commonly assumed that this protects participants’ training data. However, research challenges 
this assumption, demonstrating that gradients can leak sensitive information, which raises serious ethical questions 
about user consent and data confidentiality. The authors explicitly question whether gradients truly protect privacy 
and show that it is possible to completely reconstruct the original training data from shared gradients. They propose 
a novel attack called Deep Leakage from Gradients (DLG), an optimization-based method that reconstructs training 
inputs and labels using only the gradients. DLG works by optimizing dummy inputs and labels to minimize the 
distance between the dummy gradients and the original gradients. Remarkably, the authors can achieve pixel -
perfect images and token-perfect texts without any generative model or prior knowledge of the data. Experimental 
results show that the reconstructed samples are almost identical to the original training data, highlighting a critical 
ethical challenge: even sharing seemingly anonymized gradients can compromise user privacy [60]. 
Computation and performance limitations also pose ethical implications [61]. Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE), 
such as Gentry’s ideal lattice -based FHE [Gentry 2009], supports any number of computations over the data. 
However, the high computational overhead and mathematical complexity of FHE make its practical implementation 
challenging. This leads to ethical issues in terms of fairness, as some organizations cannot afford the required 
infrastructure for privacy. The bootstrapping operation is very expensive for FHE, though some leveled FHE provides 
a solution for improving performance. Researchers continue to explore somewhat homomorphic encryption (SWHE) 
schemes that balance security and practicality, but applying them in real-time or large-scale IDS scenarios remains 
an open problem. 
Additionally, privacy -preserving methods like Differential Privacy introduce a privacy –utility trade -off, which 
carries both technical and ethical implications [32].While adding noise to gradients or data helps protect sensitive 
information, overly conservative noise can significantly degrade model performance, potentially affecting the 
system’s reliability. Factors like batch size and noise scale ( σ) significantly affect the accuracy of the model. If not 
tuned properly, the accuracy of the model will be completely destroyed. From an ethical perspective, the designers 
of privacy-preserving IDS must balance the moral responsibility to protect sensitive user data with the need to 
maintain effective system performance, highlighting an ongoing tension between security, usability, and ethical 
accountability.

11 
 
Unsolved Problems / Research Gaps 
Practical, scalable integration of HE and FL for resource -limited IDS remains a challenge [62]. Existing solutions 
often suffer from high computational and communication overheads. Even optimized HE schemes require further 
validation to be feasible in real -world scenarios, such as real -time recommender systems. Communication costs 
remain high for large-scale applications. 
Another gap is robust privacy under non-IID data distributions [63]. the training data across clients is often not IID, 
resulting in the divergence of the model weights during the training of the FL models. Attackers can also extract 
sensitive information from the model parameters in such settings, thus the need for priva cy-preserving mechanisms 
that are robust even in the presence of non -IID data distributions. Finally, the importance of governance and 
compliance mapping cannot be overemphasized. The data minimization, purpose limitation, privacy by design, and 
risk management principles of the GDPR, CCPA, and NIST Privacy Framework are all relevant in the context of 
data privacy. The use of data sheets, model cards, and privacy budget ledgers provides a measure of 
accountability and transparency. While the use of FL is technically compliant with the regulations, the need for the 
use of DP and aggregation mechanisms to address the privacy risk further underscores the ethical responsibility to 
ensure enforceable, transparent, and robust protections in AI-based IDS deployments. 
5. Comparative Analysis of EU, U.S., and South Korea 
5.1. Transparency and Explainability in AI-Based Intrusion Detection Systems 
The European Union, the United States, and South Korea approach transparency in AI -based intrusion detection 
systems (IDS) differently, reflecting their distinct governance priorities and organizational philosophies.  
European Union: Explainability is considered a legal requirement. According to [64], transparency is considered a 
matter of fundamental rights under the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). There are strict documentation 
and risk assessment requirements for high -risk AI systems, such as those for IDS in CI [65]. Black box IDS models, 
which are unable to provide any kind of explanation, could potentially face issues in terms of compliance, 
particularly if decisions are automatically made and have implications for individual rights [66]. 
United States: Explainability is approached as a governance and risk management practice [67]. Unlike the EU, 
the emphasis is not on the management of operational risks but rather the enforcement of the rights-based approach. 
Therefore, even though NIST’s privacy guidelines emphasize the importance of transparency and accountability, 
they do not em phasize the importance of explainability. Thus, SHAP and LIME are best practices, but it is not 
necessarily driven by legal requirements. 
South Korea: The approach finds a delicate balance between data protection and national cybersecurity concerns. 
The Personal Information Protection Act (PIPA) has robust data protection principles, similar to the GDPR [68]. The 
requirements for AI explainability are not as strong, however [69]. The national cybersecurity strategy, which 
involves various actors such as the Korea Internet & Security Agency (KISA), prioritizes the technological security of 
the country and the swift deployment of AI [70]. Transparency is encouraged, but functionality is prioritized. 
Such differences give rise to asymmetric compliance pressures. For example, a black box IDS deployed in a U.S. 
enterprise may not comply with EU regulations for high -risk AI due to detailed auditing requirements, whereas 
explanation requirements that comply with EU regulations may not satisfy latency requirements in South Korea’s 
cybersecurity landscape. 
 
5.2. Privacy and Data Governance: Centralization vs. Federated Approaches 
AI-based intrusion detection systems rely heavily on access to large volumes of network and user data. How this 
data is collected, stored, and processed varies across regions, reflecting different regulatory philosophies and 
cybersecurity priorities  
European Union: GDPR principles, such as data minimization, limitation of purposes, and privacy by design, impose 
strong constraints on centralized data aggregation. Training AI models on raw network logs requires Data Protection 
Impact Assessments (DPIAs). Federated le arning (FL) architectures are better aligned with EU regulations, as they 
keep data local and limit raw data sharing [64].  
United States: Privacy rules are sector -specific rather than unified. Some industries, like healthcare and finance, 
have strict data -handling regulations, but cybersecurity applications allow more flexibility. Centralized data 
collection for IDS training is generally p ermitted if safeguards are in place. Federated learning is often adopted 
for efficiency or innovation rather than regulatory necessity [67]. 
South Korea: PIPA enforces strict consent and data protection rules. However, the country’s centralized digital 
infrastructure and government-driven cybersecurity efforts allow large-scale data processing for national defense 
if safeguards exist. Federated learning m ay be used to enhance trust and security, but decentralization 
requirements are not as strong as in the EU [70].  
Across all regions, federated learning alone does not eliminate privacy risks. Techniques like gradient leakage 
demonstrate that decentralizing data is not enough. Compliance depends on additional protections such as 
differential privacy or secure aggregation.

5.3. Bias, Fairness, and Operational Trade-Offs 
Ethical considerations in AI-based IDS vary significantly across regions, reflecting different regulatory frameworks, 
societal values, and operational priorities.  
European Union: Fairness and mitigation of risk are integral to the compliance measures for high-risk AI. PROVSYN 
or self-supervised learning can be employed for this [65].  
United States: Fairness in AI policy is gaining traction, but cybersecurity focuses more on the efficiency of operations. 
False positive reduction is viewed as an improvement to the effectiveness of the SOC rather than a user right [67] 
– [71].  
South Korea: AI guidelines emphasize trust and social responsibility, yet cybersecurity resilience is the top priority. 
Bias reduction in IDS is generally evaluated based on its impact on detection accuracy and national cyber defense 
[70].  
In practice, the EU may accept slightly lower detection accuracy to better protect user rights, while in the U.S. and 
South Korea, operational efficiency and detection effectiveness often take precedence over privacy or 
explainability concerns. 
 
6. Recommendations 
On the basis of the analysis provided within this study, some recommendations could be proposed to enhance 
the ethical design and deployment of AI-based intrusion detection systems.  Firstly, more emphasis should be given 
to the inclusion of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques within the IDS models. Increasing the 
transparency of the model could help security analysts to understand the decision process and detect possible errors 
within the detection process. Further research should focus on the development of efficient XAI techniques with the 
ability to provide explanations without affecting the overall computational cost. Second, the problem of dataset 
imbalance and bias should continue to receive significant attention in the field of IDS. Data generation techniques, 
data augmentation methods, and self -supervised learning methods can all contribute to the mitigation of the 
problem of dataset imbalance. On the other hand , evaluation standards need to be established to test the 
effectiveness of fairness mitigation techniques in intrusion detection systems.   
Third, privacy-preserving techniques need to be incorporated in the training of AI models to handle network 
data. Techniques like federated learning, differential privacy, and secure aggregation can minimize the need for 
data collection in the training of AI models. These techniques need to be fine -tuned to minimize the computational 
overhead in the training of AI models. Fourth, organizations need to develop a set of governance frameworks that 
support the alignment of the deployment of AI-based IDS with ethical and regulatory requirements. Documentation 
methods like model cards, data sheets, and risk reports can be useful. Moreover, the implementation of international 
privacy regulations should be integrated into the design phase of AI systems rather than the post-deployment phase.  
Lastly, the collaboration of the academic community with the industry and the government is a key factor to develop 
standardized benchmarks for the evaluation of AI -based IDS to ensure that the AI systems deployed for intrusion 
detection achieve high performance with ethical considerations. 
 
7. Conclusion 
Artificial intelligence has helped intrusion detection systems to make them quicker and better at recognizing complex 
cyber intrusions. Machine learning and deep learning techniques have helped intrusion detection systems to better 
analyze the data from the network and recognize patterns. With the increasing sophistication of cyberattacks, AI -
based intrusion detection systems have started to play an important part in the cybersecurity of organizations. 
However, the use of AI in intrusion detection systems has raised various ethical issues. Some of the issues include 
limited transparency, bias in the data used for training the AI model, and the use of collected data for breaching 
privacy. These issues have raised questions of trust in the use of AI in intrusion detection systems. In addition, different 
parts of the globe may have different strategies for the governance of AI. For example, the European Union may 
place more importance on the aspect of privacy and transparency, whereas the USA, as well as South Korea, may 
place more importance on the aspect of operational efficiency, as well as cyber security resilience. In the future, it 
is important to work towards enhancing the aspect of transparency, fairness, and privacy, as well as detection. This 
will require the efforts of different parties, including researchers, policymakers, as well as the industry, to develop 
effective intrusion detection systems based on artificial intelligence.
