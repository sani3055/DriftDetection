Abstract 
Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has demonstrated significant potential in autonomous network defense; 
however, existing models frequently suffer from "Recall Collapse" when faced with concept drift or the "Information 
Poverty" of resource-constrained edge devices. This paper proposes REACT-D3QN (Resilient Adaptive Concept-drift-
aware Dueling Double Deep Q-Network), a framework designed to maintain operationally relevant detection integrity 
during cross-dataset migration. By integrating a Dueling architecture, the framework decouples state-value estima-
tion from action advantages, allowing the agent to recognize the "intrinsic risk" of network states even under extreme 
feature pruning. A core innovation of this work is the REACT reward engine, which utilizes a 10:1 asymmetric penalty 
ratio to establish a "Recall Floor" , prioritizing the detection of drifted attack signatures over simple accuracy. Evalu-
ated through a zero-base transfer learning transition from CIC-IDS2017 to CIC-IDS2018 traffic patterns, REACT-D3QN 
achieved a 99.85% Recall while operating on an 84.4% reduced feature set (5 features). These results prove that archi-
tectural resilience can compensate for significant signal loss, providing a robust pathway for deploying high-perfor-
mance Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) on low-power edge infrastructure and setting the stage for decentralized, 
blockchain-verified autonomous defense.
Keywords Deep Reinforcement Learning, Dueling Double Deep Q-Network, Intrusion Detection Systems, 
Concept Drift, Transfer Learning, Cost-Sensitive Learning, Asymmetric Reward Shaping, Information Poverty, Feature 
Dimensionality Reduction, Edge Computing Security, Behavioral Adaptation
Introduction
The growing complexity of modern software systems 
and the emergence of diverse platforms have created 
vast exploit surfaces, making vulnerability identification 
increasingly difficult and costly (Sahingoz et  al. 2021). 
Effective risk management requires reliable Intrusion 
Detection Systems (IDS) capable of continuous network 
analysis and automated protective response (Kim et  al. 
2020).
Traditional signature-based methods are limited 
by their inability to detect novel or zero-day attacks 
(Abdulrahman and Ibrahem 2020; Zhong et  al. 2024; 
Bu and Wang 2019). Consequently, AI-driven solu -
tions–particularly Reinforcement Learning (RL)–have 
become the standard for proactive defense (Hirchoua 
et  al. 2024; Nayak et  al. 2023; Tellache et  al. 2024; Aza -
deh 2024; Taveras Cruz et al. 2025). By interacting with 
the network environment, RL agents learn to distinguish 
between benign and malicious actions, providing the 
adaptability needed to counter polymorphic malware

and living-off-the-land tactics (Wu et al. 2024; Adawad-

Deep RL (DRL) further enhances this by processing high-
dimensional traffic features to maximize a long-term 
reward signal, addressing the limitations of static data -

Double Deep Q-Network). This framework is specifically 
engineered to maintain operationally relevant detec -

environmental state-values from specific action advan -

pattern matching, REACT-D3QN establishes a robust 
pathway for autonomous defense on low-power edge 
infrastructure where traditional models typically fail.
Overall, the main contributions of this paper are:
• The REACT Mechanism: An asymmetric 10:1 risk-
aware penalty that anchors detection by prioritizing 
the cost of False Negatives (missed attacks) to main -

• Resilient Architecture: Implementation of a Dueling

• Knowledge Persistence: Successful Transfer Learning

• Operational Efficiency: Our model achieves an 84.4% 
reduction in feature dimensionality (from 32 to 5 fea-
tures) while maintaining a high 99.85% Recall, prov -
ing suitability for high-speed edge deployment.

Related work
This section situates REACT-D3QN within the broader 
landscape of autonomous cyber-defense. The integration 
of Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) has emerged as a 
transformative approach to addressing the dynamic and 
decentralized nature of modern cybersecurity threats. 
We explore existing literature across several dimensions: 
from adaptive protection in critical infrastructures–such 
as microgrids and power systems–to sophisticated intru -
sion detection systems (IDS) for the Industrial IoT and 
vehicular networks.
Furthermore, this section examines critical and techni -
cal advancements in training stability, such as the miti -
gation of overestimation bias, and addresses emerging 
safety challenges through dynamic shielding. By synthe -
sizing these diverse research directions, we establish the 
context for our framework and identify current research 
gaps–specifically the lack of resilient behavioral adapta -
tion when agents are faced with concept drift between 
datasets and severe feature constraints.
Multi‑agent architectures and decentralized coordination
A fundamental challenge in modern network security is 
the requirement for decentralized coordination in large-
scale environments. Taveras Cruz et al. (2025) performed 
a systematic review of over 150 studies, identifying MAS 
and DRL as essential components for the adaptive pro -
tection of critical cyber-physical systems. This architec -
tural shift is necessitated by the inherent non-stationarity 
and high dimensionality of modern networks (Azadeh 
2024). Tellache et al. (2024) optimized MAS for intrusion 
detection by employing cost-sensitive learning to address 
inherent class imbalances. To enhance scalability, special-
ized frameworks such as InforMARL, Nayak et al. (2023) 
utilized graph neural networks to aggregate local neigh -
borhood information, allowing decentralized agents to 
compute paths and actions effectively even as the num -
ber of network nodes grows. Table  1 highlights the MAS 
and Decentralized Coordination works.
Algorithmic advancements in value‑based learning
The stability of DRL agents is often undermined by over -
estimation bias in value-function approximation. Recent 
literature has focused on mathematical refinements to 
ensure convergence and policy optimality (Hirchoua 
et  al. 2022). Double Deep Q-Networks (DDQN) have 
been proven effective in capturing complex temporal 
dependencies in attack patterns within power systems 
(Zeng et al. 2023) while mitigating overestimation (Naga-
rajan et al. 2025). Beyond standard DDQN, Mutlu et al. 
(2024) introduced a parameter-free approach to reduce 
the underestimation bias in deterministic policy gra -
dients, while Tian et  al. (2022) proposed the Dynamic 
Partial Average (DPAV) estimator to stabilize learning in 
dialogue-based policy modules. Furthermore, multi-stage 
training strategies (Yang et al. 2022) and dueling architec-
tures (Tian et al. 2022) have been integrated to improve

Reward specification and training efficiency
Cybersecurity environments present a unique challenge 
where the cost of a False Negative (FN)–a missed attack–
far outweighs the cost of a False Positive (FP). Bates and 
Mavroudis (Bates et al. 2023) demonstrated that autono -
mous cyber-agents are highly sensitive to the magnitude 
and relative size of penalties, suggesting that traditional, 
symmetric reward structures often lead to suboptimal 
defense policies. Although curiosity-driven models (Cao 
et al. 2024) and expert demonstrations (Pettersson et al. 
2024) have been proposed to solve sparse reward issues 
and accelerate convergence, they do not inherently 
address the risk-asymmetry required for operationally 
relevant intrusion detection. A significant gap remains 
in Cost-Sensitive Reinforcement Learning, particularly 
regarding how extreme penalty ratios can be used to 
anchor model performance during domain migration. 
Existing reward shaping methods (Roy 2024) often seek 
to balance multiple objectives but rarely focus on using 
asymmetric penalties as a stabilization anchor for Recall 
when faced with concept drift. Our proposed REACT-
D3QN framework addresses this by implementing a 10:1 
risk-aware penalty, moving beyond simple efficiency 
toward a safety-first reward architecture. Table  3 pre -
sents the comparison of different works related to reward 
design and cost-sensitivity.

Adversarial resilience and robustness
As AI-driven detectors become more prevalent, the 
emergence of adversarial AI poses a significant threat. 
Saini and Chennamaneni (2024) highlight the vulner -
ability of the network IDS to data poisoning and test-time 
evasion. For instance, Chathoth and Lee (2025) demon -
strate that poisoning as little as 1% of a training dataset 
can effectively backdoor a model. Frameworks such as 
UNIDOOR (Ma et  al. 2025) and Deep PackGen (Hore 
et al. 2025) illustrate how attackers can use DRL to gen -
erate adversarial packets that evade modern detectors. 
To counter these threats, research has shifted towards 
Dynamic Shielding (Xiao et  al. 2023), which monitors 
and corrects agent behavior in real-time, and out-of-
distribution (OOD) dynamics detection (Gardille and 
Ahmad 2023) to identify anomalous behavior during 
deployment. Furthermore, techniques such as Condi -
tional GANs (Barkah et  al. 2023) are employed to syn -
thesize minority attack data, ensuring that the resulting 
IDS is robust against highly imbalanced traffic patterns. 
Table  5 highlights the comparison between different 
adversarial resilience and robustness works.
Architectural evolution: towards REACT‑D3QN resilience
While standard Double Deep Q-Networks (DDQN) are 
effective at mitigating overestimation bias (Nagarajan 
et al. 2025), they often struggle to maintain policy stabil -
ity when input features are significantly pruned or when 
the data distribution shifts. To address the requirements 
of Cross-Dataset generalization between CIC-IDS2017 
and CIC-IDS2018, the framework was evolved into 
REACT-D3QN (Resilient Adaptive Concept-drift-aware 
Dueling Double Deep Q-Network). The core innovation 
of REACT-D3QN lies in the integration of the Dueling 
architecture, which decouples the estimation of the state-
value V(s) from the advantage A(s,  a). This structural 
refinement is critical for"Information Poverty scenarios, 
such as our 5-feature restricted track. By isolating V(s), 
the agent learns to recognize the intrinsic risk of a state 
even when the specific action signals are weakened by the 
84.4% reduction in feature dimensionality. Furthermore, 
the synchronization provided by Soft Target Updates 
( τ ) ensures that the aggressive 10:1 risk-aware penalty 
anchors the model’s Recall without causing the weight 
instability often observed in high-penalty reinforcement 
learning environments.
Despite the proliferation of DRL-based IDS solutions, 
a significant gap remains in behavioral Transfer Learning 
and domain adaptation. As summarized in the preced -
ing sections, current methodologies often treat domain 
migration as a static mathematical mapping or require 
full-scale retraining when faced with new attack signa -
tures (Varghese and Taghiyarrenani 2025). There is a 
profound lack of research into how an autonomous agent 
can perform Zero-Base Transfer Learning–effectively 
transposing security intuition from one dataset iteration 
(CIC-IDS2017) to another (CIC-IDS2018) while simulta -
neously undergoing extreme feature pruning.
Existing models frequently suffer from Catastrophic 
Forgetting or a total Recall collapse when encounter -
ing the concept drift inherent in 2018 traffic patterns. 
REACT-D3QN bridges this gap by replacing rigid statis -
tical alignment with a dynamic, transfer-oriented adapta-
tion mechanism. By combining the structural resilience 
of Dueling streams with a cost-sensitive 10:1 reward 
anchor, this framework demonstrates that learned behav-
ioral patterns from 2017 can be successfully transferred 
to a 2018 environment. This achieves an 84.4% reduction 
in data overhead while preserving a Recall of 99.85%, sig -
nificantly mitigating the trade-off between cross-dataset 
knowledge persistence and operational efficiency.

The main objective of Reinforcement Learning (RL) 
is to train an agent to make decisions while interacting 
with an environment. An agent tries to maximize a total 
expected reward by taking the best actions over time. 
It is mostly based on trial-and-error mechanisms. The 
agent explores the environment and learns which actions 
give the best outcomes, and it continues to learn better 
actions as times goes on. The agent does not even need to 
be told what actions to take; it learns its actions based on 
feedback (rewards or punishments).
Markov decision processes (MDPs)
A Markov Decision Process (MDP) is a formal frame -
work for decision-making problems where outcomes are 
partly random and partly controlled by a decision-maker. 
It is defined as 5-tuple:< S, A, P, R, γ>  , where: 
1. State Space S: a set of all possible states of the envi -
ronment. Each state represents a specific situation 
the agent can be in.
2. Action Space A: a set of all possible actions the agent 
can take. The available actions depend on the current 
state.
3. Transition Probability P(s′|s, a): it is the probability 
distribution representing the likelihood of transition-
ing from state s to a new state s’ given that action a 
was taken. Formally: 
• This satisfies the Markov property, meaning that 
the next state depends only on the current state and 
action, not on the history.
4.  Reward Function R(s, a): A mapping that assigns a 
numerical reward r ∈ R for taking action a in state s: 
5.  Discount Factor γ : A scalar 0 ≤ γ< 1 determining 
the importance of future rewards. Smaller values pri -
oritize immediate rewards, while values closer to 1 
emphasize long-term rewards.
In the context of NIDS, the state S represents the high-
dimensional flow features (e.g., CIC-IDS2017/2018), 
and the action A corresponds to the binary classification 
(Benign vs. Malicious).

Double deep Q‑network
In conventional Deep Q-Networks (DQNs), the same 
network is employed for action selection (max) and eval -
uation, which can result in an overestimation bias from 
the network overestimating the Q-values due to noise or 
approximation error. Double Q-learning was proposed to 
address this issue (figure  1). It separates the action selec -
tion and evaluation steps, reducing the overestimation 
bias. The action selection uses one Q-function to choose 
the best action. While the evaluation uses a separate 
Q-function to evaluate that action.
Double DQN applies the Double Q-learning idea to 
the DQN framework (Van  Hasselt et  al. 2016). Instead 
of using the same network for both action selection and 
evaluation, it uses the current/online Q-network ( Q ) for 
selecting the action, and the target network ( Q− ) for eval-
uating the action.

Phase I: network intrusion detection as a Markov decision 
process: the source dueling DQN agent
Before introducing our adaptive framework, we establish 
a baseline using a standard Dueling Deep Q-Network. 
In this phase, the agent is trained exclusively on CIC-
IDS2017 to acquire Source Domain Expertise.

• State Space (S): A state st ∈ S at time step t is repre -

Phase II: the concept drift challenge: performance 
degradation under evolving threat landscapes
This phase investigates the limitations of static detection 
models when faced with the temporal evolution of net -
work traffic and the emergence of new cyber-threats.
To assess the generalization of the source Dueling 
DQN agent, we subjected the pre-trained models (both 
the 32-feature and 5-feature configurations) to a direct 
evaluation on the CIC-IDS2018 dataset. This test was 
conducted in a Zero-Shot manner, meaning that agents 
were required to classify traffic from a newer environ -
ment without any prior exposure or fine-tuning. In this 
study, the term zero-base transfer learning is used to 
describe the subsequent adaptation phase, in which the 
target-domain training is initialized without replaying 
source-domain samples, while preserving the learned 
policy parameters from the source environment. The goal 
was to determine whether the signatures learned from 
the 2017 baseline remained valid across different network 
infrastructures and time-frames.
The results of this direct evaluation revealed a signifi -
cant performance collapse, characterized by a drastic 
reduction in the recall and the F1-Score. Our analysis 
identifies two primary causes for this failure, collectively 
known as Concept Drift:
• Feature Distribution Shift: The statistical proper -
ties of network metrics, such as flow duration and 
inter-arrival times, underwent fundamental changes 
between the 2017 and 2018 datasets. This shift ren -
dered the decision boundaries learned by the agent in 
Phase 1 obsolete.
• Evolving Attack Signatures: The 2018 dataset intro-
duced sophisticated attack patterns (e.g., modern 
DDoS and Brute Force variations) that were not pre -
sent in the 2017 training set. The models, particularly 
the high-dimensional 32-feature version, exhibited a 
generalization gap, where they were over-specialized 
to 2017 signatures and failed to recognize the new 
behavioral traits of 2018 threats.
The failure of direct evaluation confirms that even a 
robust RL model cannot remain effective in a dynamic 
cybersecurity landscape if it remains static. Relying solely 
on historical data leads to rapid obsolescence of the 
NIDS. Consequently, this phase concludes that a simple 
training-test approach is insufficient for modern threats, 
establishing a clear necessity for an adaptive Transfer 
Learning strategy to bridge the knowledge gap between 
the source domain (2017) and the target domain (2018).
Phase III: the REACT‑D3QN framework – adaptive transfer 
learning and design
Phase III represents the core contribution of this study: 
the deployment of the REACT-D3QN (Resilient Efficient 
Adaptive Concept-drift-aware Transfer Dueling Double 
Deep Q-Network). This phase is specifically engineered 
to bridge the performance gap between the CICIDS-2017 
and CICIDS-2018 datasets by employing transfer learn -
ing and architectural resilience to combat concept drift.
Architectural foundation: D3QN with lambda aggregation
To address the instability and overestimation bias com -
mon in standard reinforcement learning, we propose 
a Dueling Double DQN architecture. The fundamen -
tal innovation lies in the separation of the network into 
two streams: one estimating the state-value function 
V(s) and the other estimating the Advantage function 
A(s,  a). These streams are combined through a special -
ized Lambda aggregation layer using the mapping func -
tion presented in Eq. 5. This structure allows the agent to 
recognize the intrinsic risk of a network state even when 
moving into the information poverty of the 5-feature 
track. To ensure knowledge persistence during the tran -
sition from 2017 to 2018, we utilize Soft Target Updates 
(Eq.  6) with a smoothing factor of τ = 0.005 , prevent-
ing the catastrophic forgetting of established threat 
signatures.
Cross‑dataset alignment and pre‑processing
A critical challenge in transfer learning is the struc -
tural evolution of the network environment. Significant 
shifts occurred in both feature labels and their statistical

Multi‑granularity state space (32 vs. 5 features)
To evaluate the framework’s resilience under varying lev-

• Comprehensive Track ( n = 32): Utilizes the full

The asymmetric REACT reward engine (10:1 Ratio)
The proposed REACT mechanism is defined by its 
cost-sensitive reward structure. Unlike traditional 
symmetric rewards, we implement an asymmetric 
10:1 penalty ratio to anchor the detection capabili -
ties of the model. The agent receives a reward of +1.0 
for correct classifications and a minor penalty of −1.0 
for false positives. However, a severe penalty of −10.0 
is applied to false negatives (missed attacks). This 
aggressive penalty forces the agent to prioritize recall 
during domain migration, ensuring that even if attack 
signatures drift in the 2018 dataset, the agent main -
tains a safety-first posture that minimizes the risk of 
system compromise.
Dynamic adaptation and training logic
Upon transition to the 2018 dataset, the exploration 
rate is reset to ǫ = 0.4 , allowing the agent to actively 
explore the new distribution while still leveraging its 
2017 weights. We employ Huber Loss for error calcu -
lation, which provides robustness against the outliers 
typically found in drifted network traffic. The training 
is supported by an Experience Replay buffer of 20,000 
transitions, allowing the agent to break temporal cor -
relations and learn from a balanced mix of historical 
and real-time environmental interactions.

Agent implementation details
The transition from a standard trainer to an adaptive 
framework involves a specific architectural shift.
Base dueling architecture
The agent’s internal structure is a direct implementation 
of the Dueling architecture formulated in Sect.  4.3.1. The 
two streams–value V (s) and advantage A (s,  a) functions–
are processed through dense layers with ReLU activation 
before being merged via a custom Lambda layer. This spe-
cific implementation choice allows the framework to center 
the advantage stream in real-time during the forward pass, 
effectively decoupling the state-value from the action-
advantage as required by the REACT-D3QN design.
REACT‑D3QN
During the adaptation phase (phase III), the agent is 
upgraded from a standard Dueling DQN to the D3QN 
variant to handle the volatility of drifted 2018 traf -
fic. Instead of the abrupt Hard Updates used in phase 
I, the synchronization of the target network is gov -
erned by the soft update logic described in Sect.  4.3.1. 
In this setup, the target weights are smoothed after 
every 5 optimization steps using the hyperparam -
eter τ = 0.005 . This implementation ensures that the 
agent’s Target Q-values remain stable, preventing the 
divergent gradients that typically occur when a model 
trained on 2017 signatures is suddenly exposed to the 
shifted feature distributions of the 2018 dataset.

measure the framework’s resilience: 
1. Recall (Detection Rate): The primary target for our 
10:1 penalty logic. 
2. Precision: Measuring the impact of False Positives. 
3. F1‑Score: The benchmark for overall framework 
effectiveness

Conclusion on the REACT mechanism
These results confirm that the REACT framework 
(D3QN + 10:1 Penalty) is an highly robust solution 
for Intrusion Detection. By training separate models 
and achieving nearly identical Recall results (99.90% vs 
99.85%), we demonstrate that the Risk-Aware logic is the 
dominant factor in performance–not the number of fea -
tures. This allows for a flexible security posture: using 32 
features for maximum precision or 5 features for high-
speed edge deployment, without ever compromising the 
safety of the network.
Resource efficiency and operational sustainability
The convergence of the 32-feature and 5-feature results 
reveals a significant breakthrough in operational effi -
ciency. By reducing the feature space from 32 to 5, the 
model achieves an 84.4% reduction in data dimensional -
ity and computational overhead. Table  14 highlights the 
discovered results. The final takeaways are:
• Computational ROI: The 5-feature model maintains 
99.95% of the detection efficacy (Recall) of the full-
suite model while requiring a fraction of the memory 
and processing power.
• Latency Minimization: In high-speed network envi -
ronments, the ability to make a safety-first decision 
based on only 5 indicators allows for significantly 
lower inference latency. This is crucial for prevent -
ing Zero-Day attacks where reaction speed is the pri -
mary defense.
• Edge Deployment Suitability:  Because the REACT 
mechanism enables high performance without full-
scale retraining or high-dimensional input, it is per -
fectly suited for resource-constrained environments 
such as IoT gateways, edge routers, and mobile secu -
rity nodes.
The data empirically demonstrates that the REACT-
D3QN framework creates a Recall Floor that prevents 
performance collapse across datasets. Whether operating 
with the full complexity of 32 features or the extreme effi-
ciency of 5, the 10:1 risk-aware penalty ensures that the 
D3QN agent remains a reliable sentinel, capable of trans-
ferring its intelligence across time (2017 to 2018) and 
across architectural constraints.
Research limitations
Despite the high performance of REACT-D3QN, certain 
limitations must be acknowledged. First, the 10:1 asym -
metric reward ratio was determined through empiri -
cal testing for the CIC-IDS environment; in real-world 
scenarios with highly volatile traffic, this fixed ratio may 
require dynamic tuning to prevent potential increases in 
False Positives. Second, while the study utilizes industry-
standard synthetic datasets, they may not fully capture 
the chaotic packet loss and jitter present in live, wild net -
work traffic. Finally, the adaptation gap observed during 
the initial phase of transfer learning–driven by the explo -
ration rate ( ǫ = 0.4)–implies that the system may require 
a brief stabilization period when first migrating to a new 
network domain.

Conclusion and future works
In this paper, we have addressed the critical challenge of 
maintaining intrusion detection integrity during domain 
migration and feature suppression. Through the develop-
ment of the REACT-D3QN framework, we demonstrated 
that the instability typically associated with cross-dataset 
transfer learning–caused by the concept drift between 
CIC-IDS2017 and 2018–can be successfully mitigated 
through deliberate architectural and reward design.
The experimental results confirmed our primary 
hypotheses: first, that a Dueling architecture allows an 
agent to retain security intuition by isolating the invari -
ant value of network states; and second, that an asym -
metric 10:1 penalty ratio successfully anchors Recall, 
preventing the catastrophic forgetting of attack classes 
during adaptation. By achieving a 99.85% Recall with only 
5 features, this work establishes that autonomous agents 
can maintain high-level security postures even in infor -
mation poverty scenarios, making them suitable for the 
next generation of decentralized, edge-deployed cyberse -
curity architectures.
Future research will focus on evolving the REACT 
mechanism into a Dynamic Penalty Scaler, where the 
10:1 ratio is automatically adjusted based on real-time 
uncertainty metrics. Additionally, we intend to evaluate 
the framework’s resilience against Adversarial Backdoor 
Attacks, exploring whether the intrinsic risk learned 
by the Value stream can detect maliciously poisoned 
gradients. A primary direction for future expansion is 
the integration of Blockchain Technology to facilitate 
decentralized, tamper-proof threat intelligence sharing. 
By utilizing Distributed Ledger Technology (DLT) and 
Smart Contracts, we aim to create a global, immutable 
registry of detected attack vectors. This would enable 
Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) to coordinate defense strat -
egies trustlessly, ensuring that reward signals and model 
updates remain resilient against manipulation or Man-
in-the-Middle attacks on the agent’s knowledge base.
