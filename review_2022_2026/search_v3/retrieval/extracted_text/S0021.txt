Abstract
Over the years, cyber-attacks have increased drastically, and their execution changed with time. One of the targets of cyber
criminals is trying to obtain sensitive information from mobile, cloud, or generally IoT devices. To avoid those risks, different
countermeasures have been developed and implemented. For instance, the IEC 60870-5-104 protocol was developed to deﬁne
the systems used for remote control in electrical engineering and power system automation applications. Starting from these
considerations, in this paper, we propose an intrusion detector based on explainable Deep Learning (DL) that is able to
detect possible attacks. In a nutshell, we consider several DL models, i.e., AlexNet, DenseNet, EfﬁcientNet, Inception, LeNet,
MobileNet, ResNet50, Standard CNN, VGG16, and VGG19 to understand whether a network trace (stored in a PCAP ﬁle) is
related to an attack. Moreover, to explain of the model attack prediction, we resort to two different Class Activation Mapping
algorithms available in the literature: Grad-CAM++ and Score-CAM. As the last step, we also calculated the IF/IM-SSIM
index to strengthen the robustness of the top-performing model and evaluate the similarity between the two CAM algorithms.
Experimental results show the effectiveness of the proposed method, and we obtained an accuracy equal to 0.900 with the
DenseNet. In conclusion, we applied the exact steps to a new dataset to conﬁrm that the proposed methodology is scalable
and applicable to other datasets and achieved promising results.

critical data in sensors to damage the principal elements of the
cybersecurity i.e., Conﬁdentiality, Integrity, and Availability
(CIA) [3].
In this paper we propose an intrusion detector based on
Deep Learning (DL) to avoid the possible threat above men-
tioned. For this purpose, we train and evaluate different DL
architectures (i.e., AlexNet, DenseNet, EfﬁcientNet, Incep-
tion, LeNet, MobileNet, ResNet50, Standard CNN, VGG16,
and VGG19) , employing two datasets published on the
web composed of PCAP (Packet Capture) ﬁles. In detail,
the ﬁrst employed dataset , named "IEC 60870-5-104 Intru-
sion Detection Dataset", was generated by simulating using
the IEC TestServer and validated with two real Remote Ter-
minal Unit (RTU) devices. The second dataset, called "TII
SSRC 23", contains less data if compared to the ﬁrst one and
was used to verify the scalability of the proposed method.
Both datasets are composed of captured packet that we con-
verted into images using a Python script written by the
authors. At the end of the training and test phases, we also
applied two different CAM algorithmsi.e., GradCAM++ and
ScoreCAM to highlight which areas of an image are most dis-
tinctive for model prediction. In addition, we also computed
the similarity between the obtained images by employing
the IF/IM-SSIM (Integrated Framework for Image Quality
Assessment) index. The main contribution of this manuscript
lies in leveraging explainability to develop a model that
provides clear explanations for classifying a sample into a
speciﬁc category. Additionally, to enhance the robustness of
the best-performing model and assess the similarity between
the two CAM algorithms, we evaluated the index using the
IF/IM-SSIM index on RGB images. To the best of our knowl-
edge, this article is the ﬁrst to propose a smart grid anomaly
detection method that also considers robustness.
The paper proceeds as follows: in the next section we
report the current state-of-art literature about the intrusion

are introduced in Sect. 3, while Sect. 4 presents the results
of the experimental analysis. Finally, in the last section, con-
clusion and future research plan are presented.

2.2 Intrusion detection
Researchers in [ 7] presented a framework to detect injected
data in a smart grid. In their study, researchers meticu-
lously utilized a Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and
a Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) network, achieving
an impressive accuracy exceeding 0.900. This robust per-
formance convincingly demonstrates the applicability and
effectiveness of Deep Learning techniques within intrusion
detection. We didn’t use the LSTM network differently from
them, but we focused on the different state-of-the-art Deep
Learning architectures, applying the best two different CAM
algorithms.
In [ 8], researchers illustrate a Machine Learning-based
intrusion detection system able to prevent with an accuracy
of 0.995 possible cyber-attacks in the smart-grid ﬁeld. In
detail, the authors employed a dataset (148,481 samples)
composed of scanning, buffer overﬂow, denial of service, and
other attacks. Distinct from them, even though we reached
a lower accuracy value, we also proposed using of explain-
ability, aiming to understand which areas of the images are
more relevant to obtain a certain prediction. In addition, we
employed the IF/IM-SSIM index to calculate the similarity
index between the CAM algorithms.
Authors in [ 9] proposed a novel intrusion detection
method that combines XGBoost and LSTM. To optimize
the methodology, Song et al. introduced a Bayesian-based
method to optimize the parameters of XGBoost and a
crossover scheme in a genetic algorithm (GA) to reduce
the impact of falling into a local optimum of Bayesian opti-
mization. Different from them, which utilized a big dataset
composed of 140,000 samples, obtaining 0.892 in accuracy,
we employed a small dataset with an accuracy value of 0.870.
In [ 10] proposed a method to detect cyberattacks in
power networks, ensuring data privacy. In detail, the pro-
cess detects malicious behaviors to prevent or mitigate
damage and identiﬁes speciﬁc attack types, achieving inter-
esting results in accuracy. Moreover, the presented method
ensures data integrity and conﬁdentiality, addressing con-
cerns about sharing sensitive information during detection.
Different from them, in our proposed method, we obtained
images from a PCAP ﬁle using a Python tool written by

3 The method
This Section provides an overview of the proposed method
to identify attacks and warnings to exploit dangerous actions
against the smart grid. In the proposed method, we converted
PCAP ﬁles belonging to the attack and possible attacks. After
this phase, we trained and tested ten Convolutional Neural
Networks to classify the images obtained and to identify the
areas of the image responsible for a speciﬁc prediction using
two different CAM algorithms. As a ﬁnal step, we re-ran all
the steps using a different dataset to validate the process and
conﬁrm the scalability of the approach. Figure 1 illustrates
the proposed method.

• AlexNet: In [19], authors presented an architecture com-
posed of eight layers. In detail, ﬁve consist of the

ers, while the remaining three and the output layer use a
Relu activation. The latter was employed to increase the
training process speed to avoid the gradient fading away
from the problem and have high calculation efﬁciency.
• DenseNet: was introduced in 2018 and stands for
Densely Connected Convolutional Networks. This archi-
tecture links each layer to every other layer in a feed-
forward fashion and is employed in different ﬁelds
like image classiﬁcation, object detection, and seman-
tic segmentation. Over the years, have been released
several versions of this architecture i.e., DenseNet-121,
DenseNet-169, DenseNet-201, and DenseNet-264. The
main difference between these versions is the number of
layers and parameters.
• EfﬁcientNet: designed by Google in [ 20], this archi-
tecture achieves high-performance accuracy with sig-
niﬁcantly fewer parameters and FLOPs. EfﬁcientNet is
widely adopted in ﬁelds like image classiﬁcation, object
detection, and transfer learning tasks. The architecture
introduces a novel scaling method, known as compound
scaling, to uniformly scale network dimensions, leading
to better accuracy and efﬁciency.
• Inception: Inception v3 is a Convolutional Neural Net-
work architecture introduced by researchers at Google in
2016 [ 21]. Several ﬁelds, such as image classiﬁcation,
object detection, and feature extraction adopt Inception
architecture. Moreover, it uses Inception modules for
capturing features at different scales, employs factorized
convolutions for efﬁciency, includes auxiliary classiﬁers
to aid training stability, incorporates batch normalization
for faster training, and is often used in transfer learning
with pre-trained models on large datasets like ImageNet.
• LeNet: This architecture was presented for the ﬁrst time
in 1998 in [ 22] and represents one of the earliest Con-
volutional Neural Networks. The latter was developed to
classify handwritten digits while currently employed to
perform simple tasks. This network consists of two con-
volution layers, two sub-sampling levels, two fully linked
layers, and an output layer with a Gaussian connection.
• MobileNet: is based on depthwise separable convolu-
tions, with a single ﬁlter applied to each input channel,
and was Google introduced in 2017 [ 23]. This intro-
duction helped solve several problems, such as the
computation and model size. In detail, the input is split
into two layers: one is aimed to ﬁlter and separate, while
the second allows the creation of a linear combination of
the output of the depthwise layer.
• ResNet50: was introduced in 2016 by the research group
of Microsoft [ 24]. ResNet50, where 50 is the number
of layers that compose it, consists of a stack of residual
blocks, each containing multiple convolutional layers. In
addition, this architecture includes global average pool-
ing and fully connected layers for classiﬁcation. The main
innovation proposed by ResNet50 is the introduction of
residual learning i.e., a technique to train deep neural
networks.
• Standard CNN : Different Convolutional Neural Net-
works have been presented in the state-of-the-art since
their introduction. All of them are based on the same prin-

3.3.2 Explainability
In the ﬁnal stage of the proposed method, we employed two
distinct Class Activation Mapping algorithms i.e., Gradient-
weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) and Score-
weighted Class Activation Mapping (Score-CAM).
Selvaraju et al. introduced the Grad-CAM algorithm in
[26], which uses gradients of the output class concern-
ing convolutional feature maps to understand how each
feature map contributes to the model’s prediction. Grad-
CAM computes the gradients of the target class concerning
the feature maps from the last convolutional layer. These
gradients indicate the importance of each feature map in
predicting the target class. By averaging these gradients
across the feature maps, weights are assigned to each fea-
ture map channel. A weighted sum of the feature maps is
then computed, followed by a ReLU operation to retain only
positive contributions, resulting in a heatmap highlighting
the regions most important for the model’s decision. The
other CAM algorithm employed is Score-weighted Class
Activation Mapping, introduced in 2020 [27]. This algorithm
represents an improvement of Grad-CAM and allows for
addressing some limitations, particularly the reliance on gra-
dients. In fact, Score-CAM generates class activation maps
by leveraging the output scores of a CNN rather than gradi-
ents. Moreover, the weighted feature maps are combined to
highlight regions important for the prediction without relying
on gradients. Despite Gradient-weighted Class Activation
Mapping, Score-CAM is more expensive, requiring multi-
ple forward passes for each feature map.
In conclusion, we also applied the IF/IM-SSIM index to
compare images by providing a score that indicates their
similarity using the algorithm proposed by Wang et al. in
[28] named Structural Similarity Index Measure (SSIM)
algorithm. In detail, we calculated Information Fidelity
(IF-SSIM) using the same CAM algorithm. Moreover, to

5 Conclusion and future work
In this paper, we proposed an intrusion detector based
on Deep Learning that can to distinguish network attacks
like Denial of Service and Man in the Middle and legiti-
mate commands that can also be used to perform malicious
actions. In detail, we employed a dataset retrieved on the
web composed of PCAP ﬁles that we converted into images
using a Python script written by the authors. Due to the
small amount of images obtained, we decided to apply the
augmentation technique to retrieve more samples in the
training and validation phases. Concluded the dataset compo-
sition, we trained and tested ten state-of-the-art architectures
such as AlexNet, DenseNet, EfﬁcientNet, Inception, LeNet,
MobileNet, ResNet50, Standard CNN, VGG16, and VGG19
using different hyper-parameters. After concluding the train-
ing, validation, and test phases, we achieved the best accuracy
results (0.900) with the model trained using DenseNet. As
the next step, we employed two different CAM algorithms
(Grad-CAM++ and Score-CAM). These algorithms allowed
us to highlight which areas of an image are most distinc-
tive during the classiﬁcation. Moreover, to provide a more
comprehensive evaluation of perceived image distortion, we
also calculated the similarity between the pictures obtained
by employing the IF/IM-SSIM index. As the last step, we
reproduced the same methodology on a new dataset, getting
promising results. In detail, we re-executed the CNNs train-
ing employing the same hyper-parameters used on the ﬁrst
dataset training. The outcomes demonstrated the ﬂexibility
of the proposed approach, suggesting that it can be utilized
among different datasets composed of PCAP ﬁles.
In future work, we will compare the results obtained using
different architecture, for instance Recurrent Neural Net-
works. Additionally, we will evaluate the robustness of the
proposed method with images generated using Generative
Adversarial Networks. Moreover, we also working on the
identiﬁcation of different threats in the smart-grid environ-
ment through the usage of the network.
