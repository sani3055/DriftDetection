The rapid growth of Internet of Things (IoT) devices has introduced critical cybersecurity challenges 
due to the large attack surface and diverse interconnected systems. This work proposes a novel self-
attention deep neural network (SA-DNN) augmented with a learnable feature gating (LFG) mechanism. 
The LFG component dynamically emphasizes security-relevant features during training, while 
suppressing redundant data. This architecture enables seamless, end-to-end feature optimization. 
It enhances the robustness of the model across varied intrusion scenarios without the need for 
conventional manual or filter-based feature selection. The self-attention module efficiently captures 
long-range relationships within network traffic, while the LFG mechanism boosts the adaptability of 
feature representations. The framework is explainable through shapley additive explanations (SHAP) 
and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) and provides transparency in intrusion 
detection decisions. Evaluations on IoT datasets BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT show superior performance 
of the proposed method over baseline and state-of-the-art methods, achieving accuracy values of 
99.3% and 99.6%, respectively. Additional experiments on the general network dataset UNSW-NB15 
demonstrate strong generalizability with 97.9% accuracy. This confirms the robustness of the model 
across diverse intrusion scenarios. The proposed approach provides a lightweight, scalable, and 
interpretable solution with strong generalizability across IoT and broader network security settings.

Recently, the IoT has become popular due to enhanced technology, increased access to the Internet, and the 
generation of big data. But this growth has also come with increased risks, which can be noticed from the 
emergence of intrusion detection systems (IDS) for IoT networks. IDS are highly useful for the detection of 
suspicious or unusual activity in such networks, raising alarms that help deter the exploitation of compromised 
devices. The distributed and massive nature of IoT networks, which include numerous and heterogeneous devices 
used for different purposes, has created new problems associated with data storage, computational capabilities, 
and security. Therefore, the creation of effective and responsive solutions is needed1–3.

On the basis of the applied model, traditional IDS techniques are broadly categorized into two types: those 
based on signature matching and those based on anomaly detection. The main strength of signature-based 
methods is that they rely on specific patterns of attack. Previous work shows difficulties in handling zero-day 
attacks, and such approaches involve trade-offs, including considerable storage and computation requirements, 
making them unfit for IoT 4. Conversely, anomaly-based IDS methods identify variations from normal traffic 
patterns and thus can detect new attack patterns. However, anomaly-based systems often generate high false 
positive rates, as benign anomalies may also be flagged as malicious activities, highlighting the need for advanced 
detection methodologies5.
The proliferation of IoT devices has increased the potential for cyberattacks due to heterogeneous devices, 
limited computational resources, and dynamic network environments. Existing IDS solutions struggle with 
zero-day attacks, high false positives, and the need for manual feature selection, thereby creating a need for a 
robust, automated, and explainable intrusion detection framework.
IoT networks span across various layers of the Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) model, where each 
layer is unique in terms of risk and attack surface. At the physical layer, potential adversarial behaviors include 
jamming and eavesdropping, where transmission of data may be disrupted or confidentiality compromised, 
respectively. The data link layer can face problems like collisions and replay attacks, which interfere with the 
rules used to send packets 6. The network layer, responsible for moving data between devices, is vulnerable 
to sinkhole and Sybil attacks that can make the network unreliable. Likewise, at the transport layer, which is 
responsible for delivering data reliably, DoS and session hijacking attacks can flood the system or intrude and 
take over an unauthorized session, respectively. Finally, the application layer, which connects directly with users, 
is often targeted by malware, phishing, and ransomware attacks that try to steal data or disrupt services7,8.
This scenario can be visualized in Fig.  1, which illustrates the integration of IoT architecture with the OSI 
model, highlighting how data flows from physical devices to applications. It also shows where deep learning 
models operate within the application layer for tasks such as intrusion detection and predictive analytics.
New machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods also offer potential solutions to these 
difficulties. These techniques help design systems that are capable of learning across multiple layers of the OSI 
model to provide better ways of detecting attacks 9,10. In particular, the application of attention mechanisms 
improves IDS performance because they allow models to focus on important features and dependencies in IoT

In contrast to traditional approaches, this model avoids the use of filter-based feature selection by employing

Applying attention mechanisms helps the model achieve better results in identifying attacks across all layers

approach is evaluated on IoT-related dataset as well as on non-IoT datasets, offering a scalable, efficient, and 
explainable technique to secure networks.
While several recent works have leveraged deep learning and attention-based models for IoT intrusion 
detection, most existing approaches still suffer from static feature selection, limited interpretability, and 
restricted validation across heterogeneous datasets. To address these gaps, our work introduces a dynamic 
LFG mechanism within a Self-Attention framework, incorporates explainability through SHAP and LIME, and 
validates performance on both IoT-specific and general-purpose datasets. These aspects distinguish our approach 
from prior studies and establish its novelty in terms of adaptability, interpretability, and generalizability.
Although the primary focus of this research is intrusion detection in IoT environments, we additionally 
evaluate the framework on the UNSW-NB15 dataset. Unlike BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT, which are IoT-specific, 
UNSW-NB15 captures a broader spectrum of modern network intrusions. Its inclusion allows us to examine 
the cross-domain adaptability of the proposed SA-DNN + LFG model and to demonstrate that the framework is 
effective not only for IoT security but also for general-purpose intrusion detection scenarios.

Related works
Intrusion detection systems (IDS) for IoT have undergone significant development, with diverse approaches 
ranging from classical machine learning to deep learning and distributed paradigms. This section reviews 
important studies, pointing out both their strengths and weaknesses, and explains how our proposed SA-
DNN + LFG framework positions into this context.
Kumar et al.12 proposed a unified IDS framework that brought multiple detection systems together under one 
structure. This modular design improved flexibility but it also added complexity and computational overhead, 
which limited its scalability. In recent years, deep learning has gained attention in IDS because of its ability 
to identify complex attack patterns. For example, Saba et al. 13 used CNNs and LSTMs to capture spatial and 
temporal traffic features, achieving strong results in DDoS detection. However, their models required significant 
resources make them difficult to deploy on IoT devices with limited capacity. Similarly, Awajan 14 showed that 
deep neural networks could scale in large IoT environments, but their reliance on high-dimensional data 
increased training time and reduced adaptability. These studies highlight the need for lightweight yet effective 
deep learning approaches. This gap is addressed in this work using learnable feature gating, which reduces 
redundancy without compromising detection accuracy.
Graph-based methods have also been explored. Lo et al.15 proposed E-Graph SAGE, which is a GNN-based 
framework to effectively capture spatial–temporal dependencies of multi-stage IoT attacks. Despite its strength 
in modeling relationships, the framework faces scalability issues in large IoT networks with sparse samples. 
Similarly, Li et al.16 applied semi-supervised learning with GANs (HDA-IDS) to detect outliers in skewed data 
distributions. Although effective in handling imbalanced datasets, GAN training remains computationally 
expensive. This limits the real-time applicability. Our proposed SA-DNN with attention avoids such overhead 
by capturing long-range relationships efficiently, while the LFG module adaptively emphasizes discriminative 
features to maintain scalability.
Lightweight IDS designs, such as those by Azimjonov and Kim 17, optimized linear SVMs with feature 
selection to balance accuracy and computational cost. However, these simplified models often lack the flexibility 
to adapt to evolving attack types. Anwar et al. 18 addressed privacy concerns using federated learning, which 
preserved data confidentiality by training models locally and aggregating updates centrally. While effective in 
enhancing privacy and fault tolerance, this approach introduces challenges in communication overhead and 
synchronization. Shahin et al.19 applied transfer learning to mitigate recalibration issues but its efficiency across

Beyond these, optimization and distributed computing techniques have been explored to enhance IDS. Vakili 
et al.20 combined Grey Wolf Optimization with MapReduce for scalable IoT service composition, while Heidari 
et al.21 developed blockchain-based IDS for drone communication. These works demonstrated scalability and

swarm intelligence. These contributions illustrate the breadth of AI applications but highlight persistent issues 
of complexity, energy cost, and generalizability. Our SA-DNN + LFG specifically addresses these by offering a 
lightweight and scalable IDS validated on multiple IoT and general datasets.
Recent research has also advanced intrusion detection in IoT and related domains using collaborative and 
federated learning approaches. For example, Khan et al.25 proposed a collaborative SRU network with dynamic 
behaviour aggregation, aiming to reduce communication overhead and enhance explainability in smart 
healthcare systems. Khan et al. 26 introduced a Federated-Boosting scheme for consumer IoT, which addresses 
data imbalance and overfitting challenges while preserving device privacy through distributed training. Similarly, 
Khan et al.27 presented Fed-Inforce-Fusion, a federated reinforcement-based intrusion detection framework for 
Internet of Medical Things (IoMT) networks, which enhances privacy and security by leveraging reinforcement 
learning for adaptive aggregation. These studies highlight the growing role of federated and distributed learning 
for privacy-preserving, resource-efficient intrusion detection. However, they primarily focus on collaborative 
learning paradigms, whereas our work distinguishes itself by employing a self-attention-based architecture with 
learnable feature gating and post-hoc interpretability (SHAP and LIME) to provide a lightweight and transparent 
IDS tailored to IoT environments.

establish its value as a scalable, trustworthy IDS solution for real-world IoT networks.
Proposed methodology
In this section, the phases involved in the proposed framework have been elucidated. The working of each phase 
and the algorithm used in the corresponding phases has been discussed in the subsequent sub section. Figure 2 
illustrates the components present in the proposed methodology to leverage about the interconnectivity among 
them. Here, the BoT-IoT, N-BaIoT, and UNSW-NB15 datasets were processed independently, not merged. Each 
dataset underwent the same sequence of preprocessing, feature extraction, sampling, and classification through 
the SA-DNN + LFG model. For IoT-specific datasets (BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT), interpretability analysis was 
conducted using SHAP and LIME to provide feature-level explanations. For UNSW-NB15, evaluation focused 
on quantitative metrics to assess generalizability. This separation ensures dataset-specific evaluation and avoids 
interpretability bias due to heterogeneous feature sets.
It is important to note that the BoT-IoT, N-BaIoT, and UNSW-NB15 datasets were not merged at any stage. 
Instead, the complete pipeline shown in Fig.  2 was executed independently for each dataset, ensuring dataset-
specific training and evaluation. Results are therefore reported separately to provide a fair and transparent 
comparison across benchmarks.
Dataset description
This study employs three publicly available datasets: N-BaIoT, BoT-IoT, and UNSW-NB15, for the training and 
evaluation of the proposed SA-DNN + LFG model. These datasets were selected due to their comprehensiveness, 
realistic representation of IoT and general-purpose network traffic, and wide acceptance as benchmarks in the 
intrusion detection research community. BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT are IoT-specific datasets that capture realistic 
smart device traffic under a variety of benign and attack scenarios, while UNSW-NB15 provides a broader 
perspective by including diverse modern attack categories beyond IoT-specific threats. Together, these datasets 
ensure that the proposed model is rigorously evaluated for both IoT environments and more general intrusion 
detection scenarios.
N-BaIoT dataset
Meidan et al.5 established the N-BaIoT dataset for benchmark testing of IoT intrusion detection systems when 
they made it publicly accessible through Kaggle in 2018. The tool functions as an industry-standard resource 
to combat smart IoT device botnet attacks because it specifically focuses on this security problem. Nine 
commercially available IoT devices composed of Edimax IP cameras and Philips B120N/10 baby monitors and 
Wemo smart plugs underwent an infection process to create this dataset. Two dominating botnets in the IoT 
field belong to them: Mirai and BASHLITE. The botnets conduct wide-ranging distributed denial-of-service 
attacks through vulnerable IoT devices. The dataset contains more than 7 million time series records, each

UNSW-NB15 dataset
To further evaluate the adaptability of the proposed framework beyond IoT-specific traffic, the UNSW-NB15 
dataset was incorporated. This dataset was created at the Cyber Range Lab of the Australian Centre for Cyber 
Security (ACCS) using the IXIA PerfectStorm tool to generate hybrid modern network traffic. It includes both 
benign traffic and nine categories of malicious activities: Fuzzers, Analysis, Backdoors, DoS, Exploits, Generic 
attacks, Reconnaissance, Shellcode, and Worms. These categories represent a diverse set of threat vectors, from 
protocol misuse and reconnaissance to malware injection and shellcode execution.
The dataset comprises approximately 2.54 million records with 49 features extracted using Argus and Bro-
IDS tools. It is divided into a training set of 175,341 records and a test set of 82,332 records. Compared to 
BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT, UNSW-NB15 is more challenging due to its overlap of attack classes, higher intra-class 
variability, and greater similarity between benign and malicious flows. These characteristics make it an effective 
benchmark for evaluating the generalizability, robustness, and resilience of intrusion detection frameworks.
Data preprocessing
To this end, in the proposed methodology, preprocessing techniques are applied to ensure that the IoT network 
traffic datasets used in training the SA-DNN + LFG model are properly standardized and encoded, with class 
distribution verified to be fairly balanced. The first steps include the elimination of some of the features in the 
dataset that are not destined to assist in intrusion detection, including pkSeqID, seq, and subcategory. More 
specific subcategories such as “Theft” are then removed in order to make the dataset even more relevant. Ports 
represented in hexadecimal are converted to decimal format to standardize numerical features.
Scaling
StandardScaler is a preprocessing utility and normalizes the features so that they have a mean of zero and a 
unit variance. This makes certain that the features participate as equal in the completion of model training so 
that features with higher variance will not overwhelm others. Standardization is performed using the following 
formula:

where:
• z is the standardized value.
• x is the original feature value.
• μ is the mean of the feature values.
• σ is the standard deviation of the feature values.
By applying this transform the values are standardized to mean = 0 and standard deviation = 1 in shape of 
normal probability distribution. This technique is particularly beneficial for machine learning models, especially 
deep neural networks, as it accelerates convergence during training and improves performance by reducing 
numerical instability.
Feature extraction
The feature extraction strategies differ across datasets due to the distinct nature of their traffic characteristics. 
For the BoT-IoT dataset, correlation-based feature selection was employed, whereas for N-BaIoT, a Random 
Forest–based technique was applied. For the UNSW-NB15 dataset, we retained the feature set provided by the 
original creators, consisting of 49 statistical and flow-based attributes, which capture packet-level, time-based, 
and content-based characteristics of network traffic. This approach preserves the heterogeneity of UNSW-NB15 
and allows testing the generalizability of the proposed framework without additional feature engineering.
BoT-IoT
In the case of BoT-IoT dataset, features like Total Length of Fwd Packets, Flow Bytes/s, Fwd Packet Length 
Mean and Fwd IAT Mean are considered. It has established that these features have a very high correlation 
with the behavior of the network and can distinguish normal activities from such attack categories as DoS 
and Reconnaissance. For example, Flow Bytes/s is an integrative parameter that reflects traffic load, and Fwd 
IAT Mean describes temporal irregularities. By focusing on these critical features, CFS reduces computational 
complexity and enhances the classification accuracy of the detection model.

N-BaIoT
Feature selection is performed by Random Forest algorithm for the N-BaIoT dataset because this algorithm

importance of each feature in deciding between normals and anomalies in IoT network traffic. The features 
that were selected comprised of ‘H_L0.01_mean’ , ‘MI_dir_L0.01_mean’ , ‘H_L0.1_mean’ , ‘MI_dir_L0.1_mean’ ,

‘HH_jit_L0.1_mean’, among others. These features represent statistical measures and mutual information values 
that capture patterns in data, such as entropy and direction-based variance.
UNSW-NB15
The UNSW-NB15 dataset includes 49 features generated using the Argus and Bro-IDS tools, spanning categories 
such as flow duration, packet size statistics, protocol flags, transaction content features, and connection behaviors. 
Examples include dur (record total duration), sbytes (source-to-destination bytes), dbytes (destination-to-
source bytes), spkts (source packets), dpkts (destination packets), and sttl/dttl (source/destination TTL values). 
These features collectively capture traffic intensity, packet exchange behavior, and flow-level semantics. Since the 
dataset is already balanced across multiple modern attack categories, no additional manual feature reduction 
was performed. Instead, all 49 features were provided to the SA-DNN + LFG model, ensuring a fair assessment 
of its adaptability to non-IoT traffic.
Sampling process
The datasets used in this work exhibit varying degrees of class imbalance. For BoT-IoT and N-BaIoT, severe 
imbalance exists between majority classes (e.g., normal traffic or DDoS) and minority classes (e.g., Keylogging, 
Data Exfiltration, gafgyt_combo). To address this, we applied a combination of random oversampling and 
random undersampling strategies. For UNSW-NB15, however, the dataset already provides a reasonably 
balanced distribution across its nine major attack categories (e.g., DoS, Exploits, Fuzzers, Backdoors, Shellcode, 
Worms, etc.) and normal traffic. Therefore, no additional resampling was applied to UNSW-NB15, allowing us 
to preserve the dataset’s native distribution for evaluating cross-domain generalizability.
Random oversampling
The random oversampling approach has been applied to increase the frequency of minority classes. For example, 
in the datasets, attack categories like Data Exfiltration or Keylogging might have far fewer samples compared 
to normal traffic or DDoS attacks. Oversampling duplicates these minority class instances to ensure their 
representation matches the majority classes. For the case of N-BaIoT dataset, minority classes like gafgyt_combo 
(59,718 samples before sampling) were increased using random oversampling to achieve a uniform distribution 
of 100,000 samples each. This process added more instances to the minority classes, improving their presence in 
the dataset and allowing the model to learn their behaviour effectively.
Random undersampling
It is also noted that after the oversampling, there is still quite imbalance in the dataset the reason being that 
normal traffic dominates most of the network connections. To do so, undersampling was carried on all classes 
and all of them were brought to a fixed level. For example, if each attack type was oversampled to 72,919 samples, 
normal traffic was undersampled to this same number. For the case of N-BaIoT dataset, classes such as mirai_
udp which had 237,665 samples before sampling were reduced through random undersampling to a class size of 
100,000 samples. This process helped to reduce the dominance of majority classes, ensuring that the model does 
not bias toward the larger classes during training.
Data splits
For all datasets, the samples were randomly shuffled and split into 70% training, 15% validation, and 15% 
testing sets. Stratified sampling was applied where applicable to preserve class distributions. For UNSW-NB15, 
stratification was performed across all attack categories to ensure balanced evaluation.
Learnable feature gating layer
The ability of the proposed model can be enhanced by prioritize the discriminative features from the high-
dimensional IoT traffic data. This study incorporates a Learnable Feature Gating layer before the classification 
stage. Unlike traditional feature selection techniques, the LFG layer enables the network to dynamically learn 
which features to emphasize or suppress during training through gradient-based optimization.
After applying random over-and under-sampling to balance the class distribution, the sampled feature 
vectors are passed through the LFG layer. This module applies a gating mechanism, where each input feature is 
modulated by a learnable gate value between 0 and 1. The gate values are determined by a shallow neural layer 
trained jointly with the rest of the model.

The SA-DNN + LFG model has seven stages which unite deep learning components with attention and gating

architecture, by combining attention and latent gating with dense representations, captures complex attack 
patterns and improves classification performance across diverse IoT datasets.

efficiency and enable deployment on resource-constrained IoT devices.

Computational complexity analysis
The proposed SA-DNN + LFG framework involves three major components: preprocessing, feature selection, 
and classification. Preprocessing and feature extraction operations are linear in nature and scale as O(N·F), 
where N is the number of samples and F is the number of features. The Learnable Feature Gating (LFG) layer 
introduces only a lightweight overhead of O(F), since each feature is gated independently. The self-attention 
mechanism, which forms the core of SA-DNN, has a time complexity of O(F2·d) and space complexity of O(F2), 
where d is the dimensionality of the query vectors. The dense layers contribute an additional cost of O(N·F·H), 
where H denotes the hidden neurons.
During training, the total complexity can be approximated as: O(E·N·(F2 + F·H)), where E is the number of 
epochs. Inference scales linearly with the feature dimension (O(F·H + F2)), which ensures efficiency for real-time 
intrusion detection. The design of the SA-DNN + LFG, including the lightweight gating and compact attention 
module, makes the framework inherently suitable for deployment in resource-constrained IoT environments.

Explainability and model transparency
The interpretability of the SA-DNN + LFG model is significantly enhanced using SHAP and LIME, which 
contribute to greater trust, accountability, and transparency in the decision-making process of intrusion 
detection systems. These techniques provide both quantitative and qualitative insights. SHAP delivers a global 
view by identifying the most influential features across all predictions. This helps security analysts understand 
which traffic attributes such as flow duration, packet count and TCP flags consistently influence classification 
outcomes. This enables better system tuning and policy development. LIME provides local interpretability by 
perturbing the input and approximating the model’s behavior around a specific instance. This helps justify why 
a given case was flagged as malicious and supports incident response teams in root-cause analysis. Together, 
SHAP and LIME increase trust in deep learning models that are often perceived as black boxes. By making 
the detection rationale auditable and explainable, these methods support model validation, debugging, and 
potential regulatory compliance in security-critical IoT deployments. In IoT intrusion detection, it’s important 
to understand why the system flags certain network events as malicious, helping security analysts make informed 
decisions. To this end, the proposed SA-DNN combined with the LFG framework incorporates Explainable 
Artificial Intelligence techniques, specifically SHAP and LIME, to provide both global and local interpretability 
of the model’s predictions. By integrating SHAP and LIME, the SA-DNN combined with LFG model achieves 
not only high detection performance but also interpretability of its outcomes. This dual perspective enables 
global understanding of how features affect model behavior, along with localized justifications for specific 
predictions. These interpretability features contribute to better trust among cybersecurity professionals and 
facilitate debugging and refinement of the system during deployment. As such, explainability is a core aspect 
of the proposed framework, supporting both technical robustness and practical usability in real-world IoT 
intrusion detection scenarios.

Robustness evaluation under adversarial conditions and noisy environments
To assess the resilience of the proposed SA-DNN + LFG model, additional experiments were conducted under 
two challenging scenarios: adversarial perturbations and varying levels of input noise. These tests were designed 
to simulate real-world conditions where attackers may attempt to evade detection through subtle feature 
manipulations or where IoT traffic data may be degraded due to signal noise, packet loss, or sensor inaccuracies. 
For adversarial robustness, a Fast Gradient Sign Method based attack was simulated by introducing perturbations 
with ε-values ranging from 0.01 to 0.1. The model retained an average detection accuracy above 89% up to 
ε = 0.05, demonstrating significant resistance to gradient-based evasion tactics. Beyond this threshold, accuracy 
gradually declined, but the performance degradation remained more graceful compared to conventional DNN 
baselines. In parallel, Gaussian noise with standard deviations (σ) of 0.01, 0.05, and 0.1 was injected into the 
feature vectors of both benign and malicious samples to simulate sensor-level distortions and environmental 
noise. The model maintained detection accuracy above 92% for σ = 0.05 and showed less than a 4% drop in F1-
score across all tested noise levels.

Interpretability analysis using SHAP and LIME: quantitative and qualitative insights
To ensure transparency and trustworthiness in intrusion detection outcomes, SHAP and LIME were employed 
to interpret the predictions made by the SA-DNN + LFG model. These tools helped uncover which features most 
influenced specific detection decisions, enabling a deeper understanding of the model’s behavior.
Quantitative assessment
Feature attribution values generated by SHAP were aggregated across the dataset to determine globally influential 
features. For the N-BaIoT dataset, features such as packet_size_variance, flow_duration, and dst_port_entropy 
consistently received high SHAP values, indicating their strong relevance in identifying attack patterns. A feature 
importance ranking derived from SHAP showed over 85% alignment with LIME-based local explanations across 
multiple samples, validating consistency between global and local interpretability. Additionally, an average SHAP 
contribution threshold (≥ 0.015) was used to filter the top 10 impactful features. Detection performance using 
only these top features remained within 4% of the full model, suggesting that explanations can guide effective 
dimensionality reduction or model simplification without sacrificing accuracy.
Qualitative assessment
Visualizations from LIME provided case-specific insights by highlighting feature contributions for individual 
predictions say for instance: classifying Mirai-Attack traffic, LIME revealed how high flow_rate and low ttl_
variance played decisive roles. These qualitative insights allow security analysts to validate predictions and trace 
decision logic in post-analysis workflows, supporting operational deployment in critical environments.
Impact and future directions
The insights derived from SHAP and LIME not only enhance model interpretability but also offer actionable 
guidance for refining intrusion detection strategies. For instance, features repeatedly shown to have minimal 
contribution could be pruned to reduce model complexity. Conversely, dominant features can inform future 
research focused on embedding domain knowledge into feature engineering or attention mechanisms. Moreover, 
these tools lay a foundation for adaptive IDS systems where model updates are driven by explainability feedback 
loops, improving resilience and transparency over time.

Computational overhead and resource efficiency
The proposed SA-DNN + LFG framework was implemented with four attention heads and 16-dimensional key, 
query, and value vectors, striking a balance between expressiveness and efficiency. This compact configuration 
enables modeling of complex inter-feature dependencies without incurring the quadratic scaling overhead typical 
of larger attention architectures. While Sect. " Computational complexity analysis" established the theoretical 
lightweight complexity of the framework, this section presents the corresponding empirical validation.
All experiments were conducted on Google Colab Pro, leveraging an NVIDIA Tesla T4 GPU (15.36 GB GPU 
memory, 13.29 GB system RAM, CUDA 12.2). The average training time per epoch was approximately 28 s for 
the BoT-IoT dataset, 34 s for N-BaIoT, and 44 s for UNSW-NB15. Peak memory usage during training remained 
below 6 GB, and inference latency per instance was consistently under 3 ms, demonstrating the feasibility of near 
real-time deployment even on moderate edge hardware.

Conclusion and future work
In this work, we developed an interpretable intrusion detection framework for IoT environments by integrating 
SA-DNN with LFG mechanism. The proposed SA-DNN + LFG model demonstrated high accuracy on IoT-
specific datasets, achieving 99.3% on BoT-IoT and 99.6% on N-BaIoT, and maintained strong generalizability 
on the broader UNSW-NB15 dataset with 97.9% accuracy. The model’s stability was further confirmed by 
reporting mean and standard deviation values across multiple experimental runs. For the IoT-specific datasets, 
SHAP and LIME were employed to provide interpretability of predictions, thereby enhancing transparency in 
feature contributions and decision-making. These results collectively validate the effectiveness, robustness, and 
explainability of the proposed framework for IoT intrusion detection, while demonstrating its adaptability to 
broader network intrusion scenarios.
In future work, we plan to extend the evaluation to additional datasets such as IoT-23 and TON_IoT to 
further assess cross-domain adaptability. We also plan to investigate adaptive learning strategies, including 
reinforcement learning for dynamic adaptation to evolving attack patterns and federated learning for privacy-
preserving distributed training. These extensions are expected to strengthen the scalability and practical 
applicability of the framework in real-world IoT environments.
