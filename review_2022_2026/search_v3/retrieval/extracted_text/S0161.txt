1. INTRODUCTION 
Enterprise network expansion to cloud services, IoT devices, and cyber -physical systems are 
generating more diverse and faster network traffic than before.  Networked environments are 
constantly under attack by a range of different forces. Just as is the c ase with brute -force attacks, 
denial-of-service (DoS) attacks, and the less common distributed denial -of-service (DDoS) attack, 
there are rogue force attacks such as port scanning, botnet attack, web attack, infiltration, and more. 
As a result, IDSs are essential for detecting malicious behavior directing against the network resources 
and safety of data, and the data’s confidentiality, integrity, and availability. However, the IDS needs to 
have high accuracy but at the same time low latency with low memory and false positive rate as well. 
A majority of machine learning -based intrusion detection studies utilize a conventional almost fixed 
batch learning approach where a model is trained just once from scratch using a fixed historical 
dataset and is subsequently tested on a separate static da taset. While it’s a useful benchmark, it is 
not entirely representative of a real network. Network traffic comes in a continuous stream, and 
patterns of both normal and malicious traffic evolve over time. There could be changes because of 
various reasons such as new applications, changes in user behavior, network configuration changes, 
attack variants and others. The changes between input features and classes with respect to time are 
called concept drift. A batch model that is trained on previous traffic wi ll gradually become less 
effective when a new distribution of data arrives and is different from the one on which it was 
originally trained. 
The incremental processing of individual flows allows online learning to serve as an alternative for 
streaming network traffic, wherein the model updates occur without re-training. Hoeffding Trees are 
well suited for this setting since they make splits based on the Hoeffding bound using a sequence of 
arriving observations. In contrast to standard decision trees, they are able to update continuously, 
with bounded memory. Nevertheless, a standard Hoeffding Tree may not adapt sufficiently when the 
distribution of network traffic changes. An Adaptive Hoeffding tree (AHT) extends this limitation using 
drift-aware mechanisms which can evaluate alternative decision branches and replace deteriorating 
subtrees when their performance falls below par. 
Adaptive Windowing (ADWIN) is a commonly used method used for detecting drift in data streams. 
It uses a window that grows and shrinks dynamically depending on the past observations. It flags 
some changes as statistically significant. ADWIN indicates that a drift event has occurred if there is a 
meaningful difference between the old and new segments. It is important for intrusion detection as 
attack regimes and normal traffic behavior can change suddenly or gradually. ADWIN is specifically 
designed to manage changing data streams with the use of an adaptive window rather than a fixed -
size window. 
While AHT and ADWIN do offer advantages, their benefits hinge on hyperparameter selection. 
Parameters like grace period, split -confidence, tie -threshold, and ADWIN -confidence control the

2. LITERATURE REVIEW 
Network intrusion detection systems (NIDSs) are increasingly required to work under non-stationary 
conditions. In other words, not only can attacks evolve, but benign traffic can also change and the 
deployment-domain can differ, all of which may render a previously-trained model unreliable. These 
changes may produce concept drift, while continuous updates mostly based on new observations 
may result in catastrophic forgetting of old attack knowledge. In recent studies, methods like 
decentralized federated learning, governance-aware response system, quantum anomaly detection, 
budget-aware multi-stage screening, and deep continual learning have been proposed. 
The XDFC -IDS is an explainable decentralized federated class -incremental intrusion detection 
framework for a streaming IoT setting, as proposed by Nguyen et al. The proposed scheme uses the 
serverless peer -to-peer aggregation architecture, CNN –GRU and LSTM  classifiers, EWC, and local 
SHAP explanations. EWC with classification loss reduces catastrophic forgetting when traffic arrives in 
sequence and collaborators have Non -IID data distribution. The assessment was conducted on the 
TONIoT and Kitsune datasets under federated and class-incremental conditions. 
 While XDFC-IDS enables privacy-respecting cooperation and feature level interpretability, it suffers 
from decentralized federation, deep-learning backbones, and under-utilization of inter-device model 
aggregation, which lead to computational, communication, and deployment complexities. 
Adabara et al. constructed a credible framework for intrusion detection and response for the 
Healthcare Internet of Medical Things. NIST AI Risk Management Framework is where the Random 
Forest flow detector, Deep Q -Network triage model and governance -rule engine integration take 
place at.  The research examines the predictive quality but also the safety type impacts, defined by 
benign blocking and decision-harm cost. The governance mechanism curtailed benign blocking from 
25.3% to 17.3% and lowered mean har m cost from 0.502 to 0.434 under shifted temporal replay. 
Although this work makes a significant contribution to safe automated response, it is more focused 
on ethical selection of response actions than on low-overhead, continual adaptation of the detection 
classifier.

Research Gap 
The literature reviewed confirms the modern NIDS research examines deployment challenges like 
concept drift, cross-domain shift, catastrophic forgetting, false positive control, resource limitations, 
response safety etc. But existing solutions often require one or more demanding requirements. 
• A system of federated deep learning infrastructure 
• Replay buffers or storing previous samples for preventing forgetfulness. 
• Monitoring of feature-space drift in batches. 
• The target-domain calibration data is labelled.  
• Process involving multiple filtering and expensive extra judges. 
• Policies for rule-governance and reinforcement-learning response. 
• Quantum encoding or special computational resources. 
Though useful, these techniques may be difficult to utilize in resource -limited or high -speed 
environments that require simple, low -latency per-flow processing. In particular, there is relatively

3. PROPOSED SYSTEM MODEL 
The suggested systems lightweight online network intrusion detection system NIDS for a continuous 
stream of network -flow. It is an ensemble using a drift detection mechanism that employs AHT 
classifier and ADWIN. The system classifies each incoming flow in an incremental manner, tracks the 
predicted degradation over time, and adapts to flow behaviour changes without the need for 
complete retraining, historical replay buffers, federated or end -to-end coordination, or labelled 
target-domain calibration. 
Adaptive trees that are based on Hoeffding are appropriate in streaming settings as they adjust 
incrementally. The Hoeffding bound is used to determine when there is sufficient proof for a split. 
ADWIN keeps an observation window of variable length. It sig nals a change when the means of two 
subwindows differ significantly. So, it may monitor the live prediction -error stream. Also, it can 
remove outdated observations after a drift is detected. 
3.1 System Architecture 
The proposed framework contains five sequential modules: 
1. Flow-stream acquisition 
Network traffic appears as flow data in chronological order.  Each record has numeric traffic 
characteristics such as flow durations, counts of packets and bytes, packet -length statistics, inter -
arrival-time measures, counts of flags, and features of forward/backward flow. 
2. Preprocessing and feature transformation 
Invalid, missing, duplicate and non -numeric values will be treated first before classification. The 
categorical labels are encoded, followed by the dirtying of numerical variables and the alignment of 
the selected feature vector for consistency across training and test-time flow instances. 
3. Bayesian hyperparameter optimization 
Prior to the actual execution of the stream, Bayesian optimization effectively searches for an AHT –
ADWIN configuration. This approach evaluates candidate configurations with an objective that 
prioritizes predictive quality while controlling false-positive, obtaining throughput, and memory use. 
4. Online AHT classification 
The Adaptive Hoeffding Tree classifies every pre -processed flow. After the prediction has been 
produced, the true label is used to update the model. Consequently, the classifier continuously gains 
knowledge from newly labelled flows.

5. ADWIN-based drift monitoring and adaptation 
The output prediction for each flow has been converted into an error indicator. The error stream is 
monitored by ADWIN. When the behavior of errors differs widely from the earlier observed 
behaviour, then the system flags it as concept drift. Using evidenc e from recent traffic, the system 
adapts the affected AHT branches.

3.6 Drift-Adaptation Procedure 
The adaptation process of AHT–ADWIN works for every incoming flow. 
1. Obtain the successive network-flow entry (𝐱𝑡, 𝑦𝑡). 
2. Carry out preprocessing and prepare the final feature vector. 
3. Use the present AHT to forecast traffic label 𝑦ˆ𝑡. 
4. Find prediction error 𝑒𝑡. 
5. Transfer 𝑒𝑡 to ADWIN. 
6. Gradually modify the AHT with inclusion of 𝐱𝑡, 𝑦𝑡. 
7. If drifts are detected update or activate alternate AHT branches with latest observations. 
8. Substituting a previous branch when its alternative branch has better predictive power. 
A test-then-train or prequential protocol is used for the learning process.

4. IMPLEMENTATION 
A Bayesian -optimized Adaptive Hoeffding Tree with ADWIN concept -drift detection is an online 
intrusion-detection framework that is implemented as a Python-based system. The system processes 
these network-flow records in turn, predicts the class of each flow before training on it, and monitors 
the prediction-error stream to determine whether there has been a change in the behavior of packets 
on the network. 
4.1 Development Environment 
The implementation relies on River or a similar stream-mining library, as it allows for model updates 
through learn_one() and provides prediction through predict_one(). An AHT is integrated with ADWIN

are intended to be used for online stream learning and may incorporate adaptive -window drift

The prediction-error stream of BO-AHT–ADWIN was monitored using external ADWIN at around 300k

5.8 Discussion 
According to the results, employing adaptive tree -learning techniques together with ADWIN drift 
detection and Bayesian hyperparameter optimization can yield improvements on the streaming 
intrusion-detection trade-off between classification efficacy and operational cost. 
First, the proposed BOAHTADWIN framework attained superior binary F1 -score and MCC in 
comparison to the Hoeffding Tree, Default AHT , and AHT + ADWIN baselines. It confirms that the 
optimized configuration yielded more value than using the adaptive tree and  drift detector with 
default settings. 
The proposed model had the least false positive of all studied models. The FPR level of 1.15% is 
particularly significant for practical NIDS deployment as it can either bombard analysts with false 
alarms or lead to blocking legitimate traffic. Despite havi ng a marginally higher F1 -score and MCC, 
Adaptive Random Forest recorded a 2.64% FPR and lesser throughput. 
The third section revealed that while binary detection is strong, multiclass detection performance is 
variable. 
In the multiclass scenario, the system was able to detect the popular families with a recall above 
94.8%, PortScan, DDoS and Dos related traffic. However, the similar activity of Brute Force and 
Infiltration was not detected. This weakness corresponds to s ignificant class imbalance: there were 
36 flows for infiltration, and a lot of attack families are less than 0.5% of the full stream. Future work

must do cost-sensitive learning, online resampling, rare-event replay, synthetic minority generation, 
hierarchical binary-then-family classification, etc. to improve minority-attack recognition. 
Ultimately, ADWIN monitoring recognized the periods of significant prediction error and generated 
drift alarms at the error spikes. The incremental learner was able to learn the new traffic effectively, 
as shown by the model’s later low error rate. Nonethe less, some periods with many alarm marks 
indicate that careful tuning of ADWIN sensitivity is required to prevent alarm flooding. In the future, 
an evaluation should compare detected alarms against the known timestamps of dataset-transitions 
and report on the following; the detection delay, false-alarm rate, and the time taken to recover post-

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 
For online intrusion detection in evolving network -flow streams, this study proposed the Bayesian -
optimized Adaptive Hoeffding Tree with ADWIN drift detection. The framework integrates 
incremental tree learning, prediction -error-based drift monitoring, and  Bayesian hyperparameter 
optimization that delivers adaptive detection capability without any full retraining or storage of the 
entire traffic history. The experiments utilized a chronological 2017 CICIDS derived stream with 
2,830,743 flows in total: 80.30 % benign and 19.70% attack. According to Bayesian optimization, the 
grace period, split confidence, tie threshold, and ADWIN delta were set to 50, 0.0055517, 0.1934701, 
and 0.0026619 respectively, with a best tuning F1-score of 0.9814. 

Journal of Intelligent Decision Making and Information Science 
Volume 3(14s), (2026) 2413-2436 
2435 
BO-AHT–ADWIN outperformed the baseline Hoeffding Tree, Default AHT and untuned AHT + ADWIN 
configurations, yielding scores of 0.9916 for F1 and 0.9681 for MCC. Despite Adaptive Random Forest 
recording a marginally higher F1 -score and MCC, the proposed fram ework achieved a much lower 
false-positive rate than Adaptive Random Forest at 1.15% as opposed to 2.64%. The system was able 
to process 2,166 flows in a second, a big improvement from the Adaptive Random Forest with 1,477 
flows a second. Recall across the classes was exceptionally high for the first four class traffic over the 
benign, portscan, ddos and dos_heartbleed. However, the performance was limited for the next two 
classes bot and web_attack. Brute force and infiltration could not be detected. The limitation shows 
the serious imbalance of the time stream, in particular only 36 Infiltration flows. The ADWIN 
monitoring further detected several elevated actuals vs. predicted periods, suggesting the method’s 
ability to detect behavioural changes and to adapt. 
In the future, research on recognition of rare attack families should use cost-sensitive learning, online 
resampling, minority -class replaying, and hierarchical classifying into binary then multiclass. For 
further validation, real NetFlow or Zeek traffic f rom newer and more diverse datasets and delayed -
label scenarios should be used.  Experiments in the future should validate ADWIN alarms against 
known transitions in traffic and report detection delay, missing drifts, false alarms, and recovery time. 
Finally achieving lightweight online explainability and feature-selection mechanisms may help sustain 
analyst trust whilst preserving the framework’s low false-positive rate and computational efficiency.
