Abstract: Industrial Control Systems (ICS) and SCADA networks underpin operational technology, yet intrusion
detection system (IDS) research keeps reporting strong laboratory numbers while sidestepping deployment
blockers—strict false-alarm budgets, real-time latency, nonstationary plant behavior. We present an operational
sensitivity audit of an explainable hybrid IDS pipeline, treating windowing/stride, thresholding, and SHAP-based
explanation generation as coupled components. On two labeled ICS datasets (CIC Modbus and TU Wien), 10 s to
30 s stride changes rarely flip attack-window decisions (2.15% and 3.87%), but on a benign-only IEC-104 trace
the false-alarm rate jumps from 1.50 to 3.55 per hour—FAR is not stride-invariant. Global SHAP explanations
stay stable under stride perturbation, yet decision-critical flip-window explanations do not (median cosine distance
0.161 on TU Wien). A KS drift detector flags an injected mean shift with zero pre-drift false alarms. Inference
and fusion run sub-millisecond; every claim maps to a regenerable artifact.
Key-Words: industrial control systems, SCADA, intrusion detection system, explainable artificial intelligence,
SHAP , concept drift, sensitivity analysis, operational technology, cybersecurity.

1 Introduction
ICS/SCADA networks underpin critical
infrastructure. IT/OT convergence is
making them
more exposed by the year. Intrusion detection is a key
compensating control—but deploying it in practice
hits the same friction points again and again: low base
rates that make false positives expensive, stringent
false-alarm budgets, real-time latency demands, and
distribution shift. Explainable IDS designs are now
expected to deliver actionable alerts, not just scores.
The trouble is that explanation quality turns fragile
once small engineering perturbations—stride/overlap
choices, threshold recalibration—push decisions near
the boundary.
One concern stands out: windowing is part of
the model. Sliding windows turn ICS telemetry into
fixed-length vectors, and the choice of window length
and stride directly sets alert volume, detection delay,
and sample dependence. Thresholds calibrated to
a target FAR can lose that FAR control under even
modest deployment shift. Explanations may look
stable on average yet become unreliable for the rare,
decision-critical alerts that operators actually inspect.
This work builds on a broader research agenda
and systematic review, [1] that shaped the research
questions and evaluation design. Here we report
the completed sensitivity experiments and the
artifact-backed evidence behind them.
We contribute a deployment-oriented sensitivity
evaluation of an explainable hybrid IDS pipeline:
1. Stride/overlap sensitivity: decision flip coverage
and paired alert-level deltas in FAR/EDR/delay
under matched operating points, including an
external benign-only FAR transfer check.
2. Explanation stability: global vs flip-window
attribution stability summaries (cosine-distance
distributions + boundary-case interpretation).
3. Drift monitoring sanity check: controlled abrupt
mean-shift and gradual ramp drift tests reporting
pre-drift false alarms and detection delay.
4. Reproducibility bundle: claim→evidence
mapping with rerunnable scripts, configs, and
deterministic seeds.
Section 2 reviews prior work and motivates the
operational metrics. Section 3 formalizes the pipeline
and sensitivity tests. Datasets and configuration
follow in Section 4. Section 5 reports results.
Section 6 discusses implications and threats to
validity; Section 7 concludes.

2.2 Explainability and Stability
SHAP/LIME attributions and local surrogate rules
dominate explainable IDS work, [14], [15], [16], [17],
[18]. Interpretability has limits, though—conceptual
ones, [19]—and alternative explanation mechanisms
keep evolving, [20], [21], [22], [23]. In high-stakes
environments, usefulness hinges on robustness and
stability, [24]. There is also a less obvious risk:
interpretability can open attack surfaces, enabling
model extraction, [25].
2.3 Drift and Reproducibility
Industrial telemetry drifts. That is the baseline
assumption, and drift taxonomies now span surveys,
continuous monitoring, and multi-stage testing,
[26], [27], [28], [29]. Streaming settings have
produced a range of detectors: EWMA-like
tests, adaptive windowed methods, unlabeled
drift approaches, class-distribution monitors, and
drift-aware ensembles, [30], [31], [32], [33], [34],
[35]. A single abrupt step is no longer enough
for evaluation; recent reviews call for gradual and
trend scenarios too, [36], [37], [38]. Drift can
also be adversarial, [39]. On the reproducibility
side, persistent failures in systems and software
research motivate stronger artifact capture and rerun
validation, [40], [41].
3 Problem Formulation and Methods
3.1 Pipeline: From Telemetry to
Deduplicated Alerts
Let L be window length and s the stride. For each
window start t, the pipeline builds a feature vector
xt from observations in [t, t + L). Overlap (s < L )
induces dependence between consecutive xt.
A detector scores each window as F (xt) ∈ [0, 1]
and raises an alarm when F (xt) > τ . Operators,
however, do not consume raw windows—they
consume events. We therefore also report a
deduplicated alert stream: alarms within a 60 s
tolerance on the same asset/session key are merged
into a single event. This separation matters.
Changing s changes the number of windows per hour
even when the decision boundary stays fixed.
3.2 Hybrid Score and Explanation Interface
The hybrid IDS runs three channels: (i) a
deterministic signature/specification channel, (ii)
an anomaly channel trained on benign behavior,
and (iii) a supervised semantic channel (when labels
exist). Fusion is a weighted combination
F (xt) = αA(xt) + (1 − α)S(xt), (1)
with extensions for additional channels and learned
fusion.
Each selected alarm carries an explanation vector
E(xt). Here, we compute deterministic additive
SHAP attributions over fusion-channel score inputs
(score_sem, score_sig, score_anom)—not over
the full raw feature set. The reason is practical:
attributions at the fusion level stay stable, cheap, and
audit-friendly.
3.3 Operational Sensitivity Tests
Three stress tests target operational concerns directly.
(T1) Stride/overlap sensitivity. Fix L and
compare stride values s ∈ { s1, s2, . . .} at matched
operating points. Two outputs: (i) attack-window
flip rate—what fraction of attack windows change
decision between strides—and (ii) paired deltas in
alert-level FAR, EDR, and delay.
The primary comparison here is s ∈ { 10s, 30s}
under a time-equivalent persistence policy:
persistence is fixed as a duration, not a window count.
Without this, shorter strides gain an unfair advantage
simply by emitting more adjacent windows.
(T2) Explanation stability under stride
perturbation. Attribution vectors are computed
at each stride and compared via cosine distance.
Two regimes matter: (i) global stability across
all evaluation windows, and (ii) decision-critical
stability on flip-windows where the stride change
flips the decision. Flip-windows sit exactly on the
boundary—the regime operators are most likely to
inspect.
(T3) Drift robustness. Drift detection is tested
on controlled injections. We report (i) pre-drift false
alarms—spurious triggers before the change—and
(ii) detection delay after injection. Both an abrupt
mean shift and a gradual ramp scenario are monitored
with a Kolmogorov–Smirnov (KS) test over sliding
windows.

4 Experimental Setup
4.1 Datasets
Three data sources are used. All results in Section 5
trace back to artifact tables and reports from the
experiment suite.
CIC Modbus. Zeek flow records over
Modbus/TCP with labeled attack annotations.
Scope: 123.3 h, 93,229 windows (12,232 attack,
80,997 benign), 238 episodes. Attack types span
reconnaissance, brute_force, query_flooding,
payload_injection, false_data_injection,
delay_response, replay, frame_stacking, and
length_manipulation. This is the main benchmark for
stride sensitivity, SHAP stability, and model-family
comparisons.
TU Wien. Pre-labeled ICS sessions with
multi-state labeling. Scope: 636.15 h, 318,103
windows (262,303 attack, 55,800 benign), 4 episodes.
A useful stress test for stride sensitivity and SHAP
stability—but the small episode count limits EDR
granularity.
IEC-104 baseline. A benign-only IEC-104 trace,
[42]. Scope: 14.6 h, no attack labels. Its sole purpose
is external FAR transfer testing.
4.2 Windowing, Stride, and Alert
Post-Processing
Window length is fixed at L = 30 s. Strides of 10 s
and 30 s are compared. Results are paired: the same
detector configuration runs under each stride. FAR is
reported on deduplicated alert events—alarms within
a 60 s tolerance on the same asset/session key are
merged.
4.3 Models and Configurations
Three channels make up the hybrid IDS: (i)
signature/specification, (ii) anomaly (Isolation Forest
on benign baselines), and (iii) supervised semantic
(Random Forest, XGBoost, or LightGBM, depending
on configuration). Fusion options include weighted,
max, and learned approaches (e.g., logistic regression
over channel scores). Thresholds target a FAR of
1.0/hr on validation data for the stride and SHAP
experiments.
4.4 Explainability Configuration
Deterministic additive SHAP attributions are
computed at the fusion stage over channel scores
(semantic, signature, anomaly). Stability is assessed
via cosine distance across strides. The background
set contains 100 matched benign windows; 500
evaluation windows per variant are sampled and
aggregated over 5 seeds.
4.5 Drift Detection Configuration
A KS-test detector runs over a 500-sample sliding
window on synthetic score timeseries with controlled
injections. The abrupt scenario injects a mean shift
of 0.3 at the midpoint (sample 2,500 of 5,000). The
gradual scenario uses a ramp. Sampling interval:
30.0 s per sample. Evaluation frequency: every 50
samples (step = 500/10 ). Detection delay equals
the first flagged evaluation index minus the injection
index. Because the rolling window mixes pre- and
post-drift samples, a 0-delay detection can occur
without accumulating a full post-drift window.
5 Results
5.1 Key Findings
Five findings stand out. (i) Stride changes (10 s vs
30 s) rarely flip attack-window decisions—flip rates
are 2.15% on CIC Modbus and 3.87% on TU Wien.
(ii) FAR is another story: on external benign IEC-104
traffic it jumps from 1.50/hr to 3.55/hr with the same
stride change. (iii) Global SHAP explanations stay
stable (median cosine distance 0.000054 on Modbus,
0.003330 on TU Wien), but flip-window explanations
can be far less so—median 0.161 on TU Wien. (iv)
A KS drift detector catches an injected mean shift
(magnitude 0.3) with 0 pre-drift alarms and 0-sample
delay; gradual drift takes longer but still produces
0 pre-drift alarms. (v) Inference and fusion are
sub-millisecond (p95 < 0.1 ms). Data loading and
windowing dominate stream-simulation runtime.

6 Discussion and Threats to Validity
6.1 Why Sensitivity Audits Matter
Operational stability is not one thing—it is layered.
Decision stability, FAR stability, and explanation
stability can all diverge under the exact same
perturbation. That is what the stride experiments
show. Few attack-window decisions flip. FAR on
external benign traffic shifts substantially. Global
explanation metrics look fine while flip-window
attributions tell a different story.
6.2 Interpreting FAR Transfer
Why does the IEC-104 benign-only baseline matter?
Because labeled datasets can underrepresent benign
diversity. Testing on labeled benign windows alone is
not sufficient evidence for deployability. The 2.37 ×
FAR increase on IEC-104 under stride perturbation
makes the case: deployment evaluation should
include at least one benign-only trace from the target
environment.
6.3 Limitations
Several limitations apply. Threshold stability under
shift is a known failure mode—FAR-calibrated
cutoffs can drift as score distributions change. TU

6.4 Reproducibility Threats
The experiment suite is configuration-driven and
deterministic. Still, runtime and FAR transfer are
sensitive to environment and background traffic
composition. Independent replication requires
complete configs, seeds, and representative benign
traces—all of which we release.
7 Conclusion
Explainable hybrid IDS for SCADA/ICS will
not become deployable without sensitivity
evidence—stability of alerts and explanations
under realistic windowing/stride perturbations
and under distribution shift. This paper
contributes an operational sensitivity audit: stride
sensitivity, decision-critical explanation stability on
flip-windows, drift monitoring sanity checks, and
runtime feasibility. The central lesson is that decision
stability does not imply FAR stability on realistic
benign traffic, and that global explainability metrics
can mask boundary-case instability. Every claim
maps to a regenerable artifact in the accompanying
reproducibility bundle.
